SpaceX Just Got Permission to Launch 1 Million AI Satellites — Your Next ChatGPT Answer Could Come From Orbit (Daily AI Update — November 26, 2026)

Look up. The next time you ask an AI chatbot a question, the answer might come from space. The U.S. Federal Communications Commission has authorized SpaceX to deploy up to one million satellites for a proposed network called Starmind — a constellation of computing stations in low Earth orbit designed to run artificial intelligence workloads above the clouds. Not internet satellites. Not cameras. Computers. SpaceX wants to move the data center itself into orbit, power it with sunlight that never sets, and beam the results back down to Earth. If it works, it would be the largest computing project ever attempted — and the strangest. If it does not work, it will still be remembered as the day AI officially left the planet. This is the Daily AI Update for Thursday, November 26, 2026: what the FCC actually approved, why anyone would put a data center in space, the enormous problems nobody has solved yet, and everything else that moved in AI today — from OpenAI quietly changing how your ChatGPT limits work to New York banning AI chatbot toys for five years.

Before we go further, here is the honest version of the headline. The FCC has cleared the way for the network to exist. It has not been built. No million-satellite constellation is in orbit today, and SpaceX still has to prove that computing at this scale can work beyond Earth. The authorization covers a potential network — permission, not a finished product.

What Actually Happened: The FCC Clears One Million AI Satellites

The Federal Communications Commission authorized SpaceX to deploy up to one million satellites for Starmind, a proposed network of computing stations in low Earth orbit. The idea is simple to describe and staggering to imagine: instead of building another giant data center on the ground, SpaceX would put the computers on satellites, let them run AI workloads using solar energy collected in orbit, and transmit the results back to Earth. The satellites would be computing stations first and communication relays second — a data center broken into a million pieces and scattered across the sky.

To understand why one million is such a startling number, compare it with what is already up there. SpaceX already operates thousands of Starlink internet satellites, the largest satellite fleet ever flown, and even that fleet counts in the thousands, not the millions. One million computing satellites would be a network orders of magnitude larger than anything humanity has ever put in orbit. Each satellite would be a node in a single distributed machine — a computer the size of the sky.

It is also worth saying plainly what this is not. This is not a launch that happened. It is not a working orbital data center. It is a regulatory green light for a proposed network, and the hard engineering is still ahead.

Why Put AI in Space? The Solar Power Argument

The case for orbital data centers starts with a problem every AI company is now living with: the computers that run modern AI are enormously hungry for electricity. Training a frontier model and then serving it to hundreds of millions of users takes data centers full of specialized chips running around the clock, and those chips draw power at a scale that strains local grids. In the United States and Europe, new data centers now compete for grid connections, land, water for cooling, and permits — and the waiting lists are measured in years, not months.

Space offers a way around the queue. Above the atmosphere, sunlight is constant and unfiltered by clouds or night — a satellite in the right orbit can collect solar energy around the clock. The pitch, as SpaceX has framed it, is that an orbital network could reduce its dependence on terrestrial electricity supplies entirely, running AI workloads on sunshine and sending only the answers back down. No land to buy. No grid connection to wait for. No local community to convince. Just sunlight and a radio link.

The Problems Nobody Has Solved Yet

Now the cold water. Scientists have questioned the economics and the engineering of orbital data centers, and their objections are serious. The biggest one is heat. A computer chip generates heat, and on Earth we get rid of that heat with air, fans, and water. In the vacuum of space there is no air, so a satellite can only shed heat by radiating it away — which is slow and inefficient. A million computers in space will need an extraordinary cooling system, and nobody has demonstrated one at this scale.

The second problem is debris. One million satellites is a staggering number of objects to keep track of in low Earth orbit, where objects travel at roughly 17,000 miles per hour. Every satellite is a potential collision, and every collision creates debris that threatens the rest. SpaceX will have to show regulators and the rest of the space industry that a million-satellite network can be operated safely, de-orbited reliably at the end of its life, and kept from turning low Earth orbit into a hazard zone. The FCC authorization is the beginning of that conversation, not the end of it.

The third problem is astronomy. Astronomers have already raised concerns about how thousands of bright satellites interfere with observations of the night sky, streaking through telescope images and drowning out faint objects. A million computing satellites would make that problem vastly worse unless the satellites are designed to be dark and unobtrusive. The scientific community will be watching this closely, and it has a legitimate seat at the table.

Then there is the economics question, which may be the hardest of all. Launching a satellite costs money, even on the cheapest rockets. Building a satellite that can survive years in orbit, communicate reliably, and compute efficiently costs money. Maintaining and replacing a million of them costs an almost unimaginable amount of money. The next milestone, as the day's coverage put it, is a working orbital data center that can demonstrate its costs and performance against Earth-based alternatives. Until SpaceX flies one and publishes real numbers, the orbital data center is a bet, not a business.

What This Means for You

If you are not an engineer or an investor, you might reasonably ask why a million satellites in orbit should matter to your daily life. The answer is that AI is becoming infrastructure, like electricity or the internet, and where that infrastructure lives decides how much it costs, how fast it answers, and who controls it. If orbital computing works, the ceiling on AI capacity lifts dramatically — more powerful models, cheaper to run, available everywhere on Earth with equal speed. If it fails, the industry will keep building data centers on the ground, and the constraints of land, power, and water will keep shaping what AI can do and what it costs.

There are also everyday implications that are easier to feel than to measure. Every AI feature on your phone — the assistant that summarizes your emails, the camera that recognizes what it sees, the app that translates a conversation in real time — runs on a data center somewhere. As those features get more powerful, the computers behind them get bigger and hungrier. An orbital data center is one answer to the question of where all that computing will live.

OpenAI Is Quietly Changing Your ChatGPT Limits

Buried in today's news is a change that affects millions of ChatGPT users directly. OpenAI will combine the usage limits for ChatGPT's Chat and Work experiences, meaning the two allowances that used to be separate will now draw from one shared pool. If you are a frequent user of both — chatting in the evening and working with the assistant during the day, for example — you may exhaust your shared allowance sooner than you are used to. The lighter Instant option remains available after the allowance runs out, and a wider rollout is planned for December.

Why does this matter? Because it is a sign of the same pressure behind the SpaceX story: AI usage is growing faster than the computing that powers it, and the companies are starting to manage demand. Combined limits are a rationing mechanism. If you use ChatGPT heavily for both work and personal use, December is the month to check your plan and your habits.

Anthropic Used Claude to Rebuild Claude — and Got 70% Faster

Anthropic published an account this week of how it used Claude agents to migrate its Messages API — the interface through which applications access Claude — from Python to Rust. The agents compared the two implementations against live requests and fixed discrepancies under human review. The results are striking: Anthropic reports roughly 70% lower processor usage per request and a 20% shorter median wait for the first response token.

This is one of those stories that sounds technical but matters to everyone who uses AI. The Messages API is the plumbing that connects your apps to Claude, and making it 70% cheaper to run per request is the kind of improvement that eventually shows up as faster answers, lower prices, or both. The migration also matters as a demonstration: the company used its own AI agents to rebuild its own infrastructure, checked the agents' work against live traffic, and kept humans in the loop for review.

Amazon and Meta Are Talking About AI Shopping — and Billions Are at Stake

Amazon and Meta have discussed lifting Amazon's September block on Meta's shopping agent, Muse. The two companies are still negotiating, with advertising revenue and control over search results remaining under discussion, and no deal has been announced yet. But the numbers explain why both sides are at the table: The Decoder cites Amazon's $68.6 billion annual advertising business, and reports that Muse accounts for 51% of AI-agent shopping orders.

Read that second number again: more than half of all shopping orders placed through AI agents already flow through Meta's Muse. That is a remarkable share for a category that barely existed two years ago, and it explains why Amazon's September block mattered so much. If you could not buy from Amazon through Muse, the biggest store on the internet was missing from the biggest AI shopping assistant. Lifting the block would put Amazon back in front of the fastest-growing shopping channel in tech — and it would hand Meta a stronger claim that its assistant is where people actually buy things.

For ordinary shoppers, the stakes are practical. AI shopping agents promise to do the tedious parts of buying — comparing options, building a basket, checking out — while you approve the result. The friction is who controls the search results, who gets the advertising money, and whose store the agent prefers. Watch for a deal announcement: if it lands, expect a rush of "buy it with Muse" buttons across Instagram and Facebook before the holiday shopping season ends.

New York Just Banned AI Chatbot Toys for Five Years

New York Governor Kathy Hochul has signed a law that bans the manufacture and sale of toys powered by AI chatbots for five years. It is one of the strongest AI safety measures any U.S. state has passed, and it lands squarely in the middle of the holiday shopping season, when AI-powered toys are on many gift lists.

Advertisement

The logic behind the ban is straightforward: a toy with a chatbot can hold open-ended conversations with a child, and parents and regulators worry about what those conversations might contain — inappropriate content, persuasive manipulation, or data collected from children too young to understand what they are sharing. Supporters of the law say five years gives lawmakers and researchers time to understand the risks and write better rules. Critics will say it bans a category before the evidence is in, and that it punishes responsible manufacturers along with reckless ones.

OpenAI's Private Inference: AI That Keeps Your Secrets

Sam Altman announced a limited customer pilot for a new offering called OpenAI Private Inference, part of a broader initiative under the name OpenAI Private Intelligence. The idea is to give businesses greater privacy when they run AI on sensitive work — the kind of work where the data cannot leave the building, or cannot be seen by anyone outside the company.

This matters because privacy has been the quiet barrier keeping AI out of the most sensitive corners of business: hospitals, law firms, banks, and government agencies all have information they cannot send to a shared cloud service. Private Inference is OpenAI's attempt to lower that barrier, letting companies use frontier models without exposing their data. The pilot is limited for now, but the direction is clear — the next phase of the AI race is not just about smarter models, but about models that organizations can actually trust with their secrets.

More AI News From Today, November 26, 2026

Today's AI news did not stop with satellites and shopping. Here is the rest of what moved, in brief.

A cheaper, more open model from Ant Group. Ant Group released Ling-3.2-air, a multimodal AI model that processes text, images, and video and targets professional tasks through software tools. Developers can download and commercially adapt its weights under the MIT license — one of the most permissive open licenses available. Ant reported a task completion rate of 61% on its own office-work automation test, up from 15.7% for its earlier Ling-3.0-flash-VL model. That is a nearly fourfold improvement in one generation, and the open license means anyone can build on it.

AI verified the code that protects your messages. Google DeepMind's AlphaProof Code resurfaced in coverage of how human-guided AI agents mathematically verified key security properties of the code behind Signal's encryption protocol. DeepMind originally announced the research on November 10. The technique uses mechanically checked proofs to establish specified properties of software, rather than relying only on test cases. It does not establish the security of every component of the Signal app — but for the core encryption code, a mathematical proof is the strongest guarantee available, and it was produced with AI assistance.

GPT-6.2 Astra solved a 200-year-old mystery in 22 minutes. According to a historian cited in today's coverage, GPT-6.2 Astra investigated a 200-year-old mystery in 22 minutes, finding a reference that led to a 162-page journal. It is a small story with a large implication: the same reasoning ability that makes these models good at writing and coding also makes them good at historical detective work, following a thread through archives that a human might take weeks to unravel.

Your old graphics card can now run a giant AI model. A company called Magnitude uses your computer's SSD storage to run models larger than your graphics card's memory, reporting 41 tokens per second on a 24 GB RTX 3090 graphics card using a 66 GB model. In plain language: a model that should not fit on a consumer graphics card now runs on one, at usable speed — putting powerful AI within reach of ordinary computers instead of expensive data centers.

AI agents that work while you sleep. Two new assistants promise to handle work even when you are away from your desk. MyClaw 3.0 lets you build a team of always-on AI agents with separate roles and shared memory, supporting OpenClaw, Claude Code, Codex, Hermes Agent, and Pi, plus more than 50 model options. And Epochal's Time Portal lets AI agents explore archived versions of the web and make predictions before the historical answers become available.

Anthropic expands in Zurich. Anthropic expanded in Zurich with plans for a larger office to recruit local AI researchers and establish a presence near some of the world's largest healthcare companies. Switzerland is becoming a quiet hub for AI research, and the healthcare angle is telling — drug discovery and medical AI are among the most valuable applications of frontier models, and the labs are going where the expertise is.
