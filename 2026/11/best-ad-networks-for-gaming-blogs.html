Your gaming blog gets 50,000 visitors a month — and earns less than a finance blog with 5,000. That is not a bug in your content. It is the economics of the gaming audience: huge, young, and armed with ad blockers. A gaming blog can still pay well in 2026, but only if you pick networks built for this audience instead of the default ones, and only if you stop treating display ads as your only income.

Why gaming is a special monetization case

The audience is enormous. There are over 3.3 billion gamers in the world (Newzoo, 2024), and browser-based gaming alone reaches 46% of online consumers aged 13–55 every month (Google × Newzoo, February 2026). Poki, the largest web-gaming platform, topped 100 million monthly active players in 2026. Traffic is the one thing a gaming blog never lacks.

The audience blocks ads. Around 64% of gamers use ad-blocking tools (inStreamly, 2026), and 47% of desktop gamers run an ad blocker (Statista, 2025). Ad-block users are also 40% more likely than average to be heavy gamers (Kantar TGI). Your most engaged readers are the most likely to never see a banner — any network you pick has to answer that, with anti-adblock recovery, formats blockers miss, or income that does not depend on display ads.

The CPMs are low, but the volume is real. Entertainment and gaming display CPMs sit around $1–$8 (AdCompares, 2026), against $25–$50+ for finance keywords. A gaming blog earns per visitor what a finance blog earns per click — and makes it up in traffic. That math only works if your network pays from a low floor and accepts your traffic from day one.

The audience accepts ads — on its terms. In Google and Newzoo's 2026 H5 gaming survey, 94% of web gamers recall seeing ads, and many prefer rewarded formats. Around 82% of mobile players would rather play a free game with ads than pay upfront (eMarketer). But 39% quit a browser game because ads interrupt play. Gamers do not hate ads — they hate ads that interrupt. Formats outside gameplay earn; formats that stop the game do not.

Best ad formats for gaming sites

Format choice matters more on a gaming blog than on any other niche, because your audience blocks banners and quits over interruptions. Ranked for gaming:

  1. Popunder — highest CPM, use sparingly. Opens behind the page on the first click, so it never stops gameplay and blockers often miss it. Tier-1 popunders run $3–$12+ (Corelume, 2026). The cost: reader goodwill — cap it at once per visitor per day.
  2. Social Bar / in-page push — the workhorse. A notification-style widget that shows to 100% of traffic including mobile, and survives most blockers because it is served in-page. Tier-1 CPMs around $2.50–$6 (community-reported). This is the format to run first on a gaming blog.
  3. Native banners — the polite earner. Blends into content as "recommended" boxes between sections. Lower CPM ($1–$5 on Tier-1), but readers tolerate it, and it stacks with the two above.
  4. Vignette / interstitial — high CPM, high risk. Full-screen units between page transitions or game restarts. They pay well ($4–$15 Tier-1, Corelume) but they are the interruption 39% of players say makes them quit (Google × Newzoo, 2026). Use only between sessions, never mid-game.
  5. Display banners — the weakest link. Classic 728×90 / 300×250 units. Lowest CPM ($0.30–$2), most blocked, least noticed. On a gaming blog, banners alone will not pay the hosting bill. Format-by-format guide: Adsterra Ad Formats Explained: Popunder, Social Bar, Native & More.

The ad-blocker problem — and what actually works

Do not pretend the problem away: with ~64% of gamers blocking ads, a chunk of your traffic will never see a display unit. What works, in order:

  • Pick a network with anti-adblock recovery. Monetag claims up to 25% of lost desktop gaming revenue recovered (Corelume, 2026) — test it, don't take it as a promise.
  • Run formats blockers miss. Popunder, Social Bar, and native units survive most blockers. A banner-only blog loses most of its gaming audience's impressions; popunder + Social Bar keeps most of them.
  • Earn from links, not impressions. Affiliate links are not ads in the blocker's sense — a "best gaming headset" link inside your guide renders as text and pays per sale. That is the next section, and for most gaming blogs it is the bigger income.
  • Ask, don't beg. A short note ("ads keep the guides free — whitelisting us helps") converts a small share of readers. A hard ad-block wall costs more readers than it recovers.
  • Respect the interruption line. Players value minimal ads (34%) and fast loading (37%) above almost anything else on a gaming site (Google × Newzoo, 2026). Every format costs goodwill; spend it where the CPM justifies it.

Gaming affiliate programs — the income that beats display ads

On a gaming blog, one affiliate sale routinely pays more than a month of display ads at gaming CPMs. The programs that fit gaming content (all figures community-reported, Oct–Nov 2026 — terms change, re-check before promoting):

  • Game keys — the natural fit. Fanatical pays ~3–10% (some roundups cite 10–15%) on digital keys and bundles, with a long 90-day cookie; CDKeys pays ~5% with a 30-day cookie; Green Man Gaming pays ~0.5–5% with a ~30-day cookie; Humble Bundle pays 10–15%. A "best indie games under $10" post with key links converts far better than any banner. Warning: G2A's Goldmine pays up to 5% with recurring commissions but carries reputational risk from past gray-market key controversies — research before promoting it.
  • Gaming gear — high order values. Razer pays up to 12%, Logitech 4–10%, Corsair runs a creator program, and Secretlab pays up to 12% on gaming chairs (7-day cookie). A $350 chair at 12% is $42 — more than a month of display ads on a small blog.
  • VPNs — the highest commissions in gaming. NordVPN pays up to 100% on the first sale plus recurring renewals (30–50% recurring is the commonly cited range); Surfshark pays 40% revenue share; ExpressVPN pays $13–$36 per signup. VPNs fit gaming content naturally (lower ping, region-locked games, privacy on public Wi-Fi).   • Amazon Associates — the easy start. No fixed traffic minimum to apply, 1–10% commission by category (some roundups cite 20% for gaming categories — treat the higher figure as unverified), but a new account must make 3 qualifying sales within 180 days or Amazon closes it, and the cookie is only 24 hours.   • Epic Games Support-A-Creator — 5% on in-game purchases (Fortnite, Rocket League), 14-day cookie. Small per sale, but it fits "best Fortnite settings" content perfectly.

The rule: promote only products you would recommend without the commission, and disclose every link. Gaming readers spot a paid shill faster than any other audience.

Honest pros and cons of monetizing a gaming blog

Pros

  • Traffic is the easy part: 3.3 billion gamers worldwide means a gaming blog can grow fast — volume is the one thing this niche never lacks.
  • Multiple income streams stack: display ads, game-key affiliates, gear affiliates, VPNs, and sponsorships can all run on the same traffic.
  • Low entry bar: Adsterra and Monetag accept gaming blogs from day one with no traffic minimum and a $5 first payout.
  • The audience accepts ads on its terms: 94% of web gamers recall seeing ads, and most prefer rewarded formats to paying upfront.

Cons

  • Ad blockers eat your impressions: ~64% of gamers block ads, so a banner-only setup earns a fraction of what your traffic suggests.
  • CPMs are low: $1–$8 for gaming display traffic against $25–$50+ for finance — you need volume, and volume takes time.
  • Interruption kills retention: 39% of players quit over ads that interrupt play, so the highest-paying formats are also the riskiest.
  • Affiliate income needs buying intent: a walkthrough audience rarely buys; a "best gear" audience does.
