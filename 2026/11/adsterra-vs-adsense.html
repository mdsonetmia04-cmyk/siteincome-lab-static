Adsterra vs AdSense (2026): Approval, CPMs & Payouts — Which Is Better for Beginners?

Every new blogger asks the same question sooner or later: should I start with Google AdSense, or with Adsterra? They are the two most common first networks — and almost opposites. AdSense is the safe, slow, strict one from Google. Adsterra is the fast, flexible one that accepts almost anyone.

This post compares them head-to-head on approval, entry requirements, ad formats, CPMs, payouts, revenue share, ad quality, account safety, and support. Every figure was checked in November 2026 against the networks' own pages or guides citing them — and where a network doesn't publish a number, the table says so instead of inventing one.

The short answer

If you want one sentence: start with Adsterra if your blog is brand new, and add AdSense once your site is established. Adsterra approves in about 10 minutes with no traffic minimum, so you can earn from day one. AdSense takes days to weeks, rejects thin sites routinely, and makes you wait for $100 before it pays — but its ads are cleaner, its brand is safer, and on US/UK traffic it is usually the more trusted long-term earner. The two are not exclusive: run both on separate placements, as many beginners do.

Approval: AdSense is a gate, Adsterra is a door

This is the biggest practical difference, and it decides the question for most beginners.

AdSense approval is a real review. Google says it usually takes a few days, but in some cases it can take two to four weeks. There is no published traffic minimum and no published post count — Google's eligibility page boils down to: you must be 18 or older, own the site, have original content that follows the program policies, and be able to edit your site's HTML. In practice, guides tracking approvals in early 2026 report that blogs with 15–25 substantial posts (800–1,000+ words each), an About page, a Contact page, a Privacy Policy, clear navigation, and an ads.txt file get approved fastest — often in 3–14 days. Thin sites get the vague "low value content" rejection, and Google does not explain itself. Reapplying means waiting at least two weeks and fixing the site first.

Adsterra approval takes about 10 minutes. You sign up, add your website, and the site status flips from Pending to Approved — usually while you are still looking at the dashboard. There is no traffic minimum, no post-count expectation, and it accepts sites in 248 GEOs, including the Tier-2 and Tier-3 traffic that premium networks ignore. AdSense's strictness protects the advertisers who pay for the ads; Adsterra's openness lets a brand-new blog earn from day one. A Blogspot blog can pass review too — this very blog runs on Blogspot — and the setup path is here: How to Monetize a Blogspot Blog in 2026 (AdSense + Adsterra Setup). Adsterra also accepts far more niches and traffic sources, including social traffic; if your blog is new, small, or non-English, Adsterra is the realistic first network, and with 20+ solid posts you can hold both accounts.

Ad formats: clean vs aggressive

AdSense formats are display, in-feed, in-article, anchor, and vignette ads — contextual, matched to your content, and the least likely to hurt your user experience, Core Web Vitals, or standing with Google. Since early 2024, AdSense pays per impression rather than per click.

Adsterra formats are popunder, Social Bar, native banners, display banners, Smartlink (direct links), and interstitials. The full format guide is here: Adsterra Ad Formats Explained (2026). The popunder — an ad that opens behind your content — is Adsterra's highest earner and its most controversial format. The Social Bar is a notification-style unit that is hard to miss.

The trade-off is real and you should choose it deliberately: AdSense's clean formats earn less per visitor but keep readers happy and coming back. Adsterra's aggressive formats earn more per visitor but cost you readers — especially the popunder on a small blog, where the few visitors you have are the ones you can least afford to annoy. A sensible beginner setup is Adsterra's native or banner formats only, with popunder switched off until traffic justifies it.

CPMs and RPMs: the honest numbers

This is the section where most comparison posts lie by omission, so here is the truth in full.

AdSense publishes no official RPM either — but its numbers are widely surveyed. Productive Blogging's 2025 survey of 187 bloggers (self-reported, mostly US/UK) found an average AdSense RPM of $2.34 per 1,000 pageviews. Publisher guides commonly cite a $2–$6 range depending on niche and country. Finance, software, and tech blogs sit at the top of that range; entertainment and general news sit at the bottom. Your RPM depends on niche, visitor country, season, and ad layout — Q4 pays the most, January the least.

Adsterra publishes no rate card at all. Any site quoting a fixed Adsterra RPM — "$X per 1,000 visitors, guaranteed" — is guessing. What publisher reports consistently show, directionally, is this: US/UK/Canadian/Australian traffic earns the most, Western European traffic earns less, and Tier-3 traffic earns the least — the same geography ladder as every other network, only steeper. Within the same GEO, finance, software, VPN, and crypto-adjacent niches pay more than entertainment or general content. Country-level detail is in a dedicated post: How Much Does Adsterra Pay Per 1,000 Visitors? (Real CPM Rates by Country)

On the same US/UK traffic, AdSense's reported $2.34 average is a low number — premium networks report $11–$35 on the same audience. Adsterra's directional range on Tier-1 traffic overlaps AdSense's range and can beat it on some GEOs and formats, especially popunder; on Tier-2/Tier-3 traffic, Adsterra usually earns where AdSense pays almost nothing. Payouts: minimums, schedules, and the first dollar

This is where Adsterra wins clearly for beginners, and the numbers are not close.

AdSense pays monthly, once your balance reaches $100 — earnings finalize around the 3rd of the following month, with payment issued between the 21st and the 26th. At a $2.34 RPM, a blog with 5,000 monthly pageviews earns about $12 a month — roughly eight months before the first payout. The money is real and reliable; it is just slow. Payment arrives by bank transfer, wire, or check depending on your country.

Adsterra pays twice a month on NET15, with minimums of $5 on Paxum/WebMoney, $25 on PayPal and local bank transfer, $100 on crypto, and $1,000 on wire. The same 5,000-pageview blog reaches a $5 payout in its first month or two. The full table, with the hold period and KYC notes, is here: Adsterra Payment Methods & Minimum Payout Explained (2026).

Third-party reviews quote different Adsterra thresholds (one says $50 PayPal, another $100) — the figures above are the ones checked against Adsterra's help center in October 2026 and this blog's own dashboard in November 2026. And the psychological difference matters more than the arithmetic: a beginner who sees their first $5 in month one keeps going, while one who sees $0 for eight months quits.

Revenue share: what each network keeps

AdSense is transparent here. Publishers keep 68% of advertiser spend on content ads when the advertiser buys through Google Ads. Since the 2024 restructure, Google describes it as publishers receiving 80% of the revenue remaining after the buy-side platform takes its fee — the same arrangement stated from a different point in the chain. Search ads are a different product at 51%.

Adsterra does not publish its revenue share — no official percentage exists in its help center or publisher terms. That is a genuine transparency gap: you cannot calculate what share of the advertiser's money you are getting, only what lands in your account.

Ad quality, safety, and support

Ad quality: AdSense wins without a contest — Google-vetted, contextual ads that don't break your layout or page speed. Adsterra's quality depends on which formats you switch on: native and banner formats are comparable to any display network, while popunder and interstitials interrupt readers and attract ad blockers. If your blog's value is reader trust, start with clean formats and use Adsterra's native/banner units only.

Account safety: both networks have a real record. AdSense is famous for sudden account bans with minimal explanation and no real appeal — invalid traffic is the most common trigger, and Google's fraud systems do not distinguish well between a publisher who bought bot traffic and one whose site was attacked by it. A banned AdSense account is effectively permanent, so the protection is boring and total: never click your own ads, never ask friends to click them, never buy traffic. Adsterra is less famous for bans, and its Trustpilot score of 3.2/5 reflects mixed reviews mostly about support speed rather than account terminations — but aggressive formats, misleading placements, and low-quality traffic can still get an account flagged. AdSense is strict at the door and strict inside; Adsterra is open at the door and stricter later.

Support: AdSense offers a help center, forums, and email — no live chat for most small publishers. Adsterra offers live chat (Intercom) from the dashboard; response times vary and a human answer can take up to 12 hours, but a chat window that exists beats one that does not.

Honest pros and cons

AdSense

Pros

  • The safest brand in advertising: Google-vetted ads that readers trust and that never hurt your standing with Google itself.
  • Published revenue share: 68% on content ads, stated openly — you can calculate what you should earn.
  • Clean ads, clean UX: contextual display ads that don't interrupt readers or wreck page speed.
  • Reliable payments: monthly, on a fixed 21st–26th schedule, once you clear $100.

Cons

  • Slow, strict approval: a few days to 2–4 weeks, routine rejections for thin sites, vague reasons.
  • $100 payout floor: at a $2.34 RPM, a small blog can wait 6–8 months for its first payment.
  • Sudden bans: invalid-traffic bans with minimal explanation and effectively no appeal.
  • Low RPM at small scale: the reported $2.34 average is the lowest among the major networks on comparable traffic.

Adsterra

Pros

  • Approval in ~10 minutes, no traffic minimum: the fastest way for a new blog to start earning.
  • $5 first payout: Paxum/WebMoney from $5, twice-monthly NET15 — you see money in month one.
  • Monetizes all GEOs: Tier-2 and Tier-3 traffic that AdSense barely pays for still earns.
  • Live support: a real chat window in the dashboard, plus a detailed help center.

Cons

  • No published revenue share or rate card: you cannot verify what cut Adsterra takes or what any fixed RPM should be.
  • Aggressive formats: popunder and interstitials pay best and annoy readers most.
  • Mixed reputation: Trustpilot 3.2/5, mostly about support speed.
  • Less advertiser trust: a lighter entry review means lower-quality inventory in some placements.

FAQ

Can I use AdSense and Adsterra together? Yes. Neither network requires exclusivity, so you can run AdSense on some placements and Adsterra's native or banner units on others. Don't run both networks' ads in the same placement, and never run a second network's ads inside a placement a managed network has claimed.

Which pays more per 1,000 visitors — AdSense or Adsterra? It depends on your traffic's geography. On US/UK traffic, AdSense's reported average is $2.34 per 1,000 pageviews (Productive Blogging 2025 survey, 187 bloggers), and Adsterra's directional range overlaps that — sometimes above it on popunder, usually near it on native/banner. On Tier-2/Tier-3 traffic, Adsterra usually pays more, because AdSense's auction has little demand for those audiences. Adsterra publishes no rate card, so treat any fixed Adsterra RPM you see quoted as a guess.

Is Adsterra legit? Yes — a real ad network operating since 2013, used by publishers in 248 GEOs, with a published help center, live support, and payouts on a NET15 schedule.
