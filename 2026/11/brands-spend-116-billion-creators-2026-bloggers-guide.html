Brands Will Spend $116 Billion on Creators in 2026: How Bloggers Can Win Their Share

Brands are about to spend $116 billion on creators in 2026, and the most important part of that number is not its size. It is where the money is moving. The sponsored post, the deal most people picture when they hear "influencer marketing," is no longer the biggest slice of the market. Paid amplification of creator content is. Brands are hiring creators the way they used to hire ad agencies, and bloggers who understand the shift can earn from it even with a small audience, because the brand now brings the distribution.

This guide breaks down the new Madison & Wall report behind the $116 billion figure, explains the five ways brand money actually flows to creators, and gives you a practical plan to position your blog and your content so brands can spend money on you. If you run a website and you have ever wondered whether brand deals are only for creators with millions of followers, the answer in 2026 is no, and the data proves it.

The $116 Billion Number, Explained

Madison & Wall, an advertising research and advisory firm, published a new report this week called "Following the Money: Sizing the Advertiser-Funded Creator Economy." Its headline finding is that marketers will spend about $116 billion globally on creator-related activity in 2026. That is up 15 percent from $101 billion in 2025, and the firm expects the total to reach $178 billion by 2030, growing about 12 percent a year. For comparison, Madison & Wall expects overall advertising to grow only about 6 percent a year over the same period. Creator spending is growing roughly twice as fast as advertising as a whole.

This growth is largely funded by reallocation, not new money: marketers are taking budget away from traditional media, digital performance advertising, and creative production, and moving it into creator activity. The creator economy itself grew about 18 percent a year from 2020 to 2025, against about 12 percent a year for the wider ad market. Creators have been taking share from the old advertising world for five years, and the pace is accelerating.

There is one important boundary to understand before you get excited. The $116 billion measures what marketers spend, not what lands in creators' bank accounts. It counts the full cost of the media inventory behind creator-led ad units, not only the creative fees paid to creators. It excludes consumer-funded income such as subscriptions, tips, merchandise, and courses, and it excludes platform payouts such as advertising revenue shares and creator funds. So the number is a map of where brand budgets are going, not a promise of what any individual creator will earn. But it shows exactly which kind of creator work brands are paying for.

Where the Money Actually Goes

Madison & Wall sorts every marketer dollar spent on creators into five mutually exclusive segments, and the ranking overturns the popular picture of how this market works. Three segments carry most of the weight, and understanding them tells you exactly what to build.

1. Media-Led Spending: $52 Billion (44 Percent)

The largest segment is media-led spending, worth about $52 billion in 2026, or 44 percent of the total: paid amplification of creator content, from boosting a post to whitelisting a creator's handle so the brand can run ads from it. The firm expects this segment to grow about 13 percent a year to $83 billion by 2030, nearly half of the projected $178 billion market.

This is the most important fact in the report for bloggers. The biggest slice of brand money no longer pays for access to a creator's audience. It pays for a creator's content, and then the brand buys the audience itself with paid media. A creator with a sharp eye, a clean edit, and a voice people trust can earn real money without a huge following, because the brand brings the media budget. Your job is to make content that works as an ad: a clear hook, a product shown in real use, and a simple call to action.

For a blogger, this changes the pitch completely: you are no longer selling your traffic, but your ability to make content that survives being shown to cold audiences as a paid ad. Display advertising still pays the bills on most blogs, and the rates vary enormously by country, which is why knowing how much ad networks actually pay per 1,000 visitors matters before you negotiate anything. But whitelisting and paid amplification are a second income stream that rewards craft over follower count.

2. Audience-Led Spending: $33 Billion (28 Percent)

The traditional model, in which a brand pays a creator to reach that creator's own audience through sponsored posts and ambassador deals, is now second, at 28 percent of the total, or about $33 billion. This is the classic influencer deal: a brand appears in front of your followers through a sponsored post, an ambassador role, or a podcast read.

This segment is still enormous, and it is still the most direct way for a blogger with a loyal readership to earn. A niche blog with 10,000 monthly readers who trust its recommendations can be more valuable to the right brand than a general-interest account with ten times the reach. The difference is that this is no longer the only game, and it is no longer the biggest one. If your entire monetization plan is "grow the audience, then sell sponsored posts," you are building for the second-largest segment while ignoring the largest one.

The practical lesson: build both muscles at once. Keep growing a loyal audience, because sponsored posts pay well and compound, but also build a small portfolio of ad-ready content, short videos, comparison posts, and tutorials that show a product in real use, so you can sell the media-led work too.

3. Asset-Led Spending: 12 Percent, and the Fastest Growing

The fastest-growing part of the market is the segment that treats creators as production companies. Asset-led spending, where brands pay for creator-made ad assets and then run them wherever they want, accounts for about 12 percent of the total. The firm expects it to grow about 16 percent a year through the end of the decade, faster than any other segment.

This is the creator-as-studio model: a brand hires a creator not for the audience, but for the ability to make content that feels native to a feed, then runs it on connected TV, display, or paid social under its own name. Early influencer marketing boiled creator value down to follower count, format, category, and cost per post. The asset-led model values something different: taste, speed, and authenticity that a traditional agency struggles to produce.

For bloggers, this is an open door: a product review, a how-to guide, or a comparison post is a short step away from a brand asset. If you can write a tutorial that ranks on Google, you can almost certainly shoot a 30-second version that a brand can run as an ad.

What "Creators as Ad Studios" Means for Bloggers

The phrase to remember from this report is "creators as ad studios." Brands are hiring creators the way they used to hire production companies, and the shift is visible in how the biggest advertisers behave. Digiday reports that Unilever works with around 500,000 creators. That is not a celebrity endorsement program. That is a supply chain. A company that large does not manage half a million relationships by hand; it uses platforms, briefs, and repeatable formats, and it needs creators who can deliver usable assets quickly and reliably.

For a blogger with a small site, the barrier to entry is lower than ever: brands running paid social need dozens of ad variations, not one perfect commercial. They need a creator who can show a kitchen gadget working in a real kitchen or a VPN app opening on a real phone. That takes a phone, decent light, and the habit of explaining things clearly, which is exactly what blogging teaches.

The Whitelisting Playbook: How Paid Amplification Actually Works

If media-led spending is the biggest slice of the market, whitelisting is the mechanism that moves most of it, so it is worth understanding in detail. When a brand whitelists your content, it gets permission to run your post as a paid ad from your handle. The ad looks like it comes from you, but the brand pays for the distribution and controls the targeting and the budget. You get paid for the content and the permission, and the brand gets an ad that performs better than its own polished creative, because it looks like a real recommendation.

Brands prefer creator content as ads for a simple reason: people scroll past polished brand creative and stop for content that looks like it belongs in their feed. A video shot in a real home outperforms a studio spot because it does not trigger the mental filter that says "this is an ad." Brands are no longer paying creators for reach. They are paying for the credibility that makes paid media work.

To prepare for whitelisting deals, keep a small library of your best-performing content, where a product appears naturally and the comments show real interest. Then write down your rates for three things separately: the content itself, the whitelisting permission, and the length of the permission. Brands expect separate line items, and bundling them into one price is how creators undercharge. Decide your minimum window before anyone asks: thirty days is a common starting point, and ninety days should cost meaningfully more.

Finally, protect your audience. A whitelisted ad runs from your handle, so your followers will see it and judge it. Only whitelist products you would genuinely recommend. One bad deal can cost you more in trust than it pays in cash, and trust is the asset that makes all of this possible.

Usage Rights: The Clause Where Bloggers Leave Money on the Table

The single most expensive mistake small creators make in brand deals is giving away usage rights for free. Usage rights are the contract terms that say where a brand can run your content, for how long, and whether paid advertising is included. They are where many creators undercharge, because the content fee feels like the whole deal, and the rights feel like paperwork. They are not paperwork. They are the product.

Advertisement

Think of it this way: a brand that pays you $200 for a video and then runs it as a paid ad for six months has bought $200 of creative work and an unlimited media run. If that video performs, the brand will spend thousands putting it in front of people. The fair price is the content fee plus a usage fee that reflects the value of the distribution, at least equal to the content fee for a 30-day paid-media window, and sharply higher for longer windows or television.

Bloggers have a natural advantage here: you would never let a company republish your article forever for the price of one post, and the same logic applies to video. Put your usage terms in writing before the work starts, even for small deals. A one-page agreement listing the content fee, the usage fee, the platforms, and the time window protects both sides, and brands respect creators who bring one.

The Geography of the Money

Where the money sits matters as much as how much there is. The United States accounted for 43 percent of creator spending last year, according to Madison & Wall, with the United Kingdom at 5 percent and Germany at 4 percent. The report also named India as the fastest-growing market, at around 20 percent. For a blogger, the message is straightforward: content aimed at US and European audiences sits closest to the money, and that is where brand budgets are already flowing.

This is also why the traffic geography of your own blog matters. A blog whose readers are mostly in the United States is worth more to a brand than a blog with the same traffic from a low-advertising market, because the brand's customers are there. If you are building a blog to earn, write for the audience the advertisers want, in the language they search in, about the products they buy.

None of this means creators outside the US and Europe cannot earn; India's growth rate shows the opposite. But be deliberate about who you write for. A blogger writing in English about software deals for American readers is competing for the biggest pool of brand money in the world. Geography is a choice, and it is one of the highest-leverage choices you make.

The Small-Creator Advantage, and the RPM Trap

The most encouraging part of this report is who it favors. The old model rewarded reach. The new model rewards the ability to make content that works as an ad, and that ability is not reserved for big accounts. Asset-led spending exists precisely because brands need many creators making many variations, and a creator with 5,000 engaged followers who can shoot a convincing product demo is more useful than a celebrity who has never used the product.

Advertisement

India's biggest podcaster illustrates the lesson. Raj Shamani told the Colin and Samir Show that clips of his podcast "Figuring Out" draw 1.6 billion views a month, while his main channel gets 110 million to 120 million. He sells bulk placements on those clips to brands that cannot afford his main channel. But his warning is the part to remember: he said AdSense pays his channel "not more than like 150k" a month, because India RPM is very bad, and he called judging a channel by its AdSense rate the "RPM trap." A thousand views in India might not bring the same advertising dollars as a thousand in America, but those thousand relationships can build a real business through sponsorships, clips, and direct deals.

That is the mindset shift this report demands. If you judge your blog only by its display ad revenue, you are measuring the smallest part of your earning potential. The real money is in brand spending, and brand spending follows creators who can make content that sells. Your RPM is not your value. Your ability to make a viewer trust a product is.

Your 7-Step Plan to Win a Share of the $116 Billion

Knowing the numbers is useful. Acting on them is what pays. Here is a practical plan any blogger can start this week.

Step 1: Pick One Product Category and Own It

Brands hire specialists, not generalists. Choose one category you genuinely use and understand, such as VPN software, web hosting, AI tools, or gaming gear, and make it your visible specialty. A blog with ten strong posts in one category beats a blog with a hundred scattered posts when a brand manager is choosing who to email.

Step 2: Build Three Ad-Ready Pieces

Make three short pieces that show a product in real use: a 30-second video, a before-and-after demonstration, and a tutorial that solves a real problem. These are your samples. When a brand asks what you can do, you send these, not your follower count.

Step 3: Publish a Media Kit Page

A media kit is a single page that says who your audience is, what you make, and what you charge. Include your monthly visitors, your audience geography, your best-performing posts, and your rates for content, whitelisting, and usage rights. Keep it updated. The premium ad networks have also lowered their entry barriers this year, as our guide to Mediavine and Raptive's new entry rules shows. A media kit is your version of the same idea: make it easy for money to find you.

Step 4: Learn the Language of Usage Rights

Before your first brand deal, write down your standard terms: content fee, usage fee, platforms, and time window. Use the rule from earlier in this guide and never give away paid-media usage for free. If a brand pushes back, you can negotiate the price, but do not negotiate away the concept. The creators who earn the most over time are the ones who treat usage rights as a product, not a favor.

Step 5: Diversify Beyond Display Ads

Display advertising should be one income stream, not the only one. Paid amplification, asset creation, sponsored posts, and affiliate revenue each reward different skills, and together they make your income resilient. Start with the stream closest to what you already do: reviews mean affiliate links, videos mean asset creation, loyal readers mean sponsored posts. And if you have never tried earning without AdSense at all, our guide to monetizing a website without AdSense lists seven real ways to start.

Step 6: Make Content That Survives Cold Traffic

Media-led spending means your content will be shown to strangers as a paid ad. The first three seconds must hook them. The product must appear in real use, not in a studio shot. The call to action must be one simple step. If your content only works for people who already trust you, it is a blog post. If it works for a stranger, it is an ad asset, and brands will pay for it.

Step 7: Track the Right Number

Stop judging your blog by pageviews alone. Track three numbers every week: how many brands contacted you, how many ad-ready pieces you published, and how much of your income came from outside display ads. When those three numbers rise, your share of the $116 billion is rising too.

The Bottom Line

Brands will spend $116 billion on creators in 2026 because creator content has become the most effective advertising there is. The sponsored post is no longer the biggest slice of that market. Paid amplification is, and the fastest-growing slice treats creators as production studios. That shift is good news for bloggers, because it rewards the exact skills blogging teaches: explaining a product clearly, earning a stranger's trust quickly, and making content that works for people who have never heard of you. Pick a category, build three ad-ready pieces, publish your rates, protect your usage rights, and start pitching. The money is moving. Make sure it can find you.
