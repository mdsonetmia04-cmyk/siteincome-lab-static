Black Friday TV Deals 2026: How Tech Blogs Earn From the Year's Biggest Shopping Searches

November 13, 20267 min readBy Sonet Mia

Black Friday 2026 falls on Friday, November 27 — two weeks from today — and the searches have already started. According to Similarweb's US search data, queries for "black friday tv deals" jumped 299% from August to September 2026, from 1,426 to 5,684 searches, while searches tied to "black friday deals" overall hit 80,600 in September, up 418% from August. TVs are the first category shoppers hunt for, ahead of laptops (+53%) and consoles. Adobe forecasts $63.3 billion in US online electronics spending this holiday season, up 5.9% on last year.

For tech bloggers, that demand curve is the opportunity. TV deal content is the highest-volume shopping content of the year — and unlike a product review, a deal post earns from readers who are ready to spend today. The blogs that earn the most from Black Friday are not the ones with the biggest teams. They are the ones whose deal posts are published, indexed, and updated before the shoppers arrive.

Why TV Deals Lead Black Friday Search Every Year

TVs are the perfect Black Friday product, and that is why they dominate the searches:

  • Big-ticket, big discount. A 65-inch Mini-LED TV at $597.99, down from $1,099.99 — 46% off, as one early deal already shows — is a saving worth searching for. Nobody Googles a 10% discount on a cable. Everybody Googles $500 off a television.
  • Confusing specifications. Mini-LED, QLED, OLED, refresh rates, HDMI 2.1, Dolby Vision — shoppers need a translator, and a blog post is the format they trust. Retailers list specs; blogs explain what they mean.
  • A replacement cycle with a deadline. People buy TVs for the holidays, for the Super Bowl, and for the living room they just redecorated. Black Friday is the year's cheapest moment, and shoppers know it.
  • Early deals stretch the season. Amazon's 12-day Black Friday sale opens November 19, and early deals from major retailers are already live. The search window is no longer one day — it is three weeks, from mid-November through Cyber Monday on November 30.

The pattern holds across electronics: shoppers search first, compare second, and buy third. Every stage of that journey is a pageview a tech blog can monetize — if the content exists when the search happens.

The Deal-Season Playbook for Tech Blogs

Publish early, update daily. The blogs that rank for "black friday tv deals" on November 27 are the ones that published in early-to-mid November and updated their posts as prices changed. Google rewards freshness during shopping events, and a post with a November 13 publish date and daily updates beats a post published on Black Friday morning. Keep one living deal post per category and update its timestamp with each change.

Answer "which TV should I buy?" — not just "what is on sale?" A list of prices is a commodity; every retailer has one. The content that ranks and gets shared is judgment: which 65-inch TV is the best value, whether Mini-LED is worth the premium over QLED, what refresh rate actually matters for gaming, and which "deals" are discounts off inflated list prices. Direct recommendations turn searchers into readers who come back.

Cover the whole season, not one day. Three phases, three sets of searches:

  1. Pre-Black Friday (now through November 26): "early black friday tv deals," "when do black friday tv deals start," "should I wait for black friday to buy a tv." Shoppers are researching.
  2. Black Friday week (November 27–30): "black friday tv deals," "best 65 inch tv black friday," "walmart vs best buy tv deals." Shoppers are buying.
  3. Post-sale (December 1–15): "tv deals still available," "cyber monday tv deals," "best tv deals december." Late shoppers and gift buyers keep searching after the main event.

Write for the living room, not the spec sheet. The reader is standing in a store aisle or scrolling on a phone, deciding between two models. Plain language wins: screen size by viewing distance, what HDR actually looks like, whether the soundbar is necessary. The spec sheet belongs in a table at the bottom, not in the headline.

Compare retailers honestly. Prices differ between Amazon, Best Buy, Walmart, and Target — and the same model number sometimes hides different panels. A post that compares the same TV across retailers, flags the differences, and notes when a deal has sold out earns more trust (and more return visits) than a post that copies one store's listing.

Link deal content to evergreen content. A reader who finds your TV deal post should reach your other tech guides in one click — and your monetization guides, if they run a blog of their own. Deal season is also the moment many readers start thinking about earning from their own sites. Monetizing Deal Pages Without Affiliate Programs

Many deal blogs earn through retailer affiliate programs — and those programs are worth joining. But affiliate approval takes time, and a new blog should not wait for it to earn from deal season. Display advertising pays from the first pageview, and deal pages earn more per pageview than ordinary content because the readers are buyers: advertisers pay premium rates to reach people who are actively shopping.

The setup that works for a new tech blog:

  • Start with the gentle formats. Native Banner and Social Bar earn steadily on deal pages without pushing readers away. A deal page's job is to keep the reader comparing — intrusive formats that drive them back to Google cost more than they earn.
  • Place ads where the reader pauses. Below the first deal table, between comparison sections, and at the end of the post — the natural stopping points, not the headline.
  • Update the post, keep the ads. A living deal post earns for three weeks. Update prices and stock daily, and the same page keeps earning through every phase of the season.

The Honest Math of Deal-Season Earnings

Deal content earns more than ordinary tech content, but the numbers still need a reality check:

  • Traffic is seasonal. A TV deal post that earns 20,000 pageviews in Black Friday week may earn 200 a week in January. The money is real, but it is concentrated — plan the year around it, do not budget as if it lasts.
  • CPMs rise with buyer intent. Shoppers are worth more to advertisers than casual readers, so deal pages typically earn a higher rate per thousand impressions than the same blog's news posts. The exact rate depends on geography: US and Western European traffic pays several times more than traffic from other regions.
  • Freshness is the ranking factor. A deal post that still shows a sold-out price on November 29 will lose its ranking to a post that was updated that morning. The update habit is the business model.
  • AI traffic is the new channel. Adobe forecasts AI chatbot traffic to US retail sites will rise 130% year over year this season, and Similarweb reports AI referrals to major retailers nearly doubling last November. Clear, factual, well-structured deal posts are the ones AI assistants cite — another reason to write for accuracy, not hype.

Black Friday TV deals are the biggest shopping searches of the year, and the season is already underway. The tech blogs that earn from it will be the ones whose deal posts are published this week, updated daily, and honest about which deals are actually worth buying.
