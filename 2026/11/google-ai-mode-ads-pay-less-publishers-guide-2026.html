Google Admitted AI Mode Ads Pay Less — What Publishers Must Do Before AI Search Takes Their Traffic

On October 28, 2026, Google told investors something every website owner should have heard. Its chief business officer, Philipp Schindler, admitted on Alphabet's third-quarter earnings call that the rapid growth of AI Mode queries was dragging down search advertising growth, because ads in AI Mode earn less than ads in traditional search. In his words, "the strong growth in AI Mode queries had an impact because of the lower monetization we were seeing in AI Mode." For bloggers and small publishers, that single sentence explains a lot about the traffic and revenue pressure they have felt all year. The searches that used to send readers to their pages are increasingly being answered inside Google itself, and the ads around those answers are worth less than the ads on the old results page.

What Google Actually Said on October 28

Alphabet's third-quarter 2026 earnings call was, by most measures, a strong one. Google Search and other advertising revenue reached 63.35 billion dollars, up 12 percent on the same quarter a year earlier. But the growth rate told a different story: search ad growth had been 19 percent in the first quarter of 2026 and 17 percent in the second, and in the third quarter it fell to 12 percent. Schindler gave investors four reasons: foreign exchange swings, European customs rule changes, advertising normalising after the FIFA World Cup, and the lower monetisation of AI Mode queries. Three of those four are temporary. The fourth is not.

The admission matters because of who made it and where. This was not a blog post or a help page. It was a statement to Wall Street, on the record, from the executive in charge of Google's advertising business. Schindler also made clear that Google is not slowing down: in the same call he said the company's ad experiments in AI surfaces had shown promising results and that Google was scaling ads inside both AI Overviews and AI Mode to more users and more queries, including the new international markets and multiturn ads in the United States. Google knows this inventory earns less today, and it is expanding it anyway, because the users are already there.

The Numbers Behind the Slowdown

The earnings numbers behind the admission show the shape of the change. Alphabet reported its highest search query growth in five years in the third quarter of 2026, and chief executive Sundar Pichai credited AI investment for that growth. People are searching more than ever. The problem is that the growth is happening in the wrong place, commercially speaking. Paid clicks rose 8 percent year over year, down from 13 percent in both the first and second quarters, while the average cost per click rose 4 percent. Advertisers are paying more per click, but the number of paid clicks is growing more slowly, even as total queries grow at their fastest rate in five years. That gap is the AI Mode effect in a single picture: more questions, fewer monetisable clicks.

Two other numbers from the call help explain why. Queries in AI Mode run about three times longer than traditional search queries, according to Pichai, and one in five AI Mode searches globally now starts with voice, an image, or a document rather than typed text. A three-word search for a product is close to a purchase. A thirty-word conversation with an AI assistant is usually the beginning of research. Google's own head of search data science told advertisers in the same period that people now arrive earlier in the buying journey, asking long questions far from a purchase. Advertisers bid on intent, and when intent drops, bids drop with it.

The squeeze is visible across the publishing industry, not just in Google's accounts. Ziff Davis, one of the largest digital publishers in the United States, reported a 37 percent decline in organic traffic tied to AI search displacement. Taboola, the content recommendation company, reported a 6.9 percent quarterly revenue decline and pointed directly at fewer ad impressions caused by AI-generated search summaries reducing publisher traffic. These are large, professional publishers with strong brands, and AI search is taking a measurable bite out of their traffic and revenue. If it is happening to them, it is happening to smaller sites too, only with less cushion.

Why AI Mode Queries Pay Less

There are three structural reasons why an AI Mode query is worth less than a traditional search, and none of them is going away.

First, the layout offers fewer places to sell. A traditional results page is a list of ten blue links with ad slots above, below, and beside them. An AI Mode answer is a single synthesized response with less room for ads, and the ads that do appear sit inside or after an answer that has already satisfied much of the user's curiosity. Fewer auction slots and a user who already has an answer is a recipe for lower prices.

Second, the intent is weaker. Traditional search rewards the advertiser because the user has typed a short query that usually signals what they want to buy, read, or do. AI Mode queries are longer, more conversational, and earlier in the journey. Advertisers bid on intent, and when intent drops, bids drop with it.

Third, the click itself is disappearing. In traditional search, the only way to get the answer was to click a result. In AI Mode, the answer is on the screen before the user ever visits a website. Every question answered inside Google is a click a publisher never receives, and every click a publisher never receives is an ad impression that never happens.

AI Mode Ads Are Expanding Anyway

While the earnings call was still being digested, Google was already rolling out the expansion Schindler had described. Ads in AI Mode are now live in ten more countries: Australia, Canada, India, Kenya, Malaysia, New Zealand, Nigeria, Pakistan, the Philippines, and Singapore. Brazil, Mexico, and the United Kingdom are scheduled to follow in the fourth quarter of 2026. At the same time, Google launched multiturn ads in the United States, so ads can now appear on follow-up questions inside a conversation, not just on the first query. If each query is worth less, the answer is more queries per session, and multiturn ads are how Google monetises the extra turns.

For publishers, the expansion has two consequences. The first is that the shift is now global, not an American experiment. The second is that competition inside AI answers will intensify: as more advertisers learn to buy AI Mode inventory, the publishers who want to be cited inside AI answers will compete for a smaller number of visible slots than the old ten blue links ever offered. Google has said click-through rates are already similar between sponsored and organic product listings inside AI Mode, so the opportunity is real. It is simply narrower, more competitive, and further up the funnel than the search traffic most bloggers were built on.

AI Overviews and AI Mode Are Not the Same Thing

One of the most common mistakes publishers make right now is confusing AI Overviews with AI Mode. They are different surfaces, and Google has said different things about how they earn.

AI Overviews are the summaries that appear at the top of a classic search results page. Google has previously said that ads on AI Overviews earn at the same rate as ads on a results page without overviews: the overview sits above the links, but the links are still there. AI Mode is different. It is a separate, conversational experience where the AI answer is the page and the links are secondary. The October earnings call was the first time Google admitted that AI Mode specifically earns less than traditional search. The surface where the answer replaces the links is the one that pays less.

The confusion matters because it leads publishers to the wrong conclusion. A blogger who hears that "AI Overviews monetise the same" may decide nothing needs to change, while their traffic is quietly being absorbed by AI Mode. Google Search Console now offers a control that lets publishers exclude their content from Google's AI features, open worldwide since August 31, 2026. It is an all-or-nothing switch, with page-level controls not expected until March 3, 2027, and Google has shared impressions from AI features without the click data publishers need to judge the trade. The United Kingdom's regulator has required Google to provide UK publishers with click data by December 3, 2026, but Google has not confirmed it will do the same globally.

What This Means for Your Blog's Traffic Right Now

The honest answer is that it depends on what kind of content you publish, and the differences are large. Sites that answer simple factual questions are the most exposed: if your most-read post is a definition, a quick fact, or a short how-to that an AI can summarise in two sentences, that traffic is the easiest to absorb. Sites that publish opinion, original reporting, and detailed comparisons are far harder to replace, because the answer is a point of view, a test, or a story.

The November 2026 core update makes this distinction more urgent. Google began rolling it out on November 10, 2026, and it is expected to keep rolling through November 24. It is the third core update of the year, and it explicitly targets site reputation abuse, Google's term for publishing low-quality third-party content on a trusted domain to borrow its ranking power. Publishers who expanded fast with thin, outsourced, or rented content are the ones at risk, while publishers with genuine expertise and original material have less to fear.

The New Math: Impressions Matter More Than Clicks

The AI Mode shift arrives on top of a change publishers are still absorbing. Since 2024, Google AdSense has paid publishers per impression rather than per click. AdSense publishers now keep 80 percent of the revenue after the advertiser's platform takes its fee, which works out to roughly 68 percent when ads are bought through Google Ads. The practical result is that volume and visibility matter more than ever, and a niche site with a small but highly engaged audience that used to earn well from clicks now earns far less from the same visitors.

This is why the AI traffic squeeze hurts twice. First, AI answers take the visit, so there are fewer page views. Second, the page views that remain are worth less, because the payment model has already moved to impressions. A publisher who only watches clicks will miss both halves of the problem. The numbers that matter now are page RPM and revenue per session. A site with 3,000 monthly page views and a page RPM of 2 to 5 dollars earns roughly 6 to 15 dollars a month from display ads. That is not a business. It is a hobby with a tip jar.

This is also why so many small publishers are rethinking their ad stack in November 2026. Raptive, one of the premium managed ad networks, recently cut its entry requirement from 100,000 to 25,000 monthly pageviews. Mediavine, its main rival, has dropped its old 50,000-session rule. The premium networks are moving downmarket because the traffic is consolidating and the rates are volatile, so the publishers who survive will be the ones who diversify early. Seven Moves Publishers Should Make Now

None of the changes in this article can be undone by a single publisher. What a publisher can do is adapt faster than the publishers around them. These seven moves are practical, cost little or nothing, and can all be started this week.

1. Publish what AI cannot summarise

Shift your content plan toward original work: real tests, prices you verified yourself, real screenshots, and real opinions. A comparison you actually ran beats one an AI assembled from other people's pages. Audit your ten most-read posts and ask whether an AI answer could replace each one. The ones that fail that test are your rewrite list.

2. Optimise to be cited inside the AI answer

Being cited inside an AI Overview or AI Mode answer is becoming the new page one, and the answer usually names only a handful of sources. Put a clear, direct answer in the first paragraph, use headings that match the question, and use lists, tables, and short factual sentences that can be lifted cleanly. Add original data, prices, and dates, and keep them current.

3. Build traffic that does not come from Google

The most important number in a publisher's business is the share of traffic that does not depend on Google. Email subscribers, social followers, direct visitors, and returning readers are traffic no algorithm can take away. Start or restart an email list and send something genuinely useful every week. A list of a thousand engaged readers is worth more than ten thousand search visits, because the list comes back on your schedule, not Google's.

4. Fix your ad stack for an impression world

Stop running a single ad network and hoping for the best. If you are on AdSense alone, add a network that pays on impressions and accepts smaller sites, so every visitor earns something. Test placements rather than guessing: above the fold, inside the article, and below the content behave very differently. Review your page RPM weekly while the core update is still rolling. 5. Chase commercial intent, not just traffic

Not all page views are equal. A thousand visits from people researching a purchase are worth far more than ten thousand visits from people who wanted a quick fact. Shift weight toward comparisons, best-of lists, deals, and buying guides, where the reader is close to spending money. A Black Friday hosting guide earns at the intersection of high traffic and high intent, which is exactly where advertisers pay the most.

6. Make every page earn from every visitor

In a world where each visit is harder to win, wasting a visit is the most expensive mistake a publisher can make. Every page should have a clear path to revenue: display ads that actually load, an affiliate link where it genuinely helps the reader, an email signup, or a related article that keeps the session going. Check your site on a real phone, on a slow connection. If your ads do not load, you are losing money on traffic you already worked for.

7. Watch the right dates and decide with data

Three dates matter before the end of the year. The November core update finishes rolling around November 24, so wait until then before judging whether your rankings have changed. UK publishers should receive AI click data from Google by December 3, the first real evidence of how much traffic AI features are taking. Brazil, Mexico, and the United Kingdom get AI Mode ads in the fourth quarter, which will show whether the monetisation gap narrows as the format matures. Until then, do not make irreversible decisions on the basis of a feeling. Make them on the basis of your own Search Console and revenue data.

Where Adsterra Fits in an AI Search World

There is a temptation to treat the AI shift as a reason to give up on display advertising. That would be a mistake. Display advertising is not dying. It is changing shape. The old model assumed a publisher could rely on one network, one traffic source, and one kind of visitor. The new model rewards publishers who combine sources: search traffic where it still converts, social and email traffic they own, and ad formats that earn from every visitor, not only the ones who click.

That is the practical case for adding a network like Adsterra alongside AdSense rather than replacing one with the other. Adsterra accepts publishers that AdSense rejects, it pays on impressions as well as clicks, and its minimum payouts are low: 5 dollars through Paxum or WebMoney and 25 dollars through PayPal, on a NET15 schedule. For a small publisher whose traffic is volatile while the core update rolls out, the difference between a 100-dollar AdSense threshold and a 5-dollar threshold is the difference between being paid this month and being paid next quarter. Its Social Bar and Smartlink formats also earn from visitors who never click a banner, which matters more with every month that passes.

None of this is a reason to abandon AdSense. For high-traffic sites in strong geographies, AdSense remains a solid earner. The point is that a single-network strategy is now a single point of failure, and the publishers who survive platform changes are the ones who never let one company decide whether they get paid. If you are starting from zero, the smartest move is to sign up for a second network now, while your traffic is still healthy, rather than in a panic after a bad month.

What to Watch Next

The next six weeks will tell publishers a great deal. The November core update finishes rolling around November 24, and the sites that recover or fall after that date will show what Google is rewarding in the AI era. December 3 will bring the first AI click data for UK publishers. The fourth-quarter expansion of AI Mode ads into Brazil, Mexico, and the United Kingdom will show whether the monetisation gap narrows as the format matures. And the holiday season will show whether the surge in advertiser spending can lift rates enough to hide the structural problem for one more quarter.

The longer view is clearer. Google is not going to make AI Mode earn less forever, and Schindler said as much. But the gap will not close because publishers wait. It will close when advertisers learn to value early-journey traffic, when AI answers learn to send more clicks, and when publishers learn to be cited inside the answers instead of competing with them.

The Bottom Line

Google has admitted, on the record, that ads in AI Mode earn less than ads in traditional search, and it is expanding AI Mode to more countries and more queries anyway. Search ad growth has already slowed from 19 percent to 12 percent this year, AI Mode queries are three times longer and far earlier in the buying journey, and large publishers are reporting real traffic losses to AI answers. Publishers cannot reverse this shift, but they can adapt: publish original work AI cannot summarise, structure content to be cited inside AI answers, build traffic Google cannot take away, fix their ad stack for an impression-based world, and chase commercial intent instead of raw page views.
