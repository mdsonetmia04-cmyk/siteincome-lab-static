Sinterklaas 2026: The Netherlands' Biggest Gift Night — Dutch Families Spend €134 on Gifts, and Online Sellers Are Missing It

On Saturday, December 5, 2026, the Netherlands will do something the rest of the world barely notices: it will hold its biggest gift night of the year, and it will not be Christmas. It is pakjesavond — presents evening — the climax of Sinterklaas, the Dutch gift-giving holiday that predates Santa Claus by centuries. Dutch families planned to spend an average of €134 on Sinterklaas gifts in 2025, according to the annual holiday survey by lootjestrekken.nl, and the money moves in a single week: in 2023, the Dutch Payments Association counted around 114 million debit card payments worth about €2.85 billion in the seven days up to and including Sinterklaas Eve. And yet almost nobody writing in English covers it. For bloggers and online sellers, Sinterklaas is a gift market hiding in plain sight — a top-10 CPM country, a nation that shops online every week, and a holiday with almost no English competition. This is the full guide: what Sinterklaas is, what Dutch families spend, what they buy, and how an online seller gets a share of it.

What Is Sinterklaas? The Dutch Gift Holiday That Came Before Santa

Sinterklaas is the Dutch celebration of Saint Nicholas, and it is older than the Santa Claus the rest of the world knows — in fact, Santa Claus grew out of it. Dutch settlers carried Sinterklaas customs to New Amsterdam, and the figure evolved into the Santa of today. But in the Netherlands, the original never went away. Every year, Sinterklaas arrives by steamboat from Spain in mid-November — in 2026, the national arrival was on Saturday, November 14, in Grave, in North Brabant — and travels the country for three weeks until pakjesavond, the evening of Saturday, December 5, when families exchange presents. The next day, Sunday, December 6, is his feast day, Saint Nicholas Day. Then he leaves, and Christmas, on December 25 and 26, is celebrated separately, mostly with religious observance and big family meals. Most Dutch families exchange their gifts on Sinterklaas, not on Christmas. That single fact is the whole opportunity: in the Netherlands, December 5 is the gift night. December 25 is dinner.

The season has its own rhythm, and it starts weeks before the presents. From mid-November, children put out their shoe in the evening — schoenzetten — usually by the fireplace or the front door, often with a carrot for Sinterklaas's horse, Amerigo. The next morning they find a small treat or gift in the shoe, a schoencadeautje: some pepernoten, a mandarin, a small toy. The daily children's news programme, the Sinterklaasjournaal, follows his journey every day from November 9 until December 5. The treats are in the shops from September: pepernoten and kruidnoten, small spiced biscuits; chocolate letters with your initial; marzipan; speculaas; and banketstaaf, an almond-paste pastry. The whole country spends three weeks building toward one evening, and on that evening, the presents come out, the poems are read, and the surprises are unwrapped.

The Numbers: €134 Per Family and a €2.85 Billion Week

The money behind Sinterklaas is real, and it is measurable. Dutch families planned to spend an average of €134 on Sinterklaas gifts in 2025, according to the lootjestrekken.nl holiday survey. That is the average across the country, and the spending breaks down into layers. For a shoe gift, most parents stick to €1 to €3 — a small toy, a few sweets, a sticker sheet. For the big present on pakjesavond, it is usually between €15 and €40, depending on the child's age and whether the gift comes from the parents or the whole family. When a group draws names — lootjes trekken — they agree on one amount beforehand, often €10 to €25 per gift. A family with two children might set aside €120 for Sinterklaas Eve, €30 for shoe gifts, and €30 for treats and other costs, from a total budget of €180. The numbers are modest per gift, but they multiply across millions of families, and they all land in the same three weeks.

The payment data shows how concentrated the spending is. In the seven days up to and including Sinterklaas Eve in 2023, the Dutch Payments Association recorded an estimated 114 million debit card payments worth around €2.85 billion. The busiest day was the last Saturday before the holiday, with around 20.4 million payments worth €579 million in a single day. Toy, hobby, and sports shops performed particularly well, with daily transaction numbers 45% to 75% higher than usual. Bookshops, jewellery shops, and department stores also performed well, with transaction numbers up to 50% higher per day. And the online share keeps growing: the number of payments to online shops rose by roughly 10% in the seven days before Sinterklaas in 2023 compared with the year before. Sinterklaas is shopping increasingly online — even if the vast majority of presents are still bought in physical shops, the direction is clear, and every year the online slice gets bigger.

How Pakjesavond Actually Works: Poems, Surprises, and Shoe Gifts

Pakjesavond is not a normal gift exchange, and the difference is what makes it interesting for sellers. In families with young children, the presents are said to come from Sinterklaas himself, and the evening is magic: a knock at the door, a sack of presents, the children's faces. In families with older children and adults, the evening turns into a game. Names are drawn — lootjes trekken — and each person buys one gift for one other person, usually within the agreed budget of €10 to €25. But the gift alone is not enough. It must come with two things: a surprise and a poem. The surprise is a handmade creation built around the gift — a paper boat for someone who loves sailing, a cardboard guitar for the musician, a shoebox castle for the dreamer. The poem is a funny rhyming verse about the person receiving the gift, teasing them gently about their habits, their hobbies, their year. The poem and the surprise are what make pakjesavond different from a normal gift exchange, and they are also what make it hard to buy for: the gift must suit the poem, and the poem must suit the person.

That structure creates three separate shopping moments, and each one is a market. First, the shoe gifts: from mid-November to December 5, children put out their shoe almost every evening, and every morning something small must be in it. Shoe gifts cost €1 to €3 each, and a family needs dozens of them across the season — stickers, ironing beads, a toy car, a sweet treat, a chocolate letter. Gift sites list over 150 shoe gift ideas starting from €0.99. Second, the big pakjesavond present: €15 to €40, one per child, chosen with care, often from a wish list the child made online. Third, the lootjes gift for adults: €10 to €25, one gift, one poem, one handmade surprise. A seller who understands these three moments can sell to the same family three times in three weeks, with three different products at three different prices. A seller who only sees "Christmas gifts" sees one moment, and misses the other two.

What Dutch Families Actually Buy

The shopping lists for Sinterklaas are practical, and they follow the season. For the shoe, something small costing a few euros: stickers, ironing beads, a toy car, or a sweet treat. For pakjesavond, one bigger present that suits the child — and with older children, often a surprise after drawing names. The categories that spike in the Sinterklaas week tell the story: toy, hobby, and sports shops see daily transactions 45% to 75% higher than usual, and bookshops, jewellery shops, and department stores see up to 50% more. Books are a strong Sinterklaas gift in the Netherlands, and the book market treats the season as one of its biggest weeks. Toys lead, because the holiday is built around children. Games follow, because pakjesavond is a family evening and the gifts are often things to play with together. And the food — the pepernoten, the chocolate letters, the speculaas, the marzipan — is bought by everyone, for the shoe, for the table, and for the office.

The wish list has moved online, and that matters for sellers. Dutch families now make gift lists on sites like Kindkado, where one list per child is shared with a single link and the family ticks off what they are giving, so nobody buys the same thing twice. The list is the purchase order, and the store that appears on the list is the store that gets the sale. For an online seller, getting onto a Dutch family's Sinterklaas list is worth more than any advertisement, because the list is shared with grandparents, aunts, uncles, and godparents — one list, many buyers. The sellers who win Sinterklaas are the ones whose products are easy to put on a list: clear prices, clear ages, clear delivery dates, and a page that a grandmother can open on her phone and understand in ten seconds.

Bol.com: The Store That Beats Amazon in the Netherlands

Anyone who wants to sell to the Netherlands needs to know one fact first: Amazon is not the biggest online store there. Bol.com is. Founded in Utrecht and owned by Ahold, bol.com was the Netherlands' first widely successful e-commerce platform, and it was still used by about twice as many shoppers as Amazon in 2024, according to Statista. The Dutch shop online constantly — over a third of consumers in the Netherlands shopped for goods online on a weekly basis in 2023 — but they shop on bol.com first. For a seller, that changes everything. An Amazon listing reaches the Dutch shopper second. A bol.com listing reaches them first. And a gift guide in English that links to bol.com is something almost nobody has built, because the English-language gift world assumes Amazon is everywhere. In the Netherlands, it is not.

The practical lesson is simple: if you write about Sinterklaas gifts, link to where Dutch shoppers actually buy. Bol.com carries the toys, the books, the games, and the gifts that fill the shoe and the pakjesavond table. Amazon.nl exists and grows, but the Dutch shopper's first stop is bol.com, and the seller who meets them there gets the sale. The same logic applies to the content itself. The Dutch read English fluently — the Netherlands has one of the highest English proficiency rates in the world — but almost no gift content is written for them in English. A Sinterklaas gift guide in English, with real prices in euros and real links to bol.com, serves a market of 17 million people that the English-language internet has mostly ignored. That is the gap, and it is wide open.

How to Sell to Sinterklaas Shoppers: Timing, Lists, and the Poem

Selling to Sinterklaas shoppers is mostly about timing. The season starts in mid-November, when the Sinterklaasjournaal begins and the shoe goes out for the first time, and it ends on December 5. The shoe gifts sell from mid-November onward, a few euros at a time, every day. The big presents sell in the last ten days, when the wish lists are finished and the budgets are set. And the lootjes gifts sell in the last week, when the names are drawn and the poems are written. A seller who lists Sinterklaas products in October is early. A seller who lists them on December 4 is too late. The window is three weeks, and the last week is the biggest. The same is true for content: a Sinterklaas gift guide published in late November catches the whole buying wave. Published on December 5, it catches nothing, because the presents are already under the tree.

The second rule is the list. Dutch families shop from shared wish lists, and the seller's job is to be on the list. That means clear product pages with the price in euros, the age range on toys, the delivery date before December 5, and a link that can be shared in a family chat. The third rule is the poem. A gift for pakjesavond is not just a product — it is a present with a poem and a surprise attached. Sellers who sell the experience win: a gift set that comes with wrapping paper and a poem card, a craft kit for building the surprise, a book of Sinterklaas poems for the parent who has to write one by Friday. The poem is the part nobody can buy ready-made, and the seller who helps with it — a template, a rhyme guide, a funny example — earns the trust that turns into the sale. Sell the gift, and you make one sale. Sell the evening, and you make the customer.

Sinterklaas vs Christmas: Two Holidays, Two Gift Nights

Visitors to the Netherlands in December are often confused, because the country seems to celebrate twice. It does, and the two holidays are different on purpose. Sinterklaas, on December 5, is the gift night: presents, poems, surprises, children, excitement. Christmas, on December 25 and 26, is the family holiday: religious observance for some, big meals, quiet days, and a calmer mood. Most Dutch families exchange gifts on Sinterklaas, not on Christmas, and Christmas itself is more of a family-meal holiday, like a calmer extension of Sinterklaas. The distinction matters for sellers, because the two holidays have two different shopping lists. Sinterklaas is toys, games, books, and chocolate letters. Christmas is food, drink, candles, and the table. A store that stocks for Christmas and ignores Sinterklaas misses the gift season entirely, because by Christmas the presents are already given. The gift money moves in November. The food money moves in December. Know which one you are selling to.

The confusion also creates an opportunity for content. English speakers searching for Dutch Christmas traditions find pages about Sinterklaas and pages about Christmas, and most of them mix the two up. A clear guide that explains the difference — Sinterklaas is December 5, the gifts; Christmas is December 25, the meal — ranks for both searches and serves both readers. The same is true for the history. Sinterklaas is the original Dutch gift-giving holiday, a centuries-old tradition that predates Santa Claus, who grew out of the customs Dutch settlers took to New Amsterdam. Telling that story is not just interesting — it is the reason the holiday exists, and it is the reason an English-speaking audience cares. Everyone knows Santa. Almost nobody knows he started as Sinterklaas.

The Bottom Line

Sinterklaas 2026 ends on Saturday, December 5, with pakjesavond — the Netherlands' biggest gift night, three weeks after Sinterklaas arrived by steamboat in Grave on November 14. Dutch families planned to spend an average of €134 on gifts in 2025, shoe gifts cost €1 to €3, the big present runs €15 to €40, and the whole country moved about €2.85 billion through its debit cards in the Sinterklaas week of 2023. The shoppers buy on bol.com twice as often as on Amazon, they shop from shared wish lists, and they need the gifts before December 5, not after. For bloggers and sellers, the lesson is simple: the best holiday to cover is the one nobody else covers, in a country that pays some of the highest ad rates in the world. Sinterklaas is that holiday. The presents are on December 5. The guide is here now. The rest is just showing up before everyone else figures it out.
