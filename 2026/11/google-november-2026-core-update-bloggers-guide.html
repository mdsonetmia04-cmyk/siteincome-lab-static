Google November 2026 Core Update: What Bloggers Must Do Before Black Friday

Google started rolling out its November 2026 core update on November 10, 2026, and if you run a blog that earns money from ads, your traffic graph probably looks strange right now. Rankings are moving in waves, some pages are up, some are down, and the timing is the worst part: the rollout is expected to finish around November 24, just days before Thanksgiving, Black Friday on November 27, and Cyber Monday on November 30 — the biggest earning week of the year for most publishers. This guide explains what the update actually is, how to tell whether your traffic drop is the update or something else, and the exact steps to take before, during, and after the rollout.

If your rankings moved this week, you are not alone, and you are probably not being punished. A core update is not a penalty. It is Google re-judging which pages best answer a search, and every site in the world gets re-judged at the same time. The bloggers who come out of a core update stronger are rarely the ones who react fastest. They react calmly, record what happened, fix real technical problems, and wait for the rollout to finish before judging the result.

What Happened: The November 2026 Core Update in Plain English

Google logged the November 2026 core update on its Search Status Dashboard on November 10, 2026, at 10:06 a.m. Pacific time. The entry says the update applies globally and to all languages, and that the rollout may take up to two weeks to complete. A full two-week run would finish around November 24. Google described it on LinkedIn, through its Search Central team, as a regular update designed to better surface relevant, satisfying content for searchers from all types of sites. It published no separate Search Central blog post about this update, and it named no sector, content type, or system the update targets.

That vagueness is normal, and it is the reason so much bad advice appears during every core update. Google almost never tells publishers exactly what changed. What it does tell us, every time, is the same set of facts: a core update is a broad change to the main ranking systems, it is not aimed at individual websites, and a page that drops in rankings is not necessarily a bad page. Google may simply have decided that other pages now answer the search better. That distinction decides what you should do next: if a page is bad, fix the page; if Google simply re-ranked the results, make the page genuinely more helpful.

This is the third confirmed core update of 2026, following the March and May releases. The March update ran for 12 days and 4 hours, and the May update for 11 days and 21 hours. If this one follows the same pattern, it finishes between about November 21 and November 28 — and Google's own wording allows it to run the full two weeks to around November 24. Either way, the rollout ends right on top of the Thanksgiving shopping week.

Why This Update Hits Bloggers Harder Than Most

For a blogger who earns from advertising, traffic is income. Fewer Google clicks means fewer page views, and fewer page views means fewer ad impressions. A core update does not touch your AdSense or Adsterra account directly, but it moves the organic traffic that feeds those accounts, and in the fourth quarter that traffic is worth more than at any other time of year. Advertisers pay more to reach shoppers in November and December, so every lost click in this window costs more than the same click would have cost in February.

What the March update tells us about the scale

We do not yet have hard data on how much this November update is moving rankings, because it is still rolling out. But the March 2026 core update gives us a sense of the scale these updates can reach. After the March update, SE Ranking found that 79.5 percent of URLs in the top three positions changed position, up from 66.8 percent after the December 2025 update. About 24.1 percent of pages in the top ten fell out of the top 100 entirely, against 14.7 percent in December. Both figures were reported by PPC Land. An analysis by SEO consultant Aleyda Solis, using Sistrix data, found that aggregators, directories, and comparison sites took the heaviest losses in March.

Those numbers are a warning, not a prediction. Every core update is different, and November may be gentler or harsher than March. But they explain why your rankings can move a lot in a single week without anything being wrong with your site. When nearly eight in ten top-three results change position, almost every publisher feels it.

Is Your Traffic Drop the Core Update — or Something Else?

A drop that starts the same week as a core update is not always caused by it. Before you blame the algorithm, rule out the other usual suspects, because each one has a different fix. Work through this checklist before you change a single word on your site.

First, check the dates. Google's September 2026 spam update ran from September 24 to October 8. If your drop started between those dates, look at the spam policies first. A core update will not undo a spam problem. If your rankings were steady through October and started moving on or after November 10, the core update is the likely cause. If your traffic was falling before September 24, look elsewhere: a site redesign, a hosting problem, broken tracking, or a competitor who simply published something better.

Second, check Search Console itself. Search Engine Roundtable reported on November 9 that the Performance report looked wrong for many sites, before the update even began. A data anomaly in Search Console can look exactly like a traffic drop. Before you conclude that you lost clicks, compare your analytics tool with Search Console. If analytics shows steady visits while Search Console shows a cliff, the problem may be in the reporting, not in your rankings.

Third, check for manual actions and security issues. Both have their own reports in Search Console, and both can strike in the same week as a core update by pure coincidence. A hacked site that serves spam pages to Google can lose rankings overnight, and no amount of waiting for the rollout to finish will fix it. If either report has an entry, that is your real problem, and it outranks everything else in this guide.

Fourth, list your own changes. Every plugin update, theme change, migration, redirect rule, and robots.txt edit from the last few weeks belongs on this list. Bloggers often change three things at once, then blame Google for the result. If you changed your theme, moved your site, or edited a batch of URLs in late October or early November, your traffic move may be self-inflicted, and the fix is to undo the change.

Finally, check whether the drop is real or just one page. Open the Performance report, filter to the pages that lost the most clicks, and look at the queries that drove them. A single page losing one big query usually means a competitor published something better for that query. Twenty pages losing a little each usually means the update reassessed your site as a whole. You cannot know which one you are dealing with until you look at the page level, and the fix is different in each case.

Your Plan While the Rollout Runs

Google's own guidance for core updates is clear: wait until the rollout is finished, then wait at least one more full week, then compare your Search Console data. That sounds like doing nothing, and it is not. There is a real job to do during the rollout — it is just not rewriting your content. Here is the plan, step by step.

Step 1: Mark the date and save a baseline

Open Search Console and export the Performance report for November 3 to November 9, the full week before the rollout began. Save total clicks, total impressions, average position, and your top 20 pages and top 20 queries. Do the same in your analytics tool. This baseline is the single most valuable thing you can produce this week, because without it you will be guessing in December about what actually changed.

Step 2: Rule out technical problems first

A traffic drop that is not caused by the core update has a different fix, and technical problems are the most common non-update cause. Check that your site loads, that your most important pages return a 200 status, that your sitemap is reachable, and that nothing in your robots.txt is accidentally blocking Google. Check the Page Indexing report for a sudden spike in errors, and check Core Web Vitals for pages that became slow after a plugin or theme change.

Step 3: Separate search from Discover

In Search Console, look at the Search results report and the Discover report separately. If search is steady and Discover fell, your problem is Discover: it rewards fresh, visually strong, genuinely interesting content, and it punishes clickbait and recycled news. If Discover is steady and search fell, your problem is the core update. If both fell at once, start with the technical checks, because a site-wide problem is more likely than two separate ranking problems arriving on the same day.

Step 4: Do not rewrite, delete, or redirect anything yet

This is the hardest step, and the most important one. Rewriting pages or deleting content in the middle of a rollout is how site owners turn a temporary dip into a lasting one. Rankings move in waves during a rollout: a page can drop on Tuesday, recover on Friday, and drop again the following Monday. If you rewrite the page on Wednesday based on Tuesday's numbers, you are reacting to noise. The same applies to deleting underperforming posts. A post that looks dead during the rollout may recover when the rollout finishes, and once you delete it, its history, its links, and its rankings are gone for good. Make a list of pages you suspect need work, and do the work after the rollout is marked complete — not before.

After the Rollout Ends: How to Judge the Damage Honestly

When Google marks the rollout complete on the Search Status Dashboard, do not judge your site the next morning. Wait at least one more full week, then compare that week in Search Console with the week before November 10. Compare pages and queries, not just totals. A site that lost 15 percent of its clicks overall may have lost one page that drove half its traffic, while everything else is fine. Or it may have lost a little on every page, which points to a site-wide reassessment. The totals cannot tell you which one happened.

For the pages that genuinely lost rankings after the rollout, read Google's own guidance on core updates and ask the honest questions it suggests: does the page provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis? Does it give a complete description of the topic? Is the content written by someone who knows the topic, and would you trust it enough to share it with a friend? If the answer to several of those is no, you have found your work. Improve the page in a real way — add the missing information, update the outdated facts, answer the follow-up questions.

The Black Friday Problem: Ranking Swings in the Biggest Earning Week

The timing of this update is genuinely awkward, and it is worth saying so plainly. If the rollout runs its full two weeks, it ends around November 24. Thanksgiving falls on November 26, Black Friday on November 27, and Cyber Monday on November 30. That means rankings may still be settling when advertisers are paying the most and shoppers are searching the hardest.

The practical answer is to stop relying on organic search alone for this one week. If your Black Friday plan depends entirely on Google sending you traffic, you have a single point of failure, and this update just showed you how fragile it is. Push your best deals content through every channel you already have: your email list and your social accounts. Update the deals posts you already rank for — prices, availability, and end dates — because a fresh, accurate deals page is the kind of page that tends to hold its ranking through a volatile week. There is also a defensive move worth making this week: protect the pages that earn you money. If a small number of pages drive most of your ad revenue, check them every day during the rollout. Make sure they load fast, that no ad unit is broken, and that no recent edit damaged them. If your ad setup itself needs work, this is a good week to review it: our guide to Adsterra ad formats explains which formats earn best in high-traffic weeks, and our comparison of AdSense alternatives is worth reading if all of your income depends on a single network.

How to Protect Your Ad Revenue During the Volatility

A core update moves traffic, and traffic moves income, but the size of the income swing depends on how your monetization is set up. A blog with one ad network, one format, and one traffic source feels every ranking change in full. A blog with two networks, several formats, and traffic from more than one channel feels the same update as a smaller bump.

Start with formats. In a high-traffic week, the formats that earn best are the ones people actually see: a well-placed native banner inside the article, a 300x250 unit near the top of the content, and a Social Bar unit that stays visible while the reader scrolls. Popunders can add real revenue during deal season, but they are also the format most likely to annoy a reader who arrived from a search and is deciding whether to trust your page. During a volatile week, trust is the asset you cannot afford to spend.

Next, look at your traffic mix. If more than 80 percent of your visits come from Google search, you have a concentration problem, and this update is the evidence. You do not fix that in a week, but you can start: publish one piece this week that is designed for a different channel, such as a deals roundup for your email list. The goal is not to replace Google. The goal is to have one more channel that still works on the day Google does not.

What NOT to Do During a Core Update

Do not mass-delete old posts. A thin post from 2024 may still earn links, still rank for long-tail queries, and still contribute to your site's topical depth. Deleting it in a panic removes all of that, and you cannot undo it. If a post is truly worthless, noindex it or improve it.

Do not buy links, traffic, or "SEO fixes" from anyone who contacts you this week promising to recover your rankings. There is no service that can reverse a core update, and the sellers know it. The money is gone, and the links they build can create a real spam problem on top of a temporary ranking dip. If someone guarantees recovery by a date, they are lying.

Do not change your URLs, your site structure, or your theme in the middle of the rollout. Each of those changes forces Google to re-learn your site at the exact moment it is already re-judging it, and the combination makes your data unreadable. You will not know whether a later change in traffic came from the update or from your own edit.

Do not rewrite your best pages based on one bad day of data. Rankings during a rollout move in waves, and a page that looks broken on Tuesday can be back on Friday. Wait for the rollout to finish, wait one more week, and only then decide what needs rewriting. And do not chase the winners, either. If a competitor's page jumped above yours, read it, learn from it, and then write something better — do not copy it.

One more mistake is easy to overlook: do not ignore AI content questions while you are at it. If your site uses AI-assisted writing, the core update is a good moment to check that every AI-assisted post still reads like a human expert wrote it, with real detail and real opinions. Our guide on whether Google penalizes AI content covers the rules in full, but the short version is simple: Google does not penalize AI content as a category, it penalizes unhelpful content whatever produced it.

Frequently Asked Questions

When will the November 2026 core update finish?

Google said the rollout may take up to two weeks from its start on November 10, 2026, which puts the end around November 24. The March update took 12 days and 4 hours and the May update took 11 days and 21 hours, so a finish between November 21 and November 24 is the most likely outcome. Google will mark the rollout complete on its Search Status Dashboard.

Will my traffic recover on its own?

Sometimes, yes. Rankings move in waves during a rollout, and pages often recover some or all of their lost positions as the rollout settles. But recovery is not guaranteed. If your pages genuinely fell because other pages now answer the query better, the rankings will not come back until your pages improve.

Is this update targeting AI content?

Google did not say so. It described the update as a regular one, with no named target, and its guidance on AI content has not changed: content is judged on whether it helps the reader, not on how it was produced. A thin AI-written page that adds nothing will struggle in a core update, but so will a thin human-written page.

The Bottom Line

The November 2026 core update started on November 10, it is the third core update of the year, and it will probably finish around November 24 — days before the biggest earning week of the year. That timing is stressful, but the update itself is ordinary: a broad re-ranking that moves almost every site, not a penalty aimed at yours. Save your baseline from November 3 to November 9, rule out technical problems, manual actions, and the September spam update before you blame the algorithm, and do not rewrite, delete, or redirect anything until the rollout is marked complete and you have waited one more week. Then compare your pages and queries, improve the pages that genuinely lost, and leave the winners alone. Protect your Black Friday earnings by leaning on every channel you have, not just Google. Core updates reward the bloggers who stay calm and get better.
