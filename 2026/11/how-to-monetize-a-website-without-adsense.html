How to Monetize a Website Without AdSense (7 Real Ways, 2026)

Google AdSense is the default answer to "how do I earn from my website" — and for many publishers, it is also the first wall they hit. Approval can take weeks and still end in rejection. Even after approval, the $100 payout floor means a small site can wait months for its first payment. And AdSense is famous for sudden account bans with minimal explanation and no real appeal.

Here is the good news: AdSense is one monetization method, not the only one. Plenty of websites earn real money without ever touching it. This post covers 7 real ways, ranked from easiest for a beginner to hardest, with honest pros and cons for each. No get-rich-quick claims — every dollar figure here is a clearly labeled illustration, not a promise.

Why people leave AdSense (and when it is still worth applying to)

Before the alternatives, the honest diagnosis. People usually look for a life without AdSense for one of four reasons:

  • Approval is slow and strict. AdSense reviews your site for original content, policy compliance, and a minimum site age. New or thin sites are routinely rejected, and reapplying after a rejection can take weeks each time.
  • The $100 payout floor is brutal at low traffic. AdSense pays monthly, but only once your balance clears $100. A blog earning $8 a month waits more than a year for its first payment.
  • Bans come with no real appeal. AdSense can disable an account for invalid traffic or policy violations — sometimes triggered by things outside your control — and the appeal process rarely reverses it.
  • The RPM is low for small sites. In Productive Blogging's 2025 survey of 187 bloggers, AdSense averaged $2.34 per 1,000 pageviews — the lowest of any network in that survey. At 5,000 monthly pageviews, that is about $12 a month.

None of that makes AdSense worthless. With a clean, content-rich site and mostly Tier-1 traffic (US, UK, Canada, Australia), it is still worth applying to: its ads are the gentlest on user experience, and it pays reliably once you clear the floor. 3. Affiliate marketing — the best pay per visitor for beginners

Affiliate marketing means recommending products you genuinely use, with a link that pays you a commission when readers buy. A single affiliate sale can pay more than a month of display ads on a small blog — which is why this is the method that most often out-earns ads for beginners.

The classic starting point is Amazon Associates: free to join, no fixed traffic minimum to apply, and commission of roughly 1–10% by category. But it has a trap beginners miss — a new account must make three qualifying sales within 180 days or Amazon closes it. The cookie window is only 24 hours (stretching to 89 days once the item is in the cart), and payment arrives roughly 60 days after month end, with a $10 minimum by direct deposit. Build the content first; the 180-day clock starts when you sign up, not when you start getting traffic.

The higher-paying end is SaaS and hosting affiliates: software companies pay 20–40% recurring commissions, so one referred customer can pay you every month for years. VPN, hosting, and SEO tools are the usual starting points in this niche — and they fit naturally into "best X for Y" comparison posts, which is exactly the content type that converts.

Honest pros: The best income per visitor of any method here at low traffic — one sale beats a month of ads. No traffic minimum to start, and the products you recommend can be ones you already use and trust.

Honest cons: It only works when your content targets people ready to buy ("best X for Y", reviews, comparisons) — informational posts rarely convert. Commissions change without warning, cookies are short, and every link must be disclosed — undisclosed affiliate links are illegal in the US and destroy trust. A thousand visitors who never buy earn you $0.

Best for: Blogs in buying niches (tech, software, VPNs, tools) with comparison and review content. The niche economics behind it: Best Blogging Niches That Make Money in 2026.

4. Sponsored posts — brands pay you to write about them

Once your site has steady traffic, brands will pay you to publish a post about their product — a review, a roundup mention, or a dedicated article. Rates vary wildly by niche: a sponsored post on a finance or software blog pays many times one on a lifestyle blog, because the audience is worth more to the advertiser.

Honest pros: The highest pay per post of any method here — a single sponsored post can pay more than a month of display ads. You control the topic and the price, and the work is writing, which you already do.

Honest cons: Realistic only after you have steady traffic (typically 10,000+ monthly visitors) and a media kit — brands don't sponsor blogs nobody reads. Sponsored content must be disclosed by law in the US, and undisclosed sponsorships destroy reader trust fast. It is also lumpy income: feast months and dry months, with no guarantee of the next deal.

Best for: Established blogs in commercial niches with a media kit and a traffic screenshot to prove the audience. Not a month-one method — a year-one or year-two one.

5. Selling digital products — highest margin, most work

Ebooks, templates, courses, printables, prompt packs, presets. You keep nearly 100% of the price, and the product sells while you sleep — the closest thing to passive income that actually exists. A $29 template that sells 10 times a month is $290 a month from a single product, with no ad network and no middleman.

Honest pros: The highest margin of any method here — no revenue share, no payout floor, no algorithm deciding your rate. Your product is also an asset: it keeps selling from posts you wrote months ago.

Honest cons: Creating something worth buying takes weeks, and selling it takes an audience that trusts you — a year-two method, not a month-two one. You also handle support, refunds, and updates, and a bad product damages the trust your whole blog runs on.

Best for: Bloggers with a specific, proven skill their audience asks about repeatedly — the product should answer a question your readers already ask you.

6. Services and freelancing — fastest cash, least passive

Freelance writing, consulting, coaching, or done-for-you work in your niche. This is the fastest way to your first $100, because you are trading skill for money directly — no traffic threshold, no approval queue, no payout floor. Many bloggers use services to fund the blog's early months, then phase them out as ad and affiliate income grows.

Honest pros: The fastest first income of any method here. No minimums of any kind — one client is enough. It also teaches you what your audience will actually pay for, which makes every other method smarter.

Honest cons: It stops paying the moment you stop working — the least passive income here. It also eats the time you would spend writing posts, so it can slow the blog's growth, and it does not scale: there are only so many hours in your week.

Best for: Anyone who needs money next month, not next year — and for bloggers whose niche skill (writing, design, SEO, teaching) is directly sellable.

7. Donations, memberships, and direct ad sales — the audience-pays-you methods

The last group skips the middleman entirely: readers pay you directly.

  • Donations (Buy Me a Coffee, Ko-fi): A tip jar on your site: readers who love your work send $3–5. No minimums or approval — but donations alone rarely pay a bill, so treat them as a bonus, not a business model.
  • Memberships (Patreon, Substack, paid newsletter): Readers pay monthly for extra content, early access, or a community. This is the most stable reader-funded income, but it needs a loyal audience that already trusts you — typically hundreds of regular readers before the first paying member appears.
  • Direct ad sales: You sell a banner or sponsored slot on your site directly to a business, at a flat monthly rate, with no network taking a cut. The rate is yours to set — but you do the selling, the invoicing, and the reporting yourself, and you need enough traffic for a business to care.

Honest pros: No network, no payout floor, no revenue share, no algorithm. The money comes straight from the people who value your work, which makes it the most resilient income here.

Honest cons: Every one of these needs an audience first — donations and memberships pay nothing until readers care enough to open their wallets, and direct ad sales need traffic numbers a business can justify. They are also the most hands-on: you are the sales team, the support desk, and the billing department.

Two warnings that save more money than any method switch:

  1. Don't chase all seven at once. Pick the method that fits your traffic level and run it for 30 days. One working income stream beats three half-built ones.
  2. Traffic first, monetization second. Every method above pays almost nothing at 100 monthly visitors — and aggressive formats (popunders on day one) drive away the few readers you have. Grow content first; experiment after 1,000 sessions a month.

Honest pros and cons of going AdSense-free

Pros

  • Faster first payout: Adsterra and Monetag pay from $5; AdSense makes you wait for $100. For a small blog, that is the difference between seeing money in month one and month six.
  • Higher pay per visitor exists: Affiliate marketing and sponsored posts can pay many times AdSense's $2.34 average RPM — if your content targets buyers.
  • No ban risk from one company: Your income does not depend on a single approval decision that can be reversed without explanation.
  • Traffic that AdSense ignores still pays: Adsterra and Monetag monetize Tier-2/Tier-3 traffic and non-English audiences that premium networks won't take.

Cons

  • AdSense is the safest network: Google's ads are the least likely to hurt your user experience, your Core Web Vitals, or your standing with Google itself. Aggressive formats (popunder, push) earn more per visitor and cost you readers.
  • Most alternatives are less passive: Affiliate links, sponsors, products, and services all need more work per dollar than pasting ad code once.
  • Income is lumpier: Sponsored posts and product sales come in bursts; ad networks pay on a schedule. A mixed stack smooths this out, but a single method does not.
  • Some methods need an audience first: Sponsors, memberships, and products pay $0 until you have traffic and trust — exactly when a beginner needs income most.
