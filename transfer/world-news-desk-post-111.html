TITLE: Nvidia Just Halted AI Server Shipments to 4 Countries — Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia Frozen Out by New US Export Rules LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Nvidia has told Dell and Supermicro to stop shipping AI servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia under new US export controls. ---BODY---

Nvidia has told its manufacturing partners to stop shipping servers containing its most advanced artificial intelligence chips to four South-East Asian countries, in the most dramatic escalation yet of Washington's campaign to keep cutting-edge American computing power out of Chinese hands. The Financial Times reported on November 26, 2026, citing three people familiar with the matter, that the chipmaker had instructed partners including Dell and Super Micro Computer to halt shipments to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. The partners have in turn told customers in the four countries that Nvidia will not allow their orders to be shipped. It is not clear how long the suspension will last, and Nvidia declined to comment on the report. But the message to the market was unmistakable: the world's most valuable chip company is now willing to freeze sales across an entire region rather than risk breaching American export controls.

The move follows new rules from the United States Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security, announced on November 16 and in effect since November 19, that require American government authorisation for exports of advanced computing hardware to the four countries, as well as to Brazil. The rule, formally titled "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors", is aimed at a workaround that Washington says Chinese companies have been using for years: instead of buying restricted chips directly, they rent computing power from data centres in third countries that have bought servers packed with Nvidia graphics processing units. The four South-East Asian countries have become favoured destinations for exactly that kind of arrangement, and Singapore in particular has grown into one of the largest markets in the world for Nvidia hardware. The Financial Times report landed just days after Nvidia's Singapore subsidiary disclosed revenue of 104 billion dollars for 2025, a rise of 72 percent on the previous year, a figure that shows just how much business is now caught in the freeze.

For Nvidia, the timing could hardly be worse. The company has spent the past week lurching from one crisis to another. Its chief executive, Jensen Huang, resigned suddenly and was then reinstated by the board on November 23 after a unanimous vote, sending the company's market value up by about one trillion dollars in a single day. Days later, on November 25, Nvidia shares fell 8.6 percent in the worst day for the Nasdaq since 2008, as the wider market plunged on fears about tariffs, inflation and the artificial intelligence boom itself. Now the company faces a compliance freeze across four of its fastest-growing markets, with no clear date for resumption. The shares have become a proxy for the entire AI trade, and every new restriction on where its chips can go is read by investors as a direct threat to the growth story that has carried the stock to extraordinary heights.

What exactly has Nvidia done — and what has it not done

According to the Financial Times, Nvidia has instructed its manufacturing partners to halt shipments of AI servers to the four countries. Dell and Supermicro, which package Nvidia's chips into finished servers, have told buyers in Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia that their orders cannot be shipped under Nvidia's policy. The report was careful about what it did not establish. It is unclear whether the halt is temporary while Nvidia and its partners work out how to comply with the new American rules, or whether it will last longer. It is also unclear whether the suspension covers all server shipments or only those containing the most advanced chips. And the report does not establish that any existing servers already installed in data centres in the four countries are being shut down or switched off. What is clear is that new orders are not moving, and that customers who had expected deliveries are now being told to wait.

The distinction matters. A temporary compliance pause is a very different event from a permanent withdrawal, and Nvidia's own history suggests the company will look for a lawful route back into these markets as quickly as it can. The new American rule is not a blanket ban. It introduces a licensing requirement, which means shipments can resume once licences are granted. Qualifying American-headquartered end users and companies with American-headquartered ultimate parents are excluded from the destination-based licensing requirement altogether, subject to reporting obligations. And cloud providers that serve Chinese customers have 90 days to obtain a licence by proving that their end users do not include restricted entities. The 90-day window is the crucial detail: it gives the industry a defined period to sort out who its customers really are, and it gives Nvidia a defined period to decide which shipments it can lawfully resume.

But the pause is real, and it is already disrupting plans. Operators that had intended to sell GPU computing power to Chinese companies are among those most directly affected, and the new regulations are explicitly designed to disrupt exactly that business model. The Commerce Department's rule targets overseas data centres that provide significant volumes of computing power to companies headquartered in countries classified by the United States as national security concerns, including China. Under the framework, data centre operators, chip exporters and other entities in the supply chain must conduct due diligence on their customers, including checking their ownership and control structures, to establish who ultimately accesses the computing resources and whether restricted entities could benefit from them. That is a far heavier burden than simply checking a shipping address, and it is the reason Nvidia's partners have chosen to stop shipping while they work out how to comply.

The loophole Washington is trying to close

To understand why the United States has acted now, it helps to understand the loophole. For years, Washington has restricted direct exports of advanced AI chips to China. But a Chinese company that cannot buy a chip can still, in principle, rent time on a chip that sits in a data centre somewhere else. A server in Singapore or Malaysia, loaded with Nvidia's most powerful graphics processing units, can be accessed remotely from anywhere in the world. The computing happens in South-East Asia; the benefit accrues in China. American officials have argued for years that this arrangement defeats the purpose of the export controls, and the new rule is the most direct attempt yet to close it. The rule does not only cover physical shipments of chips. It also covers remote access to advanced computing power, targeting high-volume computing arrangements involving companies headquartered in China, Macau or other designated jurisdictions, including companies whose ultimate parents are headquartered there.

The four countries were chosen because they are the places where the workaround has been most visible. Singapore, in particular, has become a hub for the trade. Nvidia's Singapore subsidiary reported 104 billion dollars in revenue for 2025, a figure that is difficult to square with the size of Singapore's domestic market alone and that has fuelled long-running questions about where the chips ultimately end up. Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia have also attracted heavy investment in data centre capacity, much of it explicitly aimed at serving customers across Asia, including China. The Commerce Department's designation of the five countries — the four in South-East Asia plus Brazil — as presenting a higher-than-normal risk of diversion is a formal recognition of what American officials have been saying informally for months: that the geography of the AI chip trade has shifted, and that the controls have to shift with it.

The rule also reflects a broader hardening of American policy. The Trump administration has made the containment of China's access to advanced technology a central plank of its economic and national security agenda, and the Commerce Department has moved repeatedly to tighten the screws. The interim final rule published on November 16 was announced just days after the extraordinary drama at Nvidia itself, when Huang resigned following reports of an affair with a suspected Chinese spy who had previously worked at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud company that leased computing access to Chinese companies and is being investigated for alleged involvement in smuggling. The Straits Times, reporting the halt, noted the sequence explicitly: the Commerce Department published the new rule days after Huang's resignation. The connection between the two events has not been established by any official statement, but the timing has not gone unnoticed in Washington or in the markets.

A company in turmoil: Huang's resignation and return

The shipment halt cannot be understood without the extraordinary week that preceded it. Jensen Huang, the co-founder who built Nvidia from a graphics card maker into the most important company in the artificial intelligence age, resigned suddenly, and the board then unanimously reinstated him as chief executive on November 23, just days later. The company's market value rose by about one trillion dollars on the news of his return, a measure of how completely investors identify Nvidia with its founder. The resignation followed reports of an affair with a suspected Chinese spy who had previously worked at Megaspeed, the Singapore-based cloud provider. The details of those reports remain contested and unproven, and Huang has not been accused of any crime. But the episode placed the company's Asian business under a spotlight it had never faced before, and it raised questions in Washington about the company's relationships in the region.

President Donald Trump weighed in on November 27, saying he had seen sufficient information that Huang was not a security threat. The intervention was characteristic of a president who has taken a personal interest in the chip industry and in Nvidia in particular, and it appeared designed to draw a line under the affair. But the Commerce Department's new rule had already been published, and the shipment halt reported by the Financial Times suggests that the machinery of export control is now moving on its own logic, regardless of the politics around any individual executive. Nvidia declined to comment on the report, and Dell and Supermicro did not immediately respond to requests for comment. The silence is itself telling: no company in the supply chain wants to be seen to be ahead of the regulator on an issue as sensitive as this one.

The market reaction has been brutal. Nvidia shares fell 8.6 percent on November 25, the same day the Nasdaq plunged 4.15 percent in its worst November session since 2008, as investors sold technology stocks on fears about tariffs, a hot inflation reading and the sustainability of AI spending. The Dow Jones Industrial Average topped 50,000 on the same day, a split that showed investors rotating out of growth and into value. Nvidia had already fallen 5.86 percent in an earlier session in the same week. The stock that had carried the market for two years was suddenly the stock dragging it down, and the shipment halt adds a new and concrete reason for investors to worry: not a rumour about demand, not a debate about valuation, but a physical stop on deliveries to four countries that together represent one of the fastest-growing sources of revenue in the company's accounts.

What the new rule actually requires

The interim final rule from the Bureau of Industry and Security, announced on November 16 and effective on November 19, introduces licensing requirements for exports, re-exports and transfers of qualifying advanced computing hardware to or within Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil. The rule is formally titled "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors", and its scope goes well beyond physical shipments. It covers the remote provision of computing power, which is the heart of the workaround. A data centre in Singapore that rents GPU capacity to a company headquartered in China is now caught by the rule, even though no chip ever crosses a border. The rule requires the operator to obtain a licence, and to obtain it the operator must demonstrate that its end users do not include restricted entities.

The compliance burden is substantial. Data centre operators, chip exporters and other entities in the supply chain must conduct due diligence on their customers, including checking their ownership and control structures. That means tracing who ultimately owns and controls the company renting the computing power, and whether that company is itself owned by, or acts on behalf of, a restricted entity. For cloud providers with thousands of customers, many of them resellers and intermediaries, that is a formidable task. The rule gives them 90 days to obtain a licence, a deadline that runs to the middle of February 2027. Until the licences are in place, the prudent course for Nvidia and its partners is to stop shipping, which is exactly what the Financial Times reported they have done.

There are exemptions, and they matter. Qualifying American-headquartered end users, and companies with American-headquartered ultimate parents, are excluded from the destination-based licensing requirement, subject to reporting obligations. That means an American company operating a data centre in Singapore can continue to receive Nvidia hardware, provided it reports as required. The exemption is designed to protect legitimate American business in the region while cutting off the route to China. But the line between a legitimate American customer and a front for a Chinese one is precisely what the due diligence requirements are meant to police, and the difficulty of drawing that line is the reason the halt is happening now. Nvidia and its partners would rather stop shipping for a while than risk a violation that could bring enforcement action, fines and a far more damaging loss of trust in Washington.

Singapore: the 104 billion dollar question

No country is more central to this story than Singapore. The city-state has become the undisputed hub of the AI chip trade in Asia, and the numbers are extraordinary. Nvidia's Singapore subsidiary reported revenue of 104 billion dollars for 2025, a rise of 72 percent on the previous year. That figure is larger than the gross domestic product of many countries, and it is far larger than Singapore's domestic demand for AI chips could plausibly explain. The discrepancy has fuelled years of speculation about the ultimate destination of the hardware, and about the role of Singapore-based cloud companies in leasing computing power to customers elsewhere in Asia, including China. The Commerce Department's decision to include Singapore in the licensing list is, in effect, an official acknowledgement that the questions are serious enough to require a regulatory answer.

The Singapore government has not commented publicly on the new rule, and the country's data centre operators have said little. But the implications are obvious. Singapore has built its economic strategy around being a trusted, neutral hub for global technology, a place where American, Chinese and European companies can all do business under the rule of law. The new American rule puts that neutrality under strain, because it effectively asks Singapore-based operators to choose between their American suppliers and their Chinese customers. The 90-day licensing window is a grace period, but it is also a deadline, and the companies that cannot demonstrate clean end users will face a choice: lose the Chinese business, or lose the American hardware. For many of them, that is not a choice at all.

Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia face the same dilemma on a smaller scale. All three have attracted heavy investment in data centre capacity in recent years, much of it from Chinese companies and from operators serving Chinese customers. Malaysia in particular has become a favoured location for new data centres, drawn by cheap land, cheap power and a government eager to attract technology investment. The new rule does not ban data centre construction in these countries, and it does not ban the sale of less advanced chips. But it does mean that the most powerful AI hardware, the hardware that makes the difference between a competitive AI model and an also-ran, will now require an American licence before it can be shipped. That is a material change in the economics of the region's data centre boom, and it is likely to slow investment decisions until the licensing picture becomes clear.

What happens next: the 90-day clock

The immediate question is how long the halt lasts. The Financial Times report said it was unclear whether the suspension would be temporary while Nvidia and its partners work through compliance, and that uncertainty is the heart of the story. If the halt is a matter of weeks, while licences are applied for and due diligence is completed, the financial impact will be modest and the episode will be remembered as a compliance blip. If it drags on for months, the impact on Nvidia's revenue, on the region's data centre pipeline and on the broader AI supply chain could be substantial. The 90-day deadline for cloud providers to obtain licences gives a natural horizon: by the middle of February 2027, the industry should know which customers are clean and which are not, and shipments should be able to resume to the clean ones. But 90 days is a long time in a market that measures growth in quarters, and every week of delay is a week in which competitors, including Chinese chipmakers, can make their pitch to the customers Nvidia cannot serve.

The second question is whether other countries will be added to the list. Brazil is already on it, and the Commerce Department has made clear that the list is based on an assessment of diversion risk, which can change. Other countries with large data centre industries and close commercial ties to China will be watching closely. The rule also sets a precedent: for the first time, the United States is regulating not just the export of chips but the remote use of chips, a step that extends American jurisdiction deep into the operations of foreign data centres. That precedent is likely to be tested, both in the courts and in the diplomacy between Washington and the affected capitals, and the outcome will shape the global AI industry for years.

The third question is what this means for the AI boom itself. Nvidia's chips are the bottleneck for the entire industry: every major AI model, from the largest language models to the smallest start-ups, depends on them. A restriction on where those chips can go is, in effect, a restriction on where AI can be built. The American argument is that this is precisely the point: the United States should not be supplying the computing power that could be used against it. The counter-argument, made by the industry and by some economists, is that restrictions push customers towards Chinese alternatives, accelerate the development of a rival chip industry in China, and ultimately weaken the American companies they are meant to protect. That debate has been running for years, and the shipment halt is its latest and most concrete chapter.

Why this matters for everyone, not just chip investors

It is easy to read this story as a niche dispute about export paperwork, of interest only to chip investors and trade lawyers. That would be a mistake. The question of who gets access to the most powerful computing hardware in the world is one of the defining questions of the decade, and the answer will shape the balance of technological power between the United States and China, the pace of AI development, and the economies of the countries caught in between. South-East Asia has spent years positioning itself as the neutral ground where the world's technology companies can meet. The new rule tests that positioning in a way that no previous American measure has, because it does not ask the region to choose a side in a speech or a summit. It asks data centre operators to name their customers, and to prove that those customers are not fronts for a restricted entity. That is a much harder ask, and it is one that will have consequences far beyond the server shipments that have already been frozen.

For ordinary readers, the practical consequences are likely to be felt in the price and availability of AI services, in the pace at which new data centres are built in the region, and in the broader tension between the United States and China that now touches everything from phones to cars to the software that runs the world. The halt is also a reminder of how quickly the rules of global trade can change. A week ago, the story was about a chief executive resigning and being reinstated. Today it is about an entire region losing access to the most sought-after hardware on earth. The next chapter will be written in the 90 days that follow, as licences are applied for, customers are vetted, and the industry discovers just how much of the AI boom in South-East Asia was built on demand that Washington now considers off-limits. The Financial Times report, the Commerce Department rule and the silence from Nvidia all point in the same direction: the era of frictionless chip shipments to Asia is over, and the new era will be slower, more bureaucratic and much more political.
