TITLE: Trump Is Weighing Killing Iran's Top Leaders to End the 9-Month War — Inside the Decapitation Debate Splitting His Administration LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Trump officials are debating strikes on Iran's top leaders to end the nine-month war, NBC reports. No final decision has been made ---BODY---

President Donald Trump during his state visit to Manila, Philippines, on November 18, 2026. His administration is debating whether to target Iran's top leaders in strikes aimed at ending the nine-month war. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

Donald Trump is weighing the most drastic option of the nine-month war with Iran: killing the country's top leaders. According to NBC News, which reported the deliberations on Tuesday, Trump administration officials are internally debating whether the United States military should target senior Iranian leaders in strikes designed to remove the hard-liners who run the regime and bring the war to an end. The option — known in military planning as a decapitation strike — has been on the table since the war began, but it has come under renewed discussion in recent weeks, as the White House searches for a way to conclude a conflict that has now dragged on for nine months, cost the life of Iran's supreme leader, and shows no sign of ending on its own. Trump has made no final decision, NBC reported, citing a United States official and a person familiar with the discussions — but the fact that the debate is happening, and that it is being described by officials on both sides of it, tells its own story about a war that has reached a stalemate, and a president who needs an exit.

The argument for the strikes, as described by their supporters inside the administration, is brutally simple. Kill the hard-liners at the top of the Iranian regime, and the war ends — because the people who are keeping it going will be dead, because the leaders who replace them will know that Washington can find and kill them at any time, and because Trump will be able to declare victory and bring the war to a close. It is, in the words of one account of the debate, a way of demonstrating that the United States can locate and kill Iranian leaders at will, and of giving the president an off-ramp — a decisive, visible act that ends the war on American terms. The supporters of the idea argue that it could pressure the remaining leadership in Tehran into making concessions, and that it could send a warning to whoever comes next: that the United States can eliminate Iranian leaders whenever it chooses, and that defiance has a price measured in lives at the very top.

The argument against the strikes is equally simple, and it is being made, according to NBC, by officials inside the administration and the military. Kill the leaders, the opponents warn, and the people left in charge will not surrender. They will dig in. They will become more hard-line, not less, because the lesson they will draw from the killing of their leaders is not that resistance is futile, but that survival depends on fighting harder. The strikes could damage American credibility in any future negotiations to end the war — because a government that assassinates the other side's leaders is not a government that can be trusted to keep a deal. And they could expose American personnel, and American allies, to heightened retaliation — because a regime that has lost its leaders has nothing left to lose, and every reason to strike back. The debate, in short, is between those who believe that killing the leadership ends the war, and those who believe that it prolongs it.

The option is not new. Targeting senior Iranian figures has long been among the military options presented to Trump, and it was considered — and, in part, carried out — at the very beginning of the war. On the first day of the conflict, February 28, 2026, American and Israeli strikes assassinated Iran's Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, ending his 36-year reign, and killed other senior military and intelligence officials. The war that followed — now in its ninth month — was ignited by those strikes, and it has been shaped by them ever since. The killing of a supreme leader did not end the war. It started it. That fact hangs over the current debate, and it is the strongest argument the opponents of a new round of leadership strikes can make: that the United States has already tried killing Iran's leader, and the result was not peace, but nine months of war.

The war so far — nine months, a dead supreme leader, and a stalemate

The war began on February 28, 2026, when the United States and Israel launched strikes that killed Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, Iran's supreme leader for 36 years, along with other senior military and intelligence officials. The assassination of a head of state — and of a religious leader revered by millions — was an act without modern precedent, and it set the terms of everything that followed. Khamenei was succeeded by his son, Mojtaba Khamenei, who took over as supreme leader — and who, according to NBC, was himself seriously wounded in the American airstrike that killed his father, and has not appeared in public since. The new leadership, headed by Mojtaba, has, by the account of officials familiar with the situation, become even more hard-line than the one it replaced.

Nine months on, the war has settled into a grinding, dangerous stalemate. The United States has imposed a naval blockade on Iranian ports, enforced by American warships in the Gulf, and has tightened economic sanctions to the point where the Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, who leads the sanctions effort, has reportedly moved to a military base in the Washington, D.C., area because of threats to his safety. Iran has fought back with the weapons it has: ballistic missiles, drones, naval mines, and proxy forces across the region. In the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which a fifth of the world's oil passes, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps said this week that two oil tankers had struck naval mines, exploded, and caught fire — the latest in a series of attacks on commercial shipping that has made the strait the most dangerous stretch of water in the world. Yemen's Houthis, Iran's allies, have attacked Saudi Arabia — striking a skyscraper in Riyadh, injuring 11 people, and hitting the Ras Tanura oil refinery — and a Saudi-led coalition has struck back in Yemen. The war has spread, in stages, across the region, and it has not stopped.

The human cost of the war is hard to measure, because neither side publishes reliable figures, but the scale of it is not in doubt. The conflict is centred, as NBC and other reporting has described it, on Tehran's nuclear programme, its ballistic missile capabilities, and its regional military activities — the three issues that the United States has said it will not allow Iran to pursue. The war has also, in the background, become a war about the future of the Iranian regime itself: whether the Islamic Republic, founded in 1979, survives the loss of its supreme leader, or whether the war that killed him will also destroy the system he built. That is the question that lies behind the decapitation debate. The supporters of leadership strikes believe that killing the men at the top will break the regime. The opponents believe that it will entrench it.

The stalemate is also a political problem for Trump. He needs an exit strategy, and he needs one that he can call a victory. The war has dragged on for nine months, far longer than the quick, decisive campaign that was promised at its start, and it has consumed American attention, American resources, and American credibility. The president has indicated, according to the NBC report, that he may wait until after the new year to take major action, while the Pentagon has expanded the range of military options before him. The delay is itself a sign of the difficulty: there is no easy way to end this war, and every option on the table carries a cost. The decapitation option is the most dramatic of them, and the most divisive — and it is the one that is now being debated in earnest.

The other options — Kharg Island, Qeshm Island, and Pickaxe Mountain

Leadership strikes are not the only option before the president. The Pentagon has, according to NBC, expanded the range of military options available to him, and the alternatives are themselves formidable. One is to resume large-scale combat operations — a return to the kind of intensive bombing campaign that opened the war, aimed at degrading Iran's military to the point where it can no longer fight. Another is to strike Iran's oil infrastructure: the oil facilities on Kharg Island, the terminal through which most of Iran's oil exports flow, and the energy infrastructure on Qeshm Island, in the Strait of Hormuz. A third is to hit the nuclear enrichment sites at Pickaxe Mountain, the hardened, deeply buried facilities where Iran's nuclear programme is centred, and which have been the focus of American and Israeli planning for years.

Each of these options has its own advocates and its own risks. Striking Kharg Island would cripple Iran's oil revenues — and would also send oil prices soaring, disrupt global energy markets, and invite retaliation against the oil infrastructure of America's allies in the Gulf. Striking Qeshm Island, in the strait itself, would be a direct challenge to Iran's control of the waterway, and could escalate into a naval war in the world's most important oil chokepoint. Striking Pickaxe Mountain would be an attempt to destroy Iran's nuclear programme by force — a goal the United States has pursued for two decades — but the facilities are buried deep underground, and there is no guarantee that even the most powerful American bombs could destroy them. And resuming large-scale combat would mean a return to a war that the United States has already been fighting for nine months, with no end in sight.

The decapitation option stands apart from these because it is not aimed at Iran's capabilities, but at its people — at the men who make the decisions. It is a political strike, not a military one, and its logic is political: that the war is being kept alive by a small group of hard-liners, and that removing them removes the obstacle to peace. The problem, as the opponents point out, is that the men who would replace them are likely to be the same kind of men — or harder. Mojtaba Khamenei, the current supreme leader, is the son of the man the United States killed, and his leadership is, by all accounts, more hard-line than his father's. If the United States kills him, too, the next leader will be chosen by a system that has now lost two supreme leaders to American strikes, and that will have learned, above all, that survival requires defiance. The logic of decapitation assumes that the regime is a machine that stops when its leaders are removed. The history of the last nine months suggests that it is a system that replaces its leaders and fights on.

Iran's answer — seven conditions, and no talks

While Washington debates, Tehran has made its own position clear — and it is not a position that suggests the war is about to end. Iran's parliament speaker and chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf, said on Tuesday that Tehran will not hold talks with the United States until seven conditions are fulfilled, according to Iranian state television. The Iranian authorities have not publicly listed all seven conditions, but Iranian state media have said they include the lifting of sanctions, an end to the American naval blockade of Iranian ports, and the release of Iranian assets frozen abroad. The message is unmistakable: Iran will not negotiate while it is under blockade and under sanctions, and it will not negotiate from a position of weakness. The war, from Tehran's point of view, is a war of survival, and survival is not negotiable.

The seven conditions are also a measure of how far apart the two sides are. The United States has demanded that Iran abandon its nuclear programme, its ballistic missile programme, and its support for proxy forces across the region — the three issues at the heart of the war. Iran has demanded that the United States lift its sanctions, end its blockade, and release its assets — the three instruments by which the United States is waging the war. Neither side is offering the other what it wants, and neither side is willing to concede. The result is a stalemate in which both sides are waiting for the other to break — and in which the debate in Washington about leadership strikes is, in part, a debate about how to break the stalemate by force.

There is a further complication, and it is one that has been reported in recent days alongside the decapitation debate. According to reports cited in the American press, Iran could be developing a radioactive "dirty bomb" — a weapon that spreads radioactive material without a nuclear explosion — and American intelligence has assessed potential Iranian plots involving chemical weapons aimed at senior officials in Washington. The reports are unverified, and they are the kind of claims that circulate in wartime, but they have added to the sense of urgency in Washington, and to the argument — made by the supporters of decisive action — that the war cannot be allowed to drag on, because the longer it lasts, the more dangerous it becomes. The opponents of escalation make the opposite argument: that the longer the war lasts, the more reason there is to find a way out of it, rather than to deepen it.

The men at the top — and the question of who is a target

NBC News did not identify any specific officials as targets of the operations under discussion, and it is important to be clear about that. The debate, as reported, is about whether to target senior Iranian leaders in general, not about a list of named individuals. But the reporting does describe the leadership that has survived the war so far, and the men who run the regime today. At the top is the Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, who succeeded his assassinated father and who, according to NBC, has not been seen in public since suffering serious injuries in the strike that killed his father. Below him are the senior figures in Iran's military and national security apparatus: the commander of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, Ahmad Vahidi; the armed forces chief of staff, Ali Abdollahi; the army chief, Amir Hatami; and Mohsen Rezaei, a former commander in chief of the Revolutionary Guard who was reportedly appointed secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council in August 2026.

These are the men who have kept the regime functioning through nine months of war, after the loss of its supreme leader and many of its senior commanders. They are, by any measure, hard-liners — men who have spent their careers in the Revolutionary Guard and the military, and who have built their authority on defiance of the United States. The supporters of decapitation strikes argue that removing them would break the regime's will to fight. The opponents argue that it would prove, to the Iranian people and to the world, that the United States is waging a war not against a government, but against a nation — and that it would unite Iranians behind the regime, rather than turning them against it. The history of leadership strikes, in other wars, offers little comfort to either side. Sometimes they work. Sometimes they make things worse. And sometimes they change nothing at all, except the names of the dead.

There is also a legal and moral question, and it is one that has been raised, quietly, by officials who oppose the strikes. The targeted killing of a head of state, or of senior government officials, is an act that sits at the edge of international law, and the assassination of Ayatollah Ali Khamenei at the start of the war was already a step that many governments and legal scholars regarded as a grave breach. To repeat it — to make the killing of leaders a routine instrument of American policy — would set a precedent that the United States itself might one day regret. A president who orders the killing of another country's leaders is declaring that leaders are legitimate targets, and that declaration cuts both ways. The opponents of the strikes have warned, according to NBC, that targeting Iranian leaders could expose American officials to retaliatory attacks — and that warning is not abstract. The Treasury Secretary has already moved to a military base because of threats to his safety. The war has already come home, in the form of threats against the men who run it.

Trump's decision — and the wait until the new year

For all the debate, the decision is Trump's, and he has not made it. NBC reported that he has made no final decision on next steps in the war, and that he has been considering a range of options. He indicated last week that he may wait until after the new year to take major action — a delay that suggests he is aware of the stakes, and of the difficulty of choosing between options that all carry risks. The White House, responding to the NBC report, did not confirm or deny the debate. A spokesperson said: "President Trump has many options available to him, and he will make the best possible decision to ensure that Iran can never have a nuclear weapon." It was a statement that said everything and nothing — a reminder that the president's options are open, and that the decision, when it comes, will be his alone.

Trump himself has spoken about Iran in terms that suggest he believes he knows more than his advisers, and that he is confident of his ability to read the situation. "We know a lot about what's happening in Iran. We know everything they say. We know who they are, where they are. We'll find out about it. We will not allow that," he told reporters at the White House on November 20, in remarks that were widely read as a reference to the reports of Iranian weapons plots. The confidence is characteristic. The question is whether it is justified — and whether a president who believes he knows where Iran's leaders are, and who they are, will decide to act on that knowledge, or to hold it in reserve.

The Pentagon, for its part, did not respond to a request for comment from NBC, which is itself a sign of the sensitivity of the debate. The military does not discuss targeting decisions in public, and it does not confirm or deny the existence of operational plans. But the fact that the debate has been described in such detail, by officials on both sides, suggests that it is a live and serious one — and that the decision, when it comes, will be one of the most consequential of the war. The president has, in the past, made decisions of this kind quickly, and with little warning. He has also, in the past, delayed them, and chosen a different path. The indication that he may wait until after the new year is, for now, the only guide to his thinking — and it is a thin one.

The stakes — escalation, retaliation, and the credibility of peace

The stakes of the decision are hard to overstate. If Trump orders strikes on Iran's top leaders, and they succeed, the war may end — or it may enter a new and more dangerous phase, in which a wounded regime, stripped of its leadership, lashes out at American forces, American allies, and the shipping of the Gulf. If the strikes fail, or if they kill the wrong people, or if they kill leaders and the regime fights on anyway, the United States will have committed an act that the world will condemn, and that will make a negotiated end to the war harder, not easier. And if Trump decides not to order the strikes, the war will go on — the blockade, the sanctions, the missiles, the mines in the strait, the attacks on Saudi Arabia, the slow grind of a conflict that has already lasted nine months and shows no sign of ending.

The opponents of the strikes have made a specific argument about credibility, and it is worth taking seriously. A war ends, in the end, with a negotiation — and a negotiation requires that both sides believe the other will keep its word. A government that assassinates the other side's leaders, in the middle of a war, is a government that is telling the other side that its leaders are not safe, that no one is safe, and that no deal can protect them. That is not a message that encourages negotiation. It is a message that encourages the other side to fight to the end, because the end is the only outcome that is safe. The supporters of the strikes argue the opposite: that the only message Iran understands is force, and that the only way to bring the regime to the table is to show it that its leaders can be killed at any time. Both arguments have been made before, in other wars, by other governments. Neither has ever been proven right in advance.

There is also the question of what "victory" means, in a war like this one. Trump needs an exit strategy, and he needs one he can call a victory. The killing of Iran's leaders would be a dramatic, visible act — the kind of act that can be announced, celebrated, and presented as a triumph. But the war that began with the killing of a supreme leader has lasted nine months, and the lesson of those nine months is that killing a leader does not kill a war. The regime survived the death of Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. It replaced him with his son. It has survived the blockade, the sanctions, and the bombing. It has fought back, in the strait, in Saudi Arabia, and through its proxies. A second round of leadership killings might break it. It might also confirm it in its belief that the war is a war of survival, and that survival requires fighting to the last man.

What happens next

For now, the debate goes on, behind closed doors, in Washington — and the war goes on, in the Gulf, in the strait, and in the skies over Iran and Saudi Arabia. The president has said he may wait until after the new year to take major action, which means that the decision is, for the moment, deferred. But the options are being prepared, the plans are being drawn up, and the men who would carry them out are waiting for the order. The Pentagon has expanded the range of options before the president. The Treasury has tightened the sanctions. The Navy has tightened the blockade. And in Tehran, the leadership that survived the first round of assassinations is waiting, too — waiting to see whether the United States will try again, and preparing, in the meantime, for the possibility that it will.

The war is nine months old. It began with the assassination of a supreme leader, and it may yet end with the assassination of another. Or it may end with a negotiation, or with a stalemate that hardens into a frozen conflict, or with an escalation that no one planned and no one can control. The debate inside the Trump administration — between those who believe that killing Iran's leaders will end the war, and those who believe that it will prolong it — is a debate about which of those futures is most likely, and about what kind of war the United States is fighting, and what kind of peace it wants. It is a debate that will be decided by one man, in the coming weeks, and the decision he makes will shape the Middle East for years to come. For now, all that is certain is that the option is on the table, that the president has not yet decided, and that the nine-month war — the war that was supposed to be short, and decisive, and over — is still going on, and still waiting for an end.
