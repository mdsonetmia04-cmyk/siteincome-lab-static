TITLE: Trump Just Hit 14 Countries With New Tariff Proposals — China Faces 20% on Top of 25% After an 8-Month Probe LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The US proposed new tariffs on 14 trading partners after an 8-month excess-capacity probe. China faces the highest rates — 20% on top of 25%. ---BODY---

President Donald J. Trump. His administration on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, proposed new tariffs on 14 major trading partners after an eight-month investigation into excess industrial capacity. Photo: Official White House portrait, Wikimedia Commons.

The Trump administration has just fired the biggest trade shot of the year. On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Office of the United States Trade Representative proposed new tariffs on 14 major trading partners, capping an eight-month investigation into what Washington calls structural excess industrial capacity — factories abroad producing far more steel, aluminium, electric vehicles, appliances and solar panels than the world can buy, with the surplus flooding global markets at prices American producers say they cannot match. China, which the administration blames most for the glut, faces the highest rates of all: a proposed 20 per cent excess-capacity tariff that would sit on top of the roughly 25 per cent in duties imposed during President Donald Trump's first term in office. The announcement, published in a Federal Register notice on Wednesday, opens a public comment period and a hearing before the duties can take effect — a process officials say could push implementation into January. But the direction of travel is now unmistakable. After the Supreme Court struck down the sweeping reciprocal tariffs of last year, the administration has spent nine months rebuilding its tariff wall brick by brick, using older and more durable trade laws. Wednesday's move is the biggest brick yet.

The scale of the announcement is hard to overstate. The 14 trading partners named in the proposal read like a map of the global supply chain: China, Bangladesh, Cambodia, the European Union, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam. Together they account for an enormous share of everything Americans buy — from the phone in your pocket to the washing machine in your kitchen, from the car in your driveway to the solar panels on your roof. Two countries that were investigated were spared, at least for now: the USTR said it did not propose tariffs on goods from Mexico and Norway, and is still reviewing the excess-capacity probes into both. The specific tariff rates for each country were to be published in the Federal Register notice on Wednesday, and a senior administration official said the duties would be tailored country by country rather than applied at a single uniform rate.

For China, the numbers are stark. The proposed 20 per cent excess-capacity tariff would come on top of the roughly 25 per cent in tariffs already imposed during Mr Trump's first term — and it would keep China above the 20 per cent rate proposed for Vietnam, the next-highest target. In other words, a Chinese-made product that once entered the United States duty-free could soon face combined duties approaching half its value. The administration's argument is that China is not merely a competitor but the engine of the global glut: state-backed factories churning out steel, aluminium, electric vehicles, appliances and solar products in volumes no market could absorb, then exporting the surplus at prices that undercut producers everywhere else. The investigation also focused on a newer wrinkle — Chinese companies setting up factories across South-East Asia, in Vietnam, Indonesia and Cambodia, to route goods around American tariffs. Wednesday's proposal answers that strategy directly, by naming those countries too.

Eight months of investigation, one long legal rebuild

To understand why this announcement matters, you have to understand what happened in February. That month, the Supreme Court ruled that the sweeping reciprocal tariff measures introduced last year were unlawful — a decision that knocked out the centrepiece of the administration's trade policy in a single stroke. Within weeks, in March, the administration launched two investigations simultaneously: one into weak enforcement of anti-forced-labour laws in 59 countries and the European Union, and one — the probe concluded on Wednesday — into excess industrial capacity in 16 major trading partners. The excess-capacity investigation was opened formally on March 11, 2026, under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, a law that gives the president broad authority to respond to unfair foreign trade practices. It is a slower, more deliberate tool than the emergency powers behind the reciprocal tariffs, and that is precisely the point: duties imposed under Section 301 rest on a formal investigation, a published report and a public comment process, making them far harder for a court to strike down.

The United States Trade Representative, Jamieson Greer, was expected to release the findings of the investigation as early as Tuesday night or sometime this week, and the announcement landed on Wednesday morning. The probe's definition of the problem is precise. The USTR defines structural excess capacity as production capacity that exceeds domestic and global demand, often sustained by government interventions or policies — subsidies, cheap state-directed credit, protected domestic markets and other supports that let factories keep running and keep exporting even when the world does not need what they make. The finding, in the administration's telling, is that this is not a natural market outcome but a policy choice by foreign governments, and that American workers and manufacturers are paying the price for it.

The new duties will not arrive alone. They will stack on top of tariffs already imposed during Mr Trump's second term, and on top of the 10 per cent to 12.5 per cent duties imposed in July on 60 countries over their failure to adequately prevent forced labour. That stacking is deliberate: the administration is layering one legal authority on another, so that even if one layer is challenged, the others stand. Ryan Majerus, a former senior Commerce Department trade official who is now a partner with the law firm King & Spalding, said he expects a lengthy report with differentiated tariff remedies for each of the 16 trading partners, and that the administration could propose rates of 15 per cent to 25 per cent on imports from countries deemed to have excess capacity in various sectors. The final rates, published in Wednesday's Federal Register notice, will show how closely that prediction holds.

The 14 countries — and the two that escaped

The full list of countries facing proposed duties is: China, Bangladesh, Cambodia, the European Union, India, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam. The list is notable as much for who is on it as for who is not. Japan, the European Union and South Korea are among America's closest allies, and all three were investigated. But the White House is expected to honour the tariff caps written into the trade deals it signed with those partners last year: 15 per cent on goods from the European Union, Japan and South Korea. That means the additional burden on those three is likely to be limited to between 2.5 and 5 percentage points, on top of the duties already in place. Malaysia, which has a negotiated cap of 19 per cent, is in a similar position. Vietnam, Cambodia, India and Malaysia, whose negotiated rates were higher, are expected to face steeper tariffs — and Vietnam, at a proposed rate just below China's, is the second-hardest hit of all.

Mexico and Norway were the two investigated countries that escaped proposed duties on Wednesday. A senior administration official said the USTR was still reviewing the excess-capacity probes into both countries and declined to give a timeline for their completion. Mexico's reprieve is particularly significant: it is America's largest trading partner, and any tariff on Mexican goods would have rippled through the deeply integrated North American supply chain — the car parts that cross the border several times before a finished vehicle rolls off a line in Michigan, the fresh produce that fills American supermarkets in winter. Norway, a smaller trading partner, was also left out for now. Whether either country is eventually named will depend on the outcome of the continuing reviews.

The inclusion of Bangladesh is a reminder of how wide the net is. Bangladesh is one of the world's largest garment exporters, and American retailers buy billions of dollars' worth of clothing from its factories every year. Cambodia, too, is a major clothing and footwear exporter, and has become a favoured destination for Chinese manufacturers relocating to avoid American tariffs. Singapore and Switzerland, by contrast, are not low-cost manufacturing hubs — they are financial and trading centres, and their inclusion shows the administration is looking at the whole architecture of global trade, not just factory floors. Taiwan is the world's most important source of advanced semiconductors, and its inclusion raises questions about how the duties will treat the chips that power everything from smartphones to cars. The Federal Register notice, with its country-by-country rates and product lists, will answer many of those questions — and raise many more.

What this means for prices — and for your wallet

The question every American household will ask is the simplest one: will this make things more expensive? The honest answer is that it depends on what you buy, where it comes from, and how companies respond. Tariffs are taxes on imports, paid by the American company that brings the goods into the country — and economists broadly agree that much of the cost is passed on to consumers in the form of higher prices, absorbed in thinner profit margins, or some combination of the two. The administration's supporters argue that the duties will protect American jobs and factories, and that the price effects will be modest and worth paying. Its critics argue that tariffs are a tax on American consumers, and that the biggest burden falls on the households that can least afford it.

Consider the sectors named in the probe. Steel and aluminium go into cars, appliances, construction and packaging — and higher input costs for American manufacturers can mean higher prices for American buyers. Electric vehicles are a particular flashpoint: Chinese-made EVs are already effectively shut out of the American market by existing duties, but the new tariffs would raise the cost of the batteries, components and materials that American EV makers buy from China and South-East Asia. Appliances — washing machines, refrigerators, air conditioners — are heavily imported from China, Vietnam, Thailand and Malaysia, and are exactly the kind of big-ticket purchase where a tariff shows up quickly on a price tag. Solar panels, which the administration says are a prime example of Chinese overcapacity, are central to the American energy transition, and higher panel prices could slow the solar build-out that utilities and homeowners have planned.

The timing adds another layer. The announcement comes the day before Thanksgiving, at the start of the busiest shopping season of the year, and days after official figures showed American consumer confidence jumping to its highest level in nearly two years. Retailers have already stocked their shelves for the holidays, so the immediate impact on Christmas prices is likely to be limited. But the duties, if implemented in January as officials suggest, would land just as retailers begin ordering for the spring — and just as American households begin the new year. The public comment period and hearing give businesses, importers and consumers a chance to argue their case, and the administration will hear from all of them. In previous tariff rounds, that process produced exemptions and carve-outs for products with no American-made alternative. Expect a similar scramble this time.

Allies, rivals and the politics of a midterm defeat

The announcement also has a political dimension that is impossible to ignore. It comes after a crushing defeat for the administration's party in the midterm elections, in which Democrats won a historic House majority of at least 259 seats — the largest since 1990, as reported earlier this week. When the new Congress is seated in January, Democrats will control the House, and with it the power to investigate, to hold hearings and to block legislation. The administration's turn to tariffs now looks, to many observers, like an effort to lock in its trade legacy before the opposition takes the gavel. One analysis noted that the move appears designed to rebuild the tariff barriers dismantled by the courts and to overcome challenges at home and abroad — and that although the step may look politically risky, the administration chose to find a workaround and restore the barriers before January.

The reaction from trading partners is likely to be swift and sharply divided. The European Union, Japan and South Korea — all capped at 15 per cent under existing deals — have the most to lose from any breach of those caps, and the most reason to insist the caps are honoured. China, the principal target, has repeatedly said it will respond to new American tariffs with countermeasures of its own, and previous rounds of the trade war saw Beijing hit American soybeans, pork, cars and aircraft with retaliatory duties. Vietnam, Cambodia and the South-East Asian countries named in the proposal face a different dilemma: they have built their economies on export manufacturing, much of it by Chinese-owned factories, and they must now decide whether to tighten rules on the origin of goods, to negotiate with Washington, or to retaliate. India, the world's most populous country and a growing manufacturing power, has been negotiating a trade deal with Washington for months, and the new proposal could complicate those talks — or become leverage inside them.

There is also the question of what happens at the World Trade Organization. Section 301 tariffs have been challenged there before, and the United States has a long history of losing such cases and ignoring the rulings. The administration's calculation appears to be that the domestic legal durability of a Section 301 action matters more than its standing in Geneva — and that the political value of acting outweighs the diplomatic cost of another trade dispute. Whether that calculation holds will become clear in the weeks ahead, as capitals from Brussels to Beijing to Hanoi respond.

What happens next: the calendar to watch

The process from here is formal and public, and it gives everyone a chance to be heard. The Federal Register notice published on Wednesday sets out the proposed rates and the products covered. A public notice and comment period follows, during which businesses, trade groups, foreign governments and ordinary citizens can submit their views. A hearing will be held before the duties go into effect. A senior administration official said that process could push implementation into January — which means the duties will not bite before Christmas, but could be in force by the time the new Congress is seated and the new year begins.

Several dates and decisions will shape the outcome. First, the Federal Register notice itself: the country-by-country rates and the product lists will determine which industries are hit hardest and which escape lightly. Second, the comment period: in previous tariff rounds, importers and manufacturers won exemptions for products with no domestic source, and the volume and quality of those submissions will matter. Third, the hearing: it will be the first public test of the administration's evidence, and the first chance for critics to challenge the finding of excess capacity. Fourth, the continuing reviews of Mexico and Norway: if either country is eventually named, the map of the tariff wall will change again. And fifth, the response of trading partners: retaliation, negotiation or a challenge at the World Trade Organization would each push the story in a different direction.

For American businesses, the practical advice is already clear. Importers should read the Federal Register notice the moment it is published, map their supply chains against the country list, and consider whether to accelerate shipments before the duties take effect. Manufacturers should assess whether the tariffs will raise their input costs or protect them from foreign competition — and, in many cases, both at once. Retailers should think about the spring ordering season, and about whether to pass higher costs to shoppers or absorb them. And consumers should understand that the price of the goods they buy is about to become a little more political — and a little less predictable — than it was yesterday.

Why this tariff fight is different from the last one

It is tempting to see Wednesday's announcement as simply another round in a trade war that has been running, on and off, for almost a decade. But there are important differences. The first round, in 2018 and 2019, was built on emergency powers and imposed quickly, with little process. The second round, last year, relied on the same emergency powers — and was struck down by the Supreme Court in February. This round is built on Section 301, a law that Congress wrote in 1974 precisely for this kind of dispute, and that requires the administration to investigate, to publish its findings, to hear from the public and then to act. That process is slower, but it is also far more durable. If the courts uphold the Section 301 duties, they will be much harder to remove than the reciprocal tariffs were — and the next administration, whatever its politics, will inherit a tariff wall that is built on statute rather than on executive whim.

The second difference is the scope. The probe covered 16 of America's largest trading partners — not one country, not one sector, but the whole architecture of global manufacturing. The finding of structural excess capacity is not about a single dumped product or a single subsidised factory; it is a judgement about how the world economy is organised, and about whether governments that support their manufacturers with subsidies and protected markets are playing fair. That is a much bigger argument than a dispute over the price of steel, and it is an argument that will not be settled by a single tariff notice. It is, in effect, the administration's answer to the deepest question in trade policy: what do you do when the world's factory floor is built with government money?

The third difference is the context. The announcement comes after a midterm defeat, before a change of control in the House, and at the start of a holiday season in which Americans will spend more than at any other time of the year. It comes as consumer confidence is at a near two-year high, as the economy is growing, and as the administration is looking for a way to define its economic legacy. Tariffs have always been as much about politics as about economics, and this round is no exception. The question is whether the politics will hold — whether the public will accept higher prices in exchange for the promise of protected jobs, and whether the trading partners named on Wednesday will negotiate, retaliate or simply wait for the next election.

The bottom line

Wednesday's announcement is the most consequential trade decision of the year, and one of the most consequential of the administration. Fourteen countries, 20 per cent on China, an eight-month investigation, a public comment period and a January implementation date: those are the facts. What they add up to is a deliberate, legally fortified attempt to rebuild the tariff wall that the Supreme Court knocked down in February — and to do it in a way that will survive the courts, the next Congress and the next election. Whether it works as trade policy will depend on the rates in the Federal Register notice, the exemptions won in the comment period, and the response of the countries named. Whether it works as politics will depend on whether American voters, who will pay the tariffs at the till, believe the trade-off is worth it. The comment period is now open, the hearing is coming, and the world is watching. The next move belongs to the 14 countries on the list — and to the American shoppers who will feel the result, one price tag at a time.
