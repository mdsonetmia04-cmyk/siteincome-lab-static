TITLE: North Korea Just Kicked Switzerland Out — Its Top Diplomat Has 48 Hours to Leave Pyongyang LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: North Korea ordered Switzerland's Pyongyang office shut and its envoy out in 48 hours after Bern sanctioned a children's camp over Ukrainian children ---BODY---

Kim Yo Jong, the powerful sister of North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. On Tuesday, November 24, 2026, she threatened to shut Switzerland's diplomatic office in Pyongyang — and on Wednesday, North Korea did exactly that. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0).

North Korea has ordered Switzerland to close its diplomatic office in Pyongyang and told the Swiss diplomat who runs it to leave the country within 48 hours, in a dispute over sanctions that ended one of the last Western diplomatic footholds in the world's most isolated capital. The order, delivered on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, came a single day after Kim Yo Jong — the powerful sister of North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, and one of the country's most senior foreign policy officials — threatened to shut the office down. The cause of the row is a children's camp. Switzerland added North Korea's Songdowon International Children's Camp, in the eastern port city of Wonsan, to its sanctions list last week, over Western allegations that the camp was involved in the transfer of children from Russian-occupied areas of Ukraine. Pyongyang called the sanctions an "open hostile act", declared the head of the Swiss office persona non grata, and gave him two days to get out. The Swiss office had been open for barely a month. It had closed in 2020, during the pandemic, and only reopened on October 19. Its closure, after less than five weeks, is a measure of how quickly a diplomatic opening can collapse when it collides with the war in Ukraine.

The sequence of events is short, sharp and unusually public, even by North Korean standards. On Tuesday, Kim Yo Jong issued a statement, carried by the state-run Korean Central News Agency, condemning Switzerland for adding the camp to its sanctions list and saying that North Korea would consider shutting the Swiss diplomatic office. On Wednesday, the North's Foreign Ministry acted on the threat. Pak Hyon-su, the director general of the ministry's European department, summoned Astier Sylvain, the head of the Swiss diplomatic office, and handed him a memorandum from the Foreign Ministry urging the closure of the office. The ministry, according to the KCNA report, "declared head of the Swiss Diplomatic Office in the DPRK persona non grata and urged him to leave the country within 48 hours", using the acronym for the country's official name, the Democratic People's Republic of Korea. The Swiss government said it was officially informed earlier on Wednesday of the decision, and its Federal Department of Foreign Affairs said it regretted the move and was arranging for the head of the office to return to the Swiss Embassy in Beijing. The 48-hour deadline runs out on Friday, November 27.

The speed of the escalation is the story. Switzerland's office in Pyongyang had been closed for six years, and its reopening in October was presented, on both sides, as a careful, deliberate step: a sign that North Korea was willing to let a Western country back into the capital, and that Switzerland — which has long prided itself on a "universalist foreign policy" that keeps it talking to everyone — was willing to return. The Swiss Foreign Minister, Ignazio Cassis, formally reopened the office in late October, and on October 30 he held talks in Pyongyang with the North's Foreign Minister, Choe Son-hui. A photograph released by the KCNA showed the two ministers sitting across from each other, Cassis fourth from the left and Choe fifth from the right, in a room that had not hosted a Western foreign minister for years. Less than four weeks later, the office is shut, its head is persona non grata, and the minister who reopened it is arranging his envoy's evacuation to Beijing. The photograph, taken on October 31, is now a record of a diplomatic opening that lasted 26 days.

The children's camp at the centre of the row

The Songdowon International Children's Camp, in Wonsan, on North Korea's east coast, is not a name that most people outside Korea will have heard before this week, and it is an unlikely cause for a diplomatic rupture. It is a summer camp for children, built on the coast near Wonsan, and it has for decades hosted children from North Korea and from other countries, including, in the past, children from Russia. The allegation against it is that it has been involved in the transfer of children from Russian-occupied areas of Ukraine — that Ukrainian children have been taken to the camp, as part of the wider programme of deportations that Western governments and the International Criminal Court have accused Russia of carrying out since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Switzerland added the camp to the list of its sanctions against Russia last week, which means that the camp's assets are frozen, that Swiss persons and companies are forbidden from dealing with it, and that the camp is, in the eyes of Swiss law, an entity involved in the forced deportation of Ukrainian children.

North Korea's response to that designation was fury, and the fury was expressed, as it usually is, through Kim Yo Jong. In her statement on Tuesday, she described the Swiss sanctions as an "open hostile act" and an infringement of the North's sovereignty, and she argued that the sanctions denied what was North Korea's obligation to provide children with opportunities for culture and recreation. The argument, in substance, is that the camp is a place where children go on holiday, and that sanctioning it is an attack on North Korean children, not on the Russian war effort. The Western argument, in substance, is that the camp is a destination in a network that moves Ukrainian children out of occupied territory and into the hands of Russia and its allies, and that the children who are taken there are not on holiday. The two arguments do not meet, and they were never going to. The camp is, for Pyongyang, a matter of sovereignty; for Bern, it is a matter of law; and for the Ukrainian children at the centre of the allegation, it is a matter of whether they will ever go home.

The allegation about Ukrainian children is part of a much larger and better-documented story. Since 2022, Ukraine, the United States and the European Union have accused Russia of deporting tens of thousands of Ukrainian children from occupied territory to Russia and to Russian-controlled areas, and in March 2023 the International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for President Vladimir Putin over the alleged unlawful deportation of children. Russia denies that the children were deported, saying that they were evacuated for their own safety, and North Korea has consistently supported Russia's position, as part of the alliance between the two countries that has deepened since the invasion. The Songdowon camp's appearance on a sanctions list is, in that context, a small detail in a large story — but it is a detail that Pyongyang chose to treat as a provocation, and to answer with the expulsion of a diplomat. The camp is, in effect, the point at which the war in Ukraine, the sanctions regime against Russia, and North Korea's diplomacy with the West all intersect, and the intersection proved explosive.

Switzerland's position, as set out by its Foreign Ministry on Tuesday, is that its sanctions policy is independent of the reopening of the Pyongyang office, and that the office was reopened "in line with our universalist foreign policy" — the principle that Switzerland maintains relations with all countries, whatever their government, and that it does not let its sanctions policy dictate its diplomatic presence. The ministry defended the sanctions on the camp on the same day that Kim Yo Jong condemned them, and it did so before the expulsion was announced. The sequence suggests that Bern knew the risk it was taking, and took it anyway: that it regarded the sanctions as a matter of principle, and the office as a matter of presence, and that it did not expect the one to destroy the other. It was wrong. The North Koreans, for their part, made no secret of the connection. The KCNA report of the expulsion said explicitly that the move was in response to Switzerland's decision to impose sanctions on the camp, and it described that decision as the cause of the closure. There was no pretence that the two were unrelated, and no attempt to find a face-saving formula. The office was shut because of the sanctions, and the sanctions were the reason given, in public, by the ministry that ordered it shut.

A school in Switzerland, and a sister who runs foreign policy

There is an irony in this dispute that has not been lost on anyone who follows North Korea, and it is that the two people at the centre of it both went to school in Switzerland. Kim Jong Un and Kim Yo Jong both reportedly attended school in Switzerland as teenagers in the 1990s, when their father, Kim Jong Il, was still in power, and Kim Jong Un's years at a school in the Swiss town of Liebefeld are one of the best-known facts about a leader whose early life is otherwise a mystery. The Swiss connection was, for years, one of the few personal links between the North Korean leadership and the West, and it was sometimes cited, by optimists, as a reason to believe that Kim Jong Un might be more open to the outside world than his father had been. That optimism has not survived the last decade, and the expulsion of the Swiss envoy is, in a sense, the final answer to it: the man who went to school in Switzerland has just thrown Switzerland's diplomat out of his country, and the sister who went to school there with him is the one who threatened to do it.

Kim Yo Jong's role in this episode is worth pausing on, because it is a measure of her power. She is not the foreign minister, and she is not a member of the government in any formal sense, but she is, by general agreement among analysts, one of the most powerful people in North Korea, and one of the most important figures in its foreign policy. She has, over the last few years, become the regime's principal voice on relations with South Korea and the United States, issuing statements that are read, in Seoul and in Washington, as the authoritative expression of Pyongyang's position. Her statement on Tuesday was, in that sense, not a threat but a warning, and the Foreign Ministry's action on Wednesday was the fulfilment of it. The sequence — Kim Yo Jong speaks, the ministry acts — is the pattern of North Korean foreign policy in the Kim Jong Un era, and it is a pattern that the rest of the world has learned to read. When she says that North Korea will consider shutting an office, the office is, in practice, already shut.

The Swiss diplomat at the centre of the story, Astier Sylvain, is a less familiar figure, and his expulsion is, for him, a personal as well as a professional blow. He had been in Pyongyang for barely a month, running an office that had been closed for six years, and he was, in effect, rebuilding Switzerland's presence in the country from scratch. The persona non grata declaration means that he is no longer welcome in North Korea, and that he must leave within 48 hours, and the Swiss ministry's statement that it is arranging for him to return to the Embassy in Beijing suggests that he will be leaving by the fastest available route. The 48-hour deadline is short, even by the standards of diplomatic expulsions, and it is a measure of how little interest Pyongyang had in giving Bern time to negotiate, or to find a compromise. The office was shut, the envoy was expelled, and the deadline was two days. There was no discussion, and no appeal.

What Switzerland loses — and what North Korea gains

The closure of the Swiss office matters, not because Switzerland is a major power in North Korea, but because it is one of the very few Western countries that has any presence in Pyongyang at all. North Korea expelled or closed the missions of most Western countries during the pandemic, and only a handful have returned. The Swedish Embassy, which has represented American interests in Pyongyang since the 1990s, remains open, and a small number of other European missions have a presence, but the Western diplomatic community in the capital is tiny, and every departure makes it smaller. Switzerland's office was, in that context, a significant addition: a sign that the West was coming back, and that North Korea was willing to let it. Its closure, after 26 days, is a sign of the opposite: that North Korea is willing to sacrifice a diplomatic opening for the sake of a point of principle, and that the war in Ukraine has made even the smallest Western presence in Pyongyang conditional on the politics of a conflict that is fought thousands of miles away.

For Switzerland, the loss is practical as well as symbolic. The office in Pyongyang was the base from which Swiss diplomats and aid workers operated in the country, and its closure means that Switzerland's ability to monitor conditions in North Korea, to deliver humanitarian assistance, and to maintain contact with the North Korean government is sharply reduced. Switzerland has long been one of the most active Western donors of humanitarian aid to North Korea, and its development cooperation office in Pyongyang was, for years, the channel through which that aid was delivered. The office's closure does not necessarily end Swiss aid to North Korea, but it makes it harder to deliver, and it removes the people on the ground who know how to deliver it. The Swiss ministry's statement that it "regrets this decision" is, in diplomatic language, a strong expression of displeasure, and it is a measure of how much Bern valued the office that it reopened only a month ago.

For North Korea, the gain is harder to see, and it may not be a gain at all. The expulsion of a Swiss diplomat does not help the children at the Songdowon camp, and it does not remove the sanctions that were imposed on the camp. The sanctions remain in force, and the camp remains on the Swiss sanctions list, and the expulsion has not changed that. What the expulsion does, in Pyongyang's calculation, is demonstrate that North Korea will not accept being sanctioned, and that it will answer a sanction with a punishment of its own. It is a message, aimed not only at Switzerland but at every country that might consider sanctioning a North Korean entity, and it is a message that is delivered in the language that North Korea has always used: the language of retaliation, of sovereignty, and of the refusal to be dictated to. Whether the message is heard, and whether it deters anyone, is another question. The history of North Korean diplomacy suggests that it is heard, and that it is also ignored, and that the cycle of sanction and retaliation continues, with each side convinced that the other will blink first.

The wider context is the deepening alliance between North Korea and Russia, which has, since 2022, become the central fact of North Korean foreign policy. North Korea has supplied Russia with artillery shells and missiles for the war in Ukraine, and Russia has, in return, supplied North Korea with food, fuel and technology. The two countries signed a mutual defence treaty in 2024, and North Korean troops have fought alongside Russian forces in Ukraine. The Songdowon camp's alleged role in the transfer of Ukrainian children is, in that context, not an isolated incident but a part of the alliance, and Switzerland's decision to sanction the camp is, in Pyongyang's eyes, an attack on that alliance. The expulsion of the Swiss envoy is, in the same logic, a defence of the alliance, and a signal to Moscow that Pyongyang will stand by its partner, even at the cost of a diplomatic relationship with a Western country that it had only just restored. The war in Ukraine has, in effect, reached Pyongyang, and it has done so through a children's camp on the east coast of North Korea.

What happens next

The immediate question is whether the 48-hour deadline will be met, and whether Sylvain will leave Pyongyang by Friday, November 27, as ordered. The Swiss ministry has said that it is arranging his return to Beijing, which suggests that the departure is already being organised, and that there will be no attempt to resist the order or to negotiate an extension. The office itself will be closed, and its staff — if there are any beyond the head of the office — will leave with him, and the building will stand empty, as it did from 2020 to October 2026. The Swiss Embassy in Beijing will take over responsibility for North Korea, as it did during the years when the Pyongyang office was closed, and Switzerland's contact with the North Korean government will, once again, be conducted from a distance, through Beijing, rather than in person, in Pyongyang.

The longer question is whether this is the end of the matter, or the beginning of a wider rupture. North Korea has, in the past, expelled diplomats and closed offices, and it has, in the past, reopened them when the political winds changed. The Swiss office was closed for six years, and it reopened when the winds changed, and it is possible that it will reopen again, when the winds change once more. But the winds that closed it are not the winds of a bilateral dispute between North Korea and Switzerland; they are the winds of the war in Ukraine, and of the sanctions regime that the West has built around that war, and those winds are not going to change soon. The sanctions on the Songdowon camp will remain in force, and the camp will remain on the list, and the North Koreans will continue to regard that as a hostile act. The office, in that sense, is not coming back soon, and the 26 days it was open may be remembered, in the history of North Korean diplomacy, as a brief and hopeful interlude that ended as suddenly as it began.

The lesson of the episode, for the diplomats who watch North Korea, is that the country's foreign policy is now driven, above all, by its alliance with Russia, and that it will sacrifice a relationship with the West — even a relationship that it had only just restored — for the sake of that alliance. The lesson for Switzerland is that its "universalist foreign policy", which has served it well for decades, has limits, and that there are countries that will not accept the distinction between sanctions and diplomacy, and that will treat a sanction as an attack, whatever the intention behind it. The lesson for the rest of the world is that the war in Ukraine is no longer a European war, and that its consequences are now felt in Pyongyang, in Wonsan, and in the office of a Swiss diplomat who had been in North Korea for a month. The camp, the sanctions, the expulsion and the 48-hour deadline are, together, a small story, but they are a small story that tells a large one: that the world is more connected than it looks, and that a children's camp on the east coast of North Korea can, in the space of a week, become the centre of a diplomatic crisis.

On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, North Korea shut Switzerland's office in Pyongyang, declared its head persona non grata, and gave him 48 hours to leave. The office had been open for 26 days. The sanctions that caused its closure remain in force. The children at the Songdowon camp remain at the centre of an allegation that neither side will drop. And the Swiss diplomat, who arrived in Pyongyang in October to rebuild his country's presence in the world's most isolated capital, will be in Beijing by Friday, with the office he ran closed behind him. The story is over, for now, and the question is what comes next — and whether the next opening, when it comes, will last longer than 26 days.
