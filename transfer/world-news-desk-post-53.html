TITLE: Russia's Own Records Show 490,334 Soldiers Have Died in Ukraine — and the True Toll May Be 700,000 LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Leaked Russian statistics analysed by the BBC show at least 490,334 Russian soldiers have died in Ukraine since 2022, with the true toll higher ---BODY---

Russian President Vladimir Putin in May 2024. Leaked records from Russia's own state statistics agency, analysed by the BBC, show that at least 490,334 Russian soldiers have died since the full-scale invasion of Ukraine began in February 2022. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (public domain, Kremlin.ru).

At least 490,334 Russian soldiers have died since Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, according to leaked death records from Russia's own state statistics agency, analysed by the BBC and published on Wednesday. The figure — drawn from more than 11 million death records held by Rosstat, Russia's Federal State Statistics Service, covering the years 2021 to September 2026 — is the most authoritative estimate yet of the human cost of a war that Vladimir Putin assumed would last only a few days, but has now dragged on for almost five years. It is also, by the account of the journalists who analysed the data, almost certainly an undercount. The independent Russian outlets Mediazona and Meduza, which analysed the same files, estimate that the real death toll, once delayed registrations are taken into account, stands at around 700,000 — and military experts cited by the BBC say the true figure could range from 527,295 to 644,471. Whichever number is right, the scale is staggering: Russia has lost, in less than five years of war in Ukraine, more soldiers than it lost in all of its post-Second World War conflicts combined, and the losses are still rising.

The records became available after a cyberattack on Rosstat, carried out by the Belarusian hacking group Cyber Partisans BY and the Ukraine-based group Silent Crow, which announced the breach on November 1 and shared the data with journalists on November 8. The BBC's Russian service gained access to more than 11 million death records from 2021 to September 2026 — a dataset so large that it captures, in anonymised form, the mortality of an entire nation across nearly six years. The files contain no names, but they do include ages, places of residence, places of death and circumstances of death, and it is those details that allowed the BBC and Mediazona to isolate the war dead from the millions of Russians who died of natural causes, disease and accidents in the same period. After removing duplicates and amended versions, Mediazona arrived at 11,658,178 unique deaths in the dataset. From those, the BBC filtered the records to deaths in Ukraine and in the Russian border regions that saw incursions by the Ukrainian army, and found 425,174 entries that explicitly state a cause of death related to military action — explosions, shrapnel wounds, and other injuries sustained during combat operations. The total of 490,334 includes those records and others identified through the pattern of deaths in the war zone.

Rosstat has admitted that it was the target of a cyberattack, but has denied that any protected information was leaked — a denial that the data itself makes difficult to sustain. The Kremlin's spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, said on Wednesday that only the Russian Defence Ministry can officially publish data on military losses, a statement that is, in effect, a refusal to confirm or deny the figures, and a reminder that the Russian state has not published a credible casualty count since the earliest days of the war. The BBC, for its part, has taken unusual care to establish that the leaked data is genuine. Together with Mediazona, it compared the Rosstat records with its own list of confirmed military deaths — a list compiled from official reports, social media posts, new memorials and graves since the beginning of the invasion — and found a match of more than 90 per cent. Mediazona and Meduza, in their own analysis, said they were confident the data was genuine. The records, in other words, have been checked against the one independent source that exists, and they agree.

How the number was reached — 425,174 combat deaths, a 90% match, and a list of 290,012 names

The methodology behind the 490,334 figure is what gives it its weight, and it is worth understanding in detail, because it is the difference between a guess and a measurement. The BBC's Russian service did not simply count deaths. It began with a dataset of more than 11 million records, and it applied a series of filters to isolate the deaths that could only have been caused by the war. First, it filtered by place of death, retaining only those deaths that occurred in Ukraine and in the Russian border regions where the Ukrainian army had carried out incursions. Then, within that subset, it identified the records that explicitly stated a military cause of death — the 425,174 entries that mention explosions, shrapnel wounds and other combat injuries. The total of 490,334 includes those explicit cases and the additional deaths that the pattern of the data identifies as war-related, even where the recorded cause of death is less specific.

The cross-check is what makes the figure credible. Since the invasion began, BBC Russian and Mediazona have maintained a list of Russian military deaths confirmed from open sources — official reports, social media announcements by families, photographs of new graves and new memorials — and that list has now reached 290,012 names. It is a documented floor, not a ceiling: it counts only the deaths that can be confirmed by name, and many deaths go unrecorded, particularly in the chaos of the front line. When the BBC compared the leaked Rosstat data with its own list, it found matches for 90 per cent of the records — a level of agreement that would be impossible if the leaked data were fabricated, and that confirms, in the most direct way possible, that the two sources are describing the same reality. The 290,012 confirmed dead are, by the BBC's own account, only a fraction of the total. Military experts estimate that the open-source list may account for only 45 to 55 per cent of the true death toll, which would put the real figure between 527,295 and 644,471 — a range that brackets the 490,334 counted directly in the leaked records, and that suggests the true number is higher still.

Mediazona and Meduza, working with the same files, went further. Their analysis, released on November 23, estimates that more than 700,000 Russian soldiers have been killed in the war, once delayed registrations are taken into account — the deaths that take time to be reflected in the statistics, because the paperwork lags behind the battlefield, and because some deaths are never formally registered at all. The report notes that military deaths take time to appear in official statistics, and suggests that the actual death toll far exceeds 490,000. The difference between the BBC's 490,334 and Mediazona's 700,000 is not a disagreement about the facts. It is a difference about how to account for the deaths that the records have not yet captured — the men who died in the last weeks of the dataset's coverage, the men whose deaths were recorded late, and the men whose deaths were never recorded at all. On either measure, the war has killed more Russian soldiers than any conflict since the Second World War, and the toll is still rising.

The young are dying first — 65% of men aged 18 to 24 who died in 2025 were killed in the war

The leaked records reveal something else, and it is perhaps the most disturbing finding in the entire analysis: the war is killing Russia's young men at a rate that is reshaping the country's demography. According to the BBC's analysis of the leaked files, in the first year of the war, the average age of those killed was 34 — already young, by the standards of a nation's mortality. But the more recent data is worse. The BBC found that 65 per cent of Russians aged 18 to 24 who died in 2025 were killed in the war, and 59 per cent of those aged 25 to 34. In other words, for Russian men in their late teens and twenties, the war is now the single most common cause of death — more common than disease, more common than accidents, more common than any other way a young man can die in Russia. For men in their early thirties, it accounts for nearly three in five deaths. The war has not merely killed a large number of Russians. It has fundamentally changed the pattern of who dies in Russia, and at what age — and it has done so in a way that will echo through the country's population for a generation.

The scale of the demographic damage is difficult to overstate. The leaked data covers more than 11 million deaths across nearly six years, and within that vast total, the war dead are a distinct and growing share — concentrated among the young, concentrated among men, and concentrated in the regions that supply the army's recruits. The men who are dying are the men who would have started families, built careers, paid taxes and raised the next generation. Their deaths are not merely a military statistic. They are a hole in the future of a country that was already facing a demographic crisis before the war began, with a falling birth rate and a shrinking population, and that has now lost, in less than five years, hundreds of thousands of its youngest and most productive citizens. The war's cost is measured not only in the dead, but in the children who will never be born to them, and in the communities — the villages, the small towns, the working-class districts — from which they were drawn, and to which they will never return.

The recruitment system that feeds the war is itself a part of the story. Russia has sustained its army through high wages, generous survivor benefits and the recruitment of prison inmates — incentives that have kept men volunteering, or at least enlisting, even as the death toll has climbed into the hundreds of thousands. But the incentives appear to be tapering, according to the analysis, and the question of how much longer Russia can sustain a war that is killing its young men at this rate is now an open one. The state has not published a casualty figure, and it will not — Peskov's statement that only the Defence Ministry can publish losses is, in practice, a guarantee that no honest number will ever be published by the state. But the leaked records have done what the state would not: they have shown the Russian people, and the world, what the war has cost, in the state's own numbers, recorded by the state's own statisticians, in a database that was never meant to be seen.

The wider toll — 1.2 million casualties, 2 million on the horizon, and a war without precedent since 1945

The 490,334 dead are only part of the toll. For every soldier killed, three or four more are wounded — the arithmetic of modern combat medicine — and the total number of Russian casualties, killed and wounded and missing, is far larger than the death toll alone. By the spring of 2026, a range of independent and government estimates had converged on a total of roughly 1.2 million Russian casualties since the February 2022 invasion: about 1.17 million by the United Kingdom's Ministry of Defence in its December 2025 count, 1.2 million by Western officials briefing Bloomberg in February 2026, and 1.1 to 1.5 million by The Economist in May 2026. The Center for Strategic and International Studies, which reached the same 1.2 million mark, framed the broader point bluntly: "No major power has suffered anywhere near these numbers of casualties or fatalities in any war since World War II." The Economist's most recent estimate, published on October 30, put the killed alone at 374,000 to 770,000, and the total killed and wounded at 1.4 to 2.1 million — a range that has risen sharply in just 15 months, from 190,000 to 350,000 killed in July 2025, and 280,000 to 518,000 in May 2026.

The trajectory is still rising. Ukraine's General Staff reported that Russian forces lost 49,040 personnel in October 2026 alone, as of October 29 — the highest monthly figure since the start of the full-scale invasion, with three days of the month still remaining, and above the previous record of 48,670 set in December 2024. Since the beginning of 2026, Ukraine's defence forces have killed or wounded 302,000 Russian troops using drones alone, according to Ukrainian figures. The head of Britain's signals intelligence agency, GCHQ, estimated in May 2026 that the Russian death toll was approaching 500,000 — a figure the leaked Rosstat data has now confirmed, and exceeded. The Netherlands' military intelligence agency estimated in April 2026 that Russia had suffered 1.2 million permanent losses, including more than 500,000 killed, as of the end of 2025. And the Meduza-Mediazona analysis, incorporating the leaked data, puts Russia on track to exceed 2 million overall casualties — a figure higher than all the wars Moscow has fought since the Second World War, combined.

The comparison with history is what gives these numbers their gravity. The Soviet Union's war in Afghanistan, which lasted nine years and helped to bring down the Soviet state, killed around 15,000 Soviet soldiers. The two Chechen wars killed, by most estimates, fewer than 10,000 Russian soldiers combined. The war in Ukraine has killed, by the leaked records alone, more than 490,000 — more than thirty times the Afghan toll, in half the time. The closest analogy, as one analysis put it, is not the Second World War that the Kremlin invokes, but the First: the trench-bound, artillery-dominated attrition of the Western Front, where armies bled by the hundred thousand for a few miles of churned earth. Since the start of 2024, Russian forces have gained less than 1.5 per cent of Ukraine, and in the spring of 2026, by the battlefield mapping most analysts treat as authoritative, they began losing ground outright. The war that was supposed to last a few days has lasted almost five years, has killed nearly half a million Russian soldiers by the state's own count, and has gained, in the last two years, almost nothing.

The other side of the ledger — Ukraine's losses, and a war that has no winners

Russia is not the only country paying a terrible price. Ukraine has also suffered astronomical losses — dead and wounded — defending against the invasion, although Kyiv has not released official figures for years. The Center for Strategic and International Studies estimated Ukraine's permanent losses — the dead and the seriously wounded — at about 600,000, and the Meduza-Mediazona analysis put the same figure at around 600,000, amid ongoing recruitment difficulties. The war has been, from the beginning, a war of attrition, and attrition is a contest in which both sides bleed. The difference is one of scale and of choice: Ukraine is fighting for its survival, on its own soil, against an invasion it did not start. Russia is fighting a war of choice, launched by a leader who assumed it would last a few days, and sustained by a state that has chosen, again and again, to keep paying the price rather than to stop.

The leaked records do not tell us how the war will end. They tell us only what it has cost so far, and the cost is now a matter of record — not in the estimates of Western intelligence agencies, not in the claims of Ukrainian officials, but in the files of Russia's own statistics agency, compiled by its own bureaucrats, from the death certificates of its own citizens. That is what makes the leak so significant, and so difficult for the Kremlin to dismiss. Peskov can say that only the Defence Ministry can publish losses. Rosstat can deny that protected data was leaked. But the data exists, it has been analysed, and it has been checked against the one independent source that exists, with a 90 per cent match. The 490,334 dead are not an estimate. They are a count — a count drawn from the state's own records, of the state's own dead, in a war the state started and the state refuses to count.

There is a further significance to the leak, and it is one that goes beyond the numbers. The breach was carried out by the Belarusian group Cyber Partisans BY and the Ukrainian group Silent Crow — hackers, operating from outside Russia, who broke into the systems of a state statistics agency and took the data that the state had refused to publish. It is a reminder that in the modern world, the secrets of even the most secretive state are not safe — and that the truth about a war, however carefully a government tries to hide it, has a way of finding its way out. The data was shared with journalists on November 8, and within weeks it had been analysed, verified and published. The Kremlin's response — to deny, to deflect, to insist that only the Defence Ministry can speak — is the response of a government that has lost control of its own story, and that knows it.

What happens next — a toll still rising, a war still grinding, and a number that cannot be unseen

The war is not over. Russian forces are still fighting in Ukraine, still losing men at a rate of tens of thousands a month — 49,040 in October alone, by Ukraine's count — and still gaining, by the most authoritative mapping, almost nothing. The 490,334 figure covers the period through September 29, 2026, and the deaths since then, in October and November, are not included. By the time the next analysis is done, the number will be higher, and the Mediazona estimate of 700,000, once delayed registrations are counted, may already have been passed. The toll will keep rising for as long as the war goes on, and the war, for now, shows no sign of ending.

For Vladimir Putin, the leaked numbers are a political problem as much as a military one. He launched the invasion in the belief that it would last a few days, that Kyiv would fall, and that the war would be a short, victorious campaign. Almost five years later, the war has killed, by his own government's count, nearly half a million Russian soldiers, wounded perhaps a million more, and left Russia on track for two million casualties — with less than 1.5 per cent of Ukraine gained since the start of 2024, and ground now being lost. The men who are dying are the young men of Russia — 65 per cent of the men aged 18 to 24 who died in 2025 were killed in the war — and their deaths are recorded, in the state's own files, in a database that the state tried to keep secret. The war was supposed to make Russia stronger. The leaked records show what it has actually done.

The number — 490,334 — is now a matter of public record, and it cannot be unseen. It will be cited, debated, and challenged, and the Kremlin will continue to deny it, as it has denied every casualty figure since the war began. But the denial no longer matters in the way it once did, because the figure does not come from an enemy, or from an estimate, or from a model. It comes from Rosstat, from the death certificates of Russian citizens, from the files of the Russian state itself. The BBC and Mediazona have done what the Russian government would not do: they have counted the dead, and they have published the number. At least 490,334 Russian soldiers have died in Ukraine. The true toll, by the best available estimate, is around 700,000. And the war that killed them is still going on.
