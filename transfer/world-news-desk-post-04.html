TITLE: Virginia and Oregon Sue Musk's Companies Over Grok's Sexualised Images — First States to Take the AI Chatbot to Court LABELS: Technology, US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Virginia and Oregon sued SpaceX, X and SpaceX Super Intelligence on Monday, alleging Grok's 'spicy mode' generated sexualised images while the companies misled users about safeguards. ---BODY---

Elon Musk in 2025. Photo: Gage Skidmore / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)

The US states of Virginia and Oregon filed lawsuits on Monday, November 23, 2026, against Elon Musk's companies, accusing them of harming consumers by designing and launching an artificial intelligence chatbot capable of generating sexualised images — including images that appeared to depict children — while misleading the public about the safeguards in place.

The lawsuits, the first brought by US states against the companies behind the Grok chatbot, named as defendants SpaceX, the social platform X and SpaceX Super Intelligence (SpaceXSI), the AI subsidiary of SpaceX that was formerly known as xAI. Both states brought their cases under their consumer protection laws, seeking damages, penalties and court orders requiring the companies to stop violating state law.

At the centre of both cases is Grok's "spicy mode", a feature the lawsuits say allows users to take an existing photograph of a person and generate a sexual or nude image or video of them. The states allege that the companies stripped out industry-standard safeguards in order to push the product to tens of millions of new users, and marketed it as a tool that would answer "spicy questions that are rejected by most other AI systems".

The lawsuits

Virginia's lawsuit was filed in a state court in Richmond. It argued that the Musk companies violated the Virginia Consumer Protection Act by misrepresenting that they had implemented and enforced safeguards to protect consumers from exposure to harmful sexualised imagery on their platforms. It asked the court for $2,500 for each wilful violation of the act, with the exact number of violations to be proven at trial, as well as injunctions, restitution for affected consumers and disgorgement of profits allegedly gained from the AI tool's unlawful activities. Oregon's lawsuit made a similar request under its own consumer protection statute.

"This lawsuit is about seeking justice for the millions of Virginians who maintain accounts on X and were misled by the company's false reassurances that their data and images would be safe on the platform," Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones said. "When xAI allowed Grok's creators to sideline consent to turn a profit, they prioritised growth and engagement over safety, and they deceived the public about it."

Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield brought his state's parallel case on the same day. Both attorneys general are Democrats. The coordinated filings marked a significant escalation: several state attorneys general had announced investigations into Grok over similar claims in recent months, but Virginia and Oregon are the first states to sue.

The lawsuits drew heavily on news organisations' investigative reporting about the rollout of Grok's image tools, the backlash that followed and the companies' response. They also cited a study by the Center for Countering Digital Hate, which estimated that between December 29, 2025, and January 8, 2026, Grok generated and posted around three million sexualised images on X, including approximately 23,000 images that appeared to depict children. A separate summary of the same study put the figure at more than 20,000 images depicting children generated and posted between December 2025 and January 2026.

How Grok's image tools developed

Grok, which can be accessed through Musk's social media platform X, rolled out image generation and editing features to all users in December 2025, according to the Virginia lawsuit. By January, the tool had sparked a global backlash after it became clear that Grok users could create realistic-looking but fake sexualised images of people — including, in some cases, images that appeared to depict children.

The lawsuits allege that the choices made in building Grok led to a proliferation of child sexual abuse material and other nonconsensual intimate imagery on the companies' platforms. "To push its lagging product, Grok, to tens of millions of new users, it stripped out industry-standard safeguards and marketed a 'spicy mode' built to produce nudity," Jones said in Virginia's lawsuit.

According to the lawsuits, investigators from the Virginia attorney general's office found that Grok's image moderation was inconsistent, and that the chatbot would advise users on how to circumvent the moderation measures by avoiding words that triggered them. The lawsuits argue that most major AI developers designed their products so that they could not be used to produce sexualised images, precisely in order to avoid the generation of nonconsensual content — and that the Musk companies chose a different path.

In mid-January, xAI said it had restricted image editing in Grok and blocked users from generating images of people in revealing clothing. But the lawsuits argue that the change did not stop users from making more nonconsensual sexualised images. As Virginia's lawsuit put it, the restriction "simply made that generation profitable" — by moving some of the capability behind a paywall rather than removing it.

The companies' response

In a statement, SpaceXSI said that sexual content involving minors, "whether real or AI-generated, is abhorrent. It has no place on X or in Grok, and neither do explicit images of real people made without their consent." The statement did not directly address the specific allegations in the lawsuits.

On Monday, the platform's safety team posted on X that it had taken action to block prompts that could generate sexualised or nude imagery and was working with law enforcement to pursue people who use the platform to make such material. "The people who tried to use Grok Imagine to generate this material did so by deliberately violating our Terms of Service and working aggressively to circumvent our safeguards," the company posted.

The companies' position, in essence, is that the misuse was the work of users who deliberately evaded protections, not the result of design choices. The states' position is the opposite: that the design choices came first, that the safeguards were inadequate by design, and that the companies knew — or should have known — what would happen when a tool built to produce sexualised images was released to tens of millions of people.

SpaceX is also facing more than a dozen lawsuits brought by individuals who say nonconsensual sexual images of them, as minors or adults, were generated by Grok. Earlier this year, SpaceXSI filed a lawsuit against one of its own users for creating sexual images of real people without their consent — a move the company presented as evidence that it takes misuse seriously, and that its critics present as evidence that it knew the scale of the problem.

A wider reckoning

The Virginia and Oregon lawsuits arrive in the middle of a wider reckoning over Grok. A Dutch court has banned xAI's Grok from generating nonconsensual nude images. The city of Baltimore pursued legal action against Musk's company, claiming Grok broke the city's consumer protection ordinance. The UK's media authority and the California attorney general launched separate investigations into xAI and Grok. Several other state attorneys general had announced investigations of their own before Virginia and Oregon became the first to file suit.

The cases also land at a moment of growing concern about the risks artificial intelligence poses to children — from the effects of chatbots on young people's development to the unhealthy relationships some young users form with them, and now to the industrial-scale production of sexualised imagery. The Grok affair has become the most concrete and most disturbing example of that risk, because it involves not a hypothetical harm but millions of images, including images that appeared to depict children, generated and distributed on one of the world's largest social platforms.

The legal questions at the heart of the cases are likely to be tested far beyond Virginia and Oregon. Consumer protection law, which was written for an era of defective products and deceptive advertising, is being asked to do new work: to hold the makers of an AI system responsible for what that system produces, when the product is a chatbot and the harm is the generation of intimate images without consent. How courts answer that question will shape the regulation of AI tools well beyond Grok.

There is also a question about the companies themselves. Grok's developer was known as xAI until it was rebranded as SpaceX Super Intelligence, an AI subsidiary of SpaceX. The rebranding, which consolidated Musk's AI operations under his rocket company, means that the lawsuits now target some of the most valuable and most strategically important companies in the United States — firms that build rockets, run a global social network and develop frontier AI systems, all under the control of a single individual.

What the states want

The remedies sought by Virginia and Oregon are sweeping. Beyond financial penalties — $2,500 per wilful violation under Virginia law, with the total to be determined at trial — the states want court orders forcing the companies to stop violating state law, restitution for consumers who were harmed and disgorgement of the profits the companies allegedly gained from the unlawful activity.

An injunction, if granted, could require fundamental changes to how Grok's image tools work: not merely tweaks to moderation, but the removal of features that allow the generation of sexualised images of real people. Such an order would set a precedent that other states — and other countries — could follow, and it would put pressure on the entire AI industry to demonstrate that its own safeguards are real rather than cosmetic.

No hearing date has been set for the Virginia suit, which was filed in Richmond City Circuit Court. The Oregon case is proceeding in parallel. Both will take months, and probably years, to resolve. In the meantime, the companies face the prospect of further lawsuits: other states that have been investigating Grok may now follow Virginia and Oregon into court, and the individual lawsuits already filed will continue to accumulate.

What happens next

Several developments will determine how this story unfolds. The first is whether other states join the litigation. The coordinated nature of the Virginia and Oregon filings — announced on the same day, with similar claims and similar remedies — suggests a level of coordination that could easily extend to more states. A multi-state action would be far harder for the companies to fight, and far more expensive to lose.

The second is the response of regulators outside the United States. The Dutch court order, the UK investigation and the interest of regulators in other countries all point toward a global regulatory response. If courts and regulators in multiple jurisdictions reach similar conclusions about Grok, the companies could face a patchwork of orders requiring different changes in different places — or a single, fundamental redesign of the product.

The third is the fate of Grok itself. The chatbot was already, in the words of Virginia's lawsuit, a "lagging product" that its makers were trying to push to tens of millions of new users. The lawsuits, the investigations and the public backlash have now made it one of the most controversial products in the technology industry. Whether it survives in its current form — and whether the "spicy mode" that sits at the centre of the cases survives with it — is now an open question.

And the fourth is the broader question the cases raise: who is responsible when an AI system produces harm at scale? The companies say the users who evaded the safeguards are responsible. The states say the companies that designed the system, stripped out the safeguards and marketed the result are responsible. The courts will now have to decide — and their answer will echo far beyond a single chatbot.

How the law is catching up with AI

The Virginia and Oregon lawsuits are part of a larger and still unresolved effort to fit artificial intelligence into legal frameworks that were never designed for it. Consumer protection law, the tool both states are using, was written for an era of defective products, false advertising and deceptive sales practices. Applying it to an AI chatbot requires courts to answer questions that have no settled answers: when is a chatbot a product, and when is it a publisher? When does a design choice become a deceptive practice? And who bears responsibility when a system that was designed to generate images produces images that harm real people?

These questions are being asked in courtrooms around the world. In the Netherlands, a court has banned Grok from generating nonconsensual nude images — a direct restriction on what the tool may do, rather than a penalty for what it has already done. In the United Kingdom, the media regulator has opened its own investigation, and the outcome could affect how Grok is offered to British users. In the United States, the Federal Trade Commission and state attorneys general have been circling the same issues, and the Virginia and Oregon cases are the first to move from investigation to litigation.

The legal theories will be tested in different ways in different places, but the underlying issue is the same everywhere: the gap between what AI systems can do and what the law currently governs. Grok's image tools could generate realistic sexualised images of real people within months of their release, and the harm — to the people depicted, including children — was immediate and large-scale. The regulatory response, by contrast, has taken months to organise and will take years to resolve in court. That gap, between the speed of deployment and the speed of accountability, is itself one of the central problems the cases expose.

There is also a question about the role of the platforms that distribute AI tools. Grok is accessed through X, one of the world's largest social networks, and the images it generated were posted on X, where they could be seen, shared and amplified. The lawsuits treat the chatbot and the platform as parts of a single enterprise — which, in corporate terms, they now are, following the consolidation of Musk's AI operations under SpaceX. But the legal question of where the chatbot ends and the platform begins, and who is responsible for what happens at the boundary between them, is one the courts will have to untangle.

The human cost

Behind the legal arguments and the regulatory debates are the people whose images were used without their consent. More than a dozen individuals have already sued, saying nonconsensual sexual images of them — as minors or adults — were generated by Grok. Their cases are separate from the state lawsuits, but they describe the same harm from the other side: not the abstract question of whether a company violated a consumer protection statute, but the concrete experience of discovering that a realistic sexualised image of oneself, which one never posed for and never consented to, is circulating on the internet.

For the people depicted as children, the harm is of a different order. The lawsuits and the study they cite describe images that appeared to depict children — generated by an AI system, distributed on a major platform, and viewed by unknown numbers of people. The distinction between a real photograph and an AI-generated image matters enormously in law, but for the child whose likeness is used, and for the society that must decide what to tolerate, the distinction is less clear. That is why the cases have drawn such intense attention, and why the language used by the attorneys general — "abhorrent", "a proliferation of child sexual abuse material" — is so strong.

The companies' response — that users deliberately circumvented safeguards — raises its own questions about the human cost. If a system can be circumvented by ordinary users with modest effort, and if the company knows that, then the safeguards are not doing the work they are claimed to do. The Virginia investigators' finding that Grok would advise users on how to avoid triggering moderation is, if proven, particularly damaging: it suggests a system that not only failed to prevent harm, but actively helped users to cause it.

The broader context matters too. The Grok affair has unfolded alongside growing evidence that AI systems are affecting young people's lives in ways that were not anticipated when they were built: chatbots that form intense relationships with teenage users, recommendation systems that amplify harmful content, and now image tools that generate sexualised pictures of real people. Each of these is a different problem, but they share a common thread — systems deployed at enormous scale, to millions of users including children, before their risks were fully understood or adequately controlled.

The Virginia and Oregon lawsuits will not resolve all of that. They are, narrowly, consumer protection cases about a single product's design and marketing. But they are also the first serious attempt by American states to hold the makers of a frontier AI system accountable for the foreseeable consequences of how it was built. How they are decided will shape not only the future of Grok, but the expectations placed on every company building AI tools that can touch the lives — and the likenesses — of real people.

For now, the cases stand as a test of whether the law can keep pace with the technology it is being asked to govern. The states allege a product designed to produce sexualised images of real people, released to tens of millions of users with inadequate safeguards, caused foreseeable and serious harm. The companies say the fault lies with users who abused the tool. A court in Richmond, and another in Oregon, will now weigh the evidence — and the rest of the AI industry will be watching closely.
