TITLE: Elon Musk Just Promised Every American Child 2 SpaceX Shares — an $18 Billion Giveaway LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Elon Musk says every child in America will get two SpaceX shares worth about $18 billion, one from the company and one from him personally ---BODY---

Elon Musk, the chief executive of SpaceX and the world's richest person, in March 2024. He has promised two SpaceX shares to every child in America. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Elon Musk has just made one of the largest personal giveaways in modern business history. On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the SpaceX chief executive announced that every child in the United States will receive two shares in his rocket and satellite company — one share donated by SpaceX itself and one share donated by Musk personally. At the price SpaceX shares closed at on Wednesday, the combined gift is worth roughly US$18 billion, and it would put a slice of the world's most valuable private space company into the hands of about 72 million American children.

"Every child in America will receive two SpaceX shares, one from the company and one from me personally," Musk wrote in a short post on X, the social media platform he owns. The announcement came hours after SpaceX itself said it would contribute one newly issued share of its Class A common stock to every currently active Trump Account, the tax-advantaged investment accounts the Trump administration created for children under the age of 18. Musk then said he would personally match the company's contribution, share for share, taking the total to two shares for every eligible child.

The numbers behind the promise are staggering, and they explain why the announcement travelled around the world within minutes. SpaceX shares closed at US$127.44 on Wednesday, according to Reuters. Two shares per child, multiplied across the roughly 72 million children under the age of 18 in the United States, according to estimates from the US Census Bureau, puts the value of the giveaway at approximately US$18.35 billion. Business Standard, using the same share price and the same population estimate, put the figure at about US$18.4 billion. The White House said in October that almost 70 million Trump Accounts had already been opened, which is the pool of accounts SpaceX says it will use for its own donation.

That is not the end of the arithmetic. SpaceX president and chief operating officer Gwynne Shotwell and her husband have separately pledged more than 2 million SpaceX shares to children under the Trump Accounts programme, a commitment they first made in October. According to a post on X by the influencer Sawyer Merritt on Wednesday, SpaceX, Musk and Shotwell will together donate a combined 142 million SpaceX shares, currently worth about US$18.1 billion, to roughly 70 million American children through Trump Accounts. The breakdown, as Merritt laid it out, is about 70 million shares from SpaceX, about 70 million shares from Musk personally, and the additional shares pledged by Shotwell.

To put the scale in context, the Musk and SpaceX pledge is roughly three times the size of the previous record contribution to Trump Accounts: the US$6.25 billion donated by Dell Technologies chief executive Michael Dell and his wife, Susan Dell. It is also, by a wide margin, the largest single act of share-based philanthropy ever tied to a private company. And it comes from a man whose personal fortune is estimated at about US$824 billion, making him the richest person on the planet by an enormous distance.

What exactly has Musk promised — and when will the shares arrive?

The promise has two parts, and the timing of the two parts is different. SpaceX said its own contribution — one newly issued share of Class A common stock for every currently active Trump Account — will be transferred next month, in December 2026. Musk's personal contribution, the second share for every child, will take longer. "The share directly from me will be transferred when my lockup expires in June next year," Musk wrote in a separate post on X. That means children will see the company's share land in their accounts within weeks, while Musk's own share will follow in June 2027, once the restriction on selling or transferring his shares expires.

There are, however, important details that Musk and SpaceX have not yet explained, and those details matter a great deal to the families who are supposed to benefit. SpaceX did not immediately respond to a request for comment from Reuters on whether Musk's personal share donations would be made through Trump Accounts or through some other route, or whether the shares would come with any kind of lockup period — a period during which the shares cannot be sold. It is also unclear whether the shares can be moved out of the Trump Accounts at all, or whether they must stay inside the accounts until the child reaches adulthood.

That uncertainty matters because a Trump Account is not an ordinary brokerage account. It is a tax-advantaged investment programme designed to give Americans under the age of 18 a stake in the stock market and to help them build wealth from an early age. Children born between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2028, also receive a one-time contribution of US$1,000 from the US Treasury when their account is opened. The accounts are meant to be long-term savings vehicles: money goes in early, grows over years, and is available when the child grows up. Whether a SpaceX share sitting inside such an account can be sold, transferred or borrowed against is a question neither Musk nor SpaceX has answered yet.

There is also a question about who exactly counts. SpaceX said its donation goes to "every currently active Trump Account". Musk's post, by contrast, said "every child in America". Those are not necessarily the same group. The White House said in October that it had opened almost 70 million accounts, while the Census Bureau estimates there are about 72 million children under 18 in the country. The gap of roughly 2 million children raises the question of whether children without an active Trump Account will still receive shares, and if so, how. Neither Musk nor SpaceX has clarified that point, and the company has not said what happens to children whose accounts are opened after the donation is made.

None of that stopped the announcement from dominating the news on the eve of Thanksgiving. It was, by any measure, a carefully timed piece of news: a feel-good story about children and wealth, delivered on the day before America's biggest family holiday, by the richest man in the world, about a company that has spent the year making headlines of its own.

This is the second time Musk has made this promise — and he doubled it

Wednesday's announcement was not the first time Musk has promised SpaceX shares to America's children. It was the second — and this time he doubled the offer. Last month, in October 2026, Musk announced that he would give one of his SpaceX shares to every child in America. "Next year, I will give one of my SpaceX shares to every kid in America. This will be the first of many," he wrote in a post on X at the time.

That first pledge, on its own, would already have been worth about US$9 billion at Wednesday's share price. The new announcement doubles it, adding a matching share from the company itself. The doubling matters for another reason: it turns a personal philanthropic gesture by one billionaire into a corporate act as well, with SpaceX issuing new shares to fund its half of the gift. Issuing new shares dilutes existing shareholders slightly, but at a company valued in the hundreds of billions of dollars, the dilution from 70 million shares is modest — and the publicity value, in a year when SpaceX has been fighting political battles on several fronts, is considerable.

The timing is also significant for SpaceX itself. The company has had a remarkable few weeks. On Wednesday, the same day as the share announcement, the Federal Communications Commission gave SpaceX the green light for Starmind, a constellation of up to one million satellites that will serve as data centres in orbit — the first such authorisation ever granted in the United States. The company, which trades under the ticker SPCX, has also been expanding its Starlink satellite internet service and its Starship rocket programme, and its shares have been climbing. A share price of US$127.44 reflects a company that investors believe is still growing fast, which is precisely what makes two shares per child a meaningful gift rather than a token one.

But the share price is also the reason the gift's value is so hard to pin down. SpaceX is a private company whose shares do not trade on a public stock exchange in the ordinary way, and the US$127.44 figure is the price at which the shares most recently changed hands. If SpaceX's value rises, the two shares in every child's account will be worth more; if it falls, they will be worth less. Two shares worth about US$255 today could be worth far more — or far less — by the time a newborn receiving them today turns 18.

What is a Trump Account, and why does it matter?

To understand the giveaway, you have to understand the vehicle Musk has chosen to deliver it. Trump Accounts are a programme created by the Trump administration to give every American child a stake in the stock market from birth. The idea is simple: open an investment account for a child, put money in it early, let it compound for 18 years, and hand the young adult a meaningful sum when they come of age. For children born between January 1, 2025, and December 31, 2028, the US Treasury contributes a one-time US$1,000 to get the account started. Parents, relatives, employers and now, apparently, billionaires can add to it.

The White House said in October that it had opened almost 70 million accounts, which suggests the programme has reached nearly every child in the country. That scale is what makes it such an attractive channel for a gift like Musk's: instead of building a new distribution system, Musk can use an existing one that already reaches almost every family. It is also what makes the programme politically significant. The accounts carry the president's name, and the largest donations to them so far have come from people and companies closely associated with the administration's agenda.

The Dell family's US$6.25 billion was the previous record. Musk's roughly US$18 billion is about three times that. The symbolism is hard to miss: the world's richest man, whose companies have received enormous government contracts and whose relationship with the Trump administration has been close, is now the largest single contributor to the president's signature children's savings programme. Supporters will call it generosity. Critics will call it something else. Either way, it ties the fortunes of 70 million children, in a small but real way, to the fortunes of SpaceX.

There is a broader debate behind the announcement, too. Musk and Anthropic, the artificial intelligence company, have recently laid out sharply different blueprints for sharing some of the spoils of the AI boom, amid growing public and political scrutiny of the industry. Anthropic has argued for structures that spread the gains from artificial intelligence more broadly through public institutions; Musk has argued for direct ownership — giving people shares in the companies building the future. Wednesday's announcement is Musk's version of that argument made real: instead of taxing the AI boom and redistributing it, give every child a piece of the company itself.

That philosophy has obvious appeal, and obvious limits. Two shares of SpaceX, worth about US$255, will not change a child's life on their own. But if SpaceX continues to grow at anything like the pace it has grown over the past decade, those two shares could be worth many times more by the time the child is 18. And the gift is, as Musk himself said last month, "the first of many" — a phrase that suggests he intends to repeat it, or to expand it, in the years ahead.

What families should know before they celebrate

For parents, the practical questions are the ones that matter most, and several of them remain unanswered. First, is your child covered? If your child has an active Trump Account, SpaceX says the company's share is coming next month. If your child does not have an account, or if the account is not yet active, the position is unclear, and parents who want to be sure should check their child's account status and, if necessary, open or activate one.

Second, when will the shares actually appear? The company's share is due to be transferred in December 2026. Musk's personal share will not arrive until his lockup expires in June 2027. Families should not expect to see two shares in the account at the same time, and should not expect to be able to do anything with the shares immediately.

Third, can the shares be sold? This is the biggest open question. SpaceX has not said whether the shares will be subject to a lockup period, and it has not said whether they can be moved out of a Trump Account. If the shares are locked inside the account until the child turns 18, then the gift is a long-term investment, not spending money — which is, after all, the point of the Trump Account programme. But families who were hoping for a quick windfall should adjust their expectations.

Fourth, what about tax? Trump Accounts are tax-advantaged, which means the tax treatment of the shares is likely to be favourable, but the details have not been published, and families with questions should wait for official guidance from the programme rather than relying on social media posts.

And fifth, what is the gift actually worth? Two shares at US$127.44 each is about US$255 today. That is real money, but it is not life-changing money, and its future value depends entirely on SpaceX's performance. The company is one of the most successful private companies in history, but it is also a company that takes enormous risks — building the largest rockets ever flown, launching tens of thousands of satellites, and betting its future on a constellation of orbital data centres that regulators only approved this week. The shares could soar. They could also fall. That is the nature of owning a piece of a single company, and it is a risk that 70 million children will now share, in a small way, whether their parents chose it or not.

The bigger picture: a new kind of philanthropy, or a new kind of politics?

Step back from the details, and Wednesday's announcement looks like something larger than a gift. It is an experiment in a new kind of philanthropy — one in which the rich do not give money to charities or foundations, but give ownership in their own companies directly to the public. It is also, inevitably, a political act. The gift flows through a programme that carries the president's name, it was announced by a man who has been one of the administration's most prominent allies, and it arrives at a moment when the administration is under pressure over the cost of living, over tariffs, and over the price of Thanksgiving dinner itself.

Whether the experiment works will depend on things Musk has not yet explained: the lockup, the transfer mechanism, the coverage of children without active accounts, and the tax treatment. It will also depend on SpaceX itself. If the company's Starmind constellation, its Starship programme and its satellite business deliver on their promise, then two shares per child could grow into a genuinely meaningful sum — a small stake in the space economy, given to every child in America at birth. If they do not, then the gift will be remembered as a headline rather than a fortune.

For now, what is certain is this: on the day before Thanksgiving 2026, the world's richest man promised every child in America a piece of his rocket company, doubled a promise he had made a month earlier, and put a number on it — about US$18 billion, 142 million shares, 70 million children, two shares each. The shares will start arriving in December. The answers to the questions around them will have to follow.

How SpaceX became valuable enough to give away $18 billion

The giveaway is only possible because of what SpaceX has become. When Musk founded the company in 2002, it was a start-up with a handful of employees and a seemingly impossible goal: to cut the cost of reaching orbit by building reusable rockets. The early years were brutal. The first three launches of the Falcon 1 rocket all failed, and by 2008 the company was weeks from bankruptcy when the fourth launch finally succeeded and NASA awarded it a US$1.6 billion contract to resupply the International Space Station.

From there, the growth was extraordinary. The Falcon 9 became the workhorse of the global launch industry, flying more than 300 times and landing its first stage back on Earth again and again — a trick no other rocket company had ever made routine. The Falcon Heavy followed, then the Crew Dragon spacecraft, which began carrying NASA astronauts to the space station in 2020. Starlink, the satellite internet constellation, grew from an idea into the largest satellite network in history, with thousands of satellites in orbit and millions of customers around the world. Starship, the largest rocket ever built, is now flying regularly, and this week the Federal Communications Commission authorised Starmind, the company's plan to put up to one million satellites in orbit as data centres — a project that, if it works, could make SpaceX as much a computing company as a rocket company.

That transformation is what a share price of US$127.44 represents. Investors are not valuing a rocket company; they are valuing a company that launches the world's satellites, carries its astronauts, sells internet from space, and now wants to put its data centres in orbit too. It is also why Musk can afford to give away 142 million shares without blinking: at that price, the shares represent a small fraction of a company valued in the hundreds of billions of dollars, and a small fraction of a personal fortune estimated at US$824 billion. The gift is enormous in absolute terms, and small in relative ones — which is, perhaps, the most honest way to describe it.
