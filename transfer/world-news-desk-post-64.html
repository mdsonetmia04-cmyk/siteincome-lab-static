TITLE: America's First Hypersonic Missile Battery Is Finally Ready — 1,725 Miles, Mach 5+, and 3 Years Late LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The US Army says its first Dark Eagle hypersonic battery is fully fielded — 1,725-mile range, Mach 5+ speed — after missing 2023 and 2025 deadlines ---BODY---

The US Army's Long Range Hypersonic Weapon system, known as Dark Eagle, deployed in Australia's Northern Territory in July 2025 for Exercise Talisman Sabre 25 — the first time the system had been used west of the International Date Line. Photo: U.S. Army photo by Sgt. Perla Alfaro, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

The United States Army has finally done it. After years of delays, failed tests, missed deadlines and quiet embarrassment, the Army's first battery of hypersonic missiles is fully fielded and ready for war. Lieutenant General Frank Lozano, the Army's top general in charge of buying munitions, told the defence publication Breaking Defense in an interview on November 19 that the first Long Range Hypersonic Weapon battery, known by its nickname Dark Eagle, "is fully fielded. It is ready to go." It was a short sentence, and it was three years late, but it marked the moment when the United States joined the small club of countries that actually possess a working hypersonic weapon — a club that, until now, has been dominated by Russia and China, and in which America has been the conspicuous latecomer.

The announcement matters because hypersonic weapons are, by most accounts, the most important new class of weapon to appear in a generation. A hypersonic missile travels at more than five times the speed of sound — Mach 5, or more than 3,800 miles per hour — and it does so not on a predictable ballistic arc, like a traditional missile, but on a manoeuvring glide path that makes it extremely difficult for air defences to track, predict and intercept. The Dark Eagle is designed to strike targets at ranges of up to 1,725 miles, according to the Congressional Research Service, which means that a battery based in the western Pacific could hit targets deep inside China, and a battery in Europe could hit targets deep inside Russia. It is a weapon built for a specific purpose: to give the United States the ability to strike high-value, time-sensitive targets — command centres, air defences, missile launchers — before an enemy can move them or shoot the missile down. And it is a weapon that, until this month, the United States did not have.

The road to this announcement has been long, expensive and, for the Army, deeply frustrating. The service initially planned to field the first Dark Eagle battery in 2023, but testing problems — including a mechanical engineering problem with the launcher — prevented it from meeting that goal. The target was then pushed to September 2025, but that deadline was missed as well. As recently as October 2026, Bloomberg reported that the Army had still not completely fielded its first battery, and the Army's own programme office told Bloomberg at the time that it "continues fielding activities" for the Dark Eagle and still had to conduct more training and other sustainment and readiness work "before declaring the system fully fielded." When Lozano was asked, in the November 19 interview, whether that October report had been inaccurate, he declined to say so directly. "I don't want to go so far as to say that," he said. "This is a rapidly evolving space." It was a careful answer, and it was the answer of a man who knew that the programme had been behind schedule for so long that nobody in Washington was inclined to believe a declaration of success without evidence.

Lozano was, in fact, careful about almost everything in the interview, and his caution is itself revealing. He declined to disclose when or where the first battery was fielded, offering few details about the milestone beyond confirming that the weapon is operational and ready for deployment. He would not say which unit has the battery, or where it is based, or whether it has been deployed overseas. He described the system as a "globally deployable combat capability" that "can be deployed and employed anywhere in the world," but he would not say where it is now. When he was asked when the first battery officially reached full fielding status, he pointed to the fact that some of the weapon's ground support equipment had been delivered as early as 2021, making it difficult, he said, to identify a specific date for the milestone. The secrecy is understandable — the location of a hypersonic battery is exactly the kind of information that an adversary would want — but it also means that the public is being asked to take the Army's word for it, and the Army's word on this programme has, over the years, been wrong more than once.

What Dark Eagle actually is

The Dark Eagle is a ground-launched missile system, and its full name is the Long Range Hypersonic Weapon, or LRHW. It consists of a two-stage solid-fuel rocket booster carrying a Common Hypersonic Glide Body, a wedge-shaped vehicle that separates from the booster at high altitude and then glides to its target at hypersonic speed, manoeuvring as it goes. The glide body is the heart of the system, and it is the part that makes the weapon so hard to defend against: because it does not follow a ballistic trajectory, existing missile defence systems, which are designed to predict where a ballistic missile will be, struggle to intercept it. The same glide body is used by the Navy's Conventional Prompt Strike weapon, which will be carried on Zumwalt-class destroyers and Virginia-class submarines, so the Army and the Navy are, in effect, sharing the most difficult and most expensive part of the technology. Lockheed Martin is the prime contractor for the booster and the weapon assembly, and Dynetics, a subsidiary of Leidos, produces the glide body.

A single Dark Eagle battery is a substantial piece of military hardware. It comprises four trailer-mounted transporter-erector-launchers, each carrying two missiles in canisters, for a total of eight rounds, as well as a Battery Operations Centre and a support vehicle. The launchers are built on modified heavy trucks, which means the battery is road-mobile: it can be driven to a firing position, set up, fired, and moved again before an enemy can locate and strike it. That mobility is a central part of the system's design, because a fixed missile site is a target, and a mobile one is a problem. The open-source cost of a single missile is widely cited at around $41 million, which makes each round one of the most expensive conventional weapons in the American arsenal, and which raises an obvious question about how many of them the Army can afford to buy, and how many it can afford to fire. A weapon that costs $41 million a shot is a weapon that will be used sparingly, and only against targets that are worth $41 million.

The Army is now working to field the second and third batteries, and Lozano said that the service is "already over halfway through production" of the third battery, though he could not speculate when either of them would be ready, citing operational security concerns. "I don't have a firm date on the wall," he said, "I think we'll see that the second battery and the third battery will realize their full capability at a faster pace and a faster rate than the first battery did." The optimism is understandable, and it may even be justified: the first battery is always the hardest, because it is the one that has to solve the engineering problems, and the ones that follow benefit from the lessons learned. But the history of this programme suggests that optimism should be treated with caution. The first battery was supposed to be ready in 2023, and it was not ready until late 2026, and the second and third batteries will have to be built, tested, trained and fielded in a world in which the Army's hypersonic budget is under pressure and the technology is still, by the Army's own admission, rapidly evolving.

Why America was late — and why it matters now

The most striking thing about the Dark Eagle announcement is not that it happened, but that it took so long. The United States tested hypersonic concepts for decades, and it spent billions of dollars on the research, but it was slow to field an actual weapon, and the reasons for that slowness are a case study in how American defence procurement can go wrong. The programme was split between the Army and the Navy, which shared the glide body but had different requirements, different timelines and different bureaucracies. The testing programme was disrupted by failures, including a launcher problem that took years to fix. The funding was uncertain, and the political attention was elsewhere, focused on counterterrorism and then on Ukraine. And the institutional culture of the American military, which has always preferred weapons that are reliable and well-tested to weapons that are new and risky, was slow to embrace a technology that was, by its nature, experimental and uncertain.

Russia and China, by contrast, moved faster, and they did so because they had a clearer sense of what they wanted the weapons for. Russia fielded the Kinzhal, an air-launched hypersonic missile, and the Avangard, a hypersonic glide vehicle, and it has used the Kinzhal in combat in Ukraine. China fielded the DF-17, a medium-range ballistic missile carrying a hypersonic glide vehicle, and it has built its entire military strategy around the ability to strike American ships and bases in the western Pacific before they can respond. Both countries treated hypersonics as a priority, and both countries were willing to accept the risks and the costs of fielding a weapon before it was perfect. The United States, which had the technology first, ended up fielding it last, and the gap between American ambition and American delivery became, over the years, a source of quiet embarrassment in Washington, and of open concern among the country's allies in the Pacific.

The timing of the announcement matters, because the strategic context has changed dramatically since the programme began. The United States is now in a period of open military competition with China in the western Pacific, and the Chinese military has built, over the past decade, a force designed to push American ships and aircraft out of the region. A hypersonic weapon that can strike targets 1,725 miles away, from a mobile launcher that is hard to find and hard to kill, is a direct answer to that challenge, and it is a weapon that the American military has been asking for, in one form or another, for years. The Dark Eagle is also a message to Russia, which has used its own hypersonic weapons in Ukraine and has made a point of advertising them, and to the American public, which has been told, repeatedly, that the United States is falling behind in the race for the weapons of the future. The announcement is, in that sense, as much a political statement as a military one: it says that the United States is back in the race, and that it intends to stay there.

The deployment that came first

There is, however, a detail in the story that complicates the simple narrative of a programme that was late and has now arrived, and it is worth setting out, because it shows how the Army has been using the Dark Eagle even before it was officially fielded. In July 2025, the 3rd Multi-Domain Task Force deployed the Long Range Hypersonic Weapon system to Australia's Northern Territory, to participate in Exercise Talisman Sabre 25, the largest bilateral military exercise between Australia and the United States. According to the Army, the exercise marked the first operational employment of the Dark Eagle west of the International Date Line and outside the continental United States. The photographs from that deployment, taken by Sergeant Perla Alfaro of the US Army, show the system's transporter-erector-launchers in the Australian outback, and they were, for months, the most public evidence that the Dark Eagle existed at all outside a test range.

The Australian deployment is significant for two reasons. The first is that it showed the system could be moved, set up and operated far from home, in a realistic exercise environment, which is a much harder test than a firing on a controlled range. The second is that it showed the Army's intent: the western Pacific is where the Dark Eagle is meant to operate, and Australia is where it is meant to be based, or at least where it is meant to train. The exercise was, in that sense, a preview of the weapon's future, and it was a preview that came more than a year before the Army was willing to say, officially, that the system was fully fielded. The gap between the preview and the declaration is a measure of how cautious the Army has been, and of how much it has learned, over the years, about the danger of declaring a weapon ready before it is.

The same caution is visible in the way the Army has talked about the weapon's deployment since the announcement. Lozano's refusal to say where the battery is, or when it was fielded, is not evasiveness for its own sake; it is the standard practice for a weapon system whose value depends, in part, on an adversary not knowing where it is. But it also means that the public, and the Congress that pays for the weapon, will have to wait for independent confirmation that the battery is what the Army says it is. The history of this programme — the missed deadlines, the October 2026 Bloomberg report, the careful language of the November interview — suggests that that confirmation will come, if it comes at all, slowly, and that the Army will be judged not on its announcement but on what it does next: whether the second and third batteries arrive on time, whether the system is deployed where it is needed, and whether it works when it is called upon.

What comes next

The most important question now is not whether the first battery is ready — the Army says it is, and there is no reason to doubt that, at least in the sense that the hardware has been delivered and the soldiers have been trained — but whether the United States can build enough of these weapons, fast enough, and cheaply enough, to matter. Eight missiles in a single battery is not a large arsenal, and $41 million a missile is not a small price. The Army will need more batteries, and more missiles, and it will need them in the Pacific, where the threat is greatest, and it will need them soon, because China is not standing still. The second and third batteries are in production, and Lozano has said that they will arrive faster than the first, but the programme's history gives little reason for confidence in that prediction, and the Congress that must fund the programme will want to see evidence before it writes the next cheque.

The second question is whether the technology itself is as decisive as its advocates claim. Hypersonic weapons are fast, and they are hard to intercept, but they are also expensive, they are difficult to build, and they are not, by themselves, a solution to the strategic problems that the United States faces in the Pacific. A hypersonic missile can destroy a command centre or an air defence site, but it cannot, on its own, win a war, and it cannot compensate for a shortage of ships, or aircraft, or the industrial capacity to build them. The Dark Eagle is a powerful weapon, and it is a weapon that the United States needed, but it is not a magic weapon, and the danger is that its arrival will be treated as if it were, and that the harder work of building the force that surrounds it will be neglected.

The third question is what Russia and China will do in response. Both countries have hypersonic weapons of their own, and both have been watching the American programme closely, and both will now have to reckon with the fact that the United States has, at last, fielded a system of its own. The history of military technology suggests that the response will be an acceleration: more Russian hypersonics, more Chinese hypersonics, and more investment, on all sides, in the defences that might, one day, be able to stop them. The hypersonic race is, in that sense, like every arms race that has come before it: it does not end with one side fielding a weapon, but with both sides fielding more of them, and the only question is how fast, and how far, it goes. The Dark Eagle is a milestone, and it is a milestone worth marking, but it is not the end of the race. It is, if anything, the beginning of its next, and faster, phase.

For now, though, the Army has something it did not have a week ago: a hypersonic weapon that it can call its own, that it can deploy anywhere in the world, and that it can, if it has to, use. It took three years longer than planned, it cost more than anyone expected, and it arrived in a world that is more dangerous than the one in which it was conceived. But it has arrived, and the fact that it has arrived matters, not only for the soldiers who will operate it, but for the country that built it, and for the adversaries who will now have to reckon with it. The Dark Eagle is, at last, ready to fly. The question is where, and when, and whether it will be enough.

By the numbers: the Dark Eagle

It is worth, at the end, setting out the numbers one more time, together, because they are the numbers that define the weapon, and because they deserve to be seen in one place. The Dark Eagle, formally the Long Range Hypersonic Weapon, has a reported range of 1,725 miles, according to the Congressional Research Service, and a speed of more than Mach 5, or more than 3,800 miles per hour. A single battery comprises four trailer-mounted transporter-erector-launchers, each carrying two missiles, for a total of eight rounds, plus a Battery Operations Centre and a support vehicle. The open-source cost of a single missile is widely cited at around $41 million. The Army originally planned to field the first battery in 2023, then pushed the target to September 2025, and missed both deadlines. Lieutenant General Frank Lozano, the Army's Program Executive for Fires, told Breaking Defense on November 19, 2026, that the battery "is fully fielded. It is ready to go." As of October 2026, Bloomberg had reported that the battery had not yet been fully fielded. The Army is now working on the second and third batteries, and is, according to Lozano, "already over halfway through production" of the third. The system uses the Common Hypersonic Glide Body, which is shared with the Navy's Conventional Prompt Strike weapon. In July 2025, the system was deployed to Australia for Exercise Talisman Sabre 25, the first time it had been used west of the International Date Line. Those are the numbers, and they are the story of a weapon that took three years longer than planned to arrive, and that has, at last, arrived.
