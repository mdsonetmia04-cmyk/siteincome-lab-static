TITLE: Macy's Just Postponed Its 100th Thanksgiving Parade — 250 Million Americans in the Path of a Monster Nor'easter LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Macy's postponed its 100th Thanksgiving Day Parade to Saturday as a nor'easter threatens 250 million Americans with 70 mph winds and flooding ---BODY---

A Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade balloon being inflated in New York City. The parade's giant balloons are the reason the 100th parade has now been postponed — the forecast winds are far above the safety limits for flying them. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA).

For the first time in its 100-year history, the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade will not happen on Thanksgiving Day. The 100th annual parade in New York City has been postponed until Saturday because of a major storm bearing down on the East Coast, Reuters reported on Wednesday evening, marking the first time the parade has ever been cancelled due to severe weather. The parade will now happen at 8:30 a.m. ET on Saturday, the retailer said, after safety concerns about the storm — and about the parade's giant balloons — forced a decision that no organiser, no mayor and no storm in a century had ever forced before. The National Weather Service has issued a high wind warning and a coastal flood warning for New York City for Thursday into Friday, and the storm behind them is not a local problem. It is a nor'easter that is expected to affect some 250 million Americans, from Florida to Maine, with heavy rain, howling winds, coastal flooding and snow, while a second system brings record king tides to the West Coast. Thanksgiving 2026, the busiest travel holiday of the year, is about to be a weather story first and a holiday second.

The postponement is a reversal that came late in the day. As recently as Wednesday afternoon, the parade was still scheduled to go ahead on Thursday morning, with organisers and city officials saying they would make a final decision on Thanksgiving morning based on the wind along the route. But the forecast kept getting worse, and by Wednesday evening the decision was made. Safety concerns regarding the storm and the large balloons that are part of the parade contributed to the decision, CBS News reported earlier, citing sources familiar with city security planning. The balloons are the heart of the problem. New York City has strict safety rules governing when the giant character balloons can fly: they cannot be flown when sustained winds exceed 23 miles per hour, or when wind gusts exceed 34 miles per hour. The forecast for Thursday morning blows straight past both limits. Wind gusts could exceed 30 miles per hour when the parade begins at 8:30 a.m., increasing to around 40 miles per hour by 10 a.m., according to the National Weather Service, and gusts could reach 40 to 50 miles per hour during the afternoon. A high wind watch was in effect for all of Thanksgiving Day in New York City, with wind gusts of up to 60 miles per hour possible. The balloons, in other words, were never going to fly — and in the end, neither would the parade itself, on its scheduled day.

The storm is a nor'easter, the kind of powerful coastal system that forms when cold air from the north meets warm, moist air over the Atlantic, and this one is arriving at the worst possible moment. It will ramp up in the Southeast on Wednesday, according to The Weather Channel, with rain, wind and coastal flooding causing hazardous conditions for travellers heading home for the holiday, before moving its way to the Northeast, the Great Lakes and the Ohio Valley. Along the coast, the strongest gusts are forecast at 50 miles per hour or more, with gusts of 45 miles per hour or more inland to the Interstate-95 corridor and the central Appalachians, and along the New Jersey shore and Delaware beaches gusts will reach 50 to 60 miles per hour, with 40 to 50 miles per hour inland. The National Weather Service in Mount Holly, New Jersey, issued a high wind watch for the coastal strip from late Wednesday night through late Thursday night, and warned that tree damage and power outages will be possible from the storm on Thanksgiving Day. The Weather Prediction Center said the biggest impact will be seen starting in the Southeast, before the storm moves north, and that while flooding is a major consideration, meteorologists are more concerned about the powerful winds accompanying the storm, which could result in tree damage, power outages and other disasters for homeowners to contend with on the big day.

Coastal flooding may be the storm's most destructive feature. As the nor'easter moves up the Eastern Seaboard, strong onshore winds, high surf and high astronomical tides will combine to push seawater into low-lying areas, and NOAA's Weather Prediction Center said significant flooding and beach erosion are expected from Georgia and the Carolinas up through the Northeast. The National Weather Service in New Jersey warned that widespread minor to moderate tidal flooding is expected, with the highest high tide forecast for Thursday morning, November 26, and widespread moderate to major coastal flooding expected along the Atlantic coasts of New Jersey and Delaware, and Delaware Bay. Some forecasters are using a comparison that will alarm anyone who remembers 2012: parts of New Jersey and Delaware Bay face the threat of Superstorm Sandy-level coastal flooding. The flooding will bring seawater over low-lying infrastructure, force numerous road closures and eat away at beaches, and the threat will not pass quickly — the coastal flooding threat area is expected to move south to Southern California on Thanksgiving Day, as the same system drags its energy across the country. Soaking rains are expected from the Southeast northward through southern New England by Thanksgiving, with rainfall amounts of 1 to 4 inches forecast in the Carolinas and the Mid-Atlantic, and 1 to 2 inches possible across southern New England.

The balloons that could not fly

The Macy's parade balloons are the most famous part of the parade, and they are also the most vulnerable to wind. The parade was planning to feature a record 100 inflatables this year, along with elaborate floats, marching bands and performances, with more than 11,000 participants and volunteers, and performances from artists including Megan Moroney and Teddy Swims. The balloons are enormous — some as tall as a five-storey building — and they are guided by teams of handlers holding ropes, which means that in strong wind they become, in effect, giant sails. That is why the city imposed the wind thresholds after a series of accidents in earlier decades, when blown-about balloons injured spectators. The thresholds exist to prevent previous mishaps from blown-about balloons, and they are absolute: sustained winds must remain below 23 miles per hour and gusts have to be lower than 34 miles per hour for the balloons to fly. On Thursday morning, the forecast puts sustained winds at around 25 miles per hour and gusts at up to 35 miles per hour — just above both limits — and the gusts are expected to keep rising through the morning.

Forecasters had been warning for days that this was coming. On Monday, AccuWeather's vice president of forecast operations, Dan DePodwin, told the New York Post that there was a significant chance the balloons would be grounded for the first time in 55 years, and that it was probably the most significant chance in recent years of having no balloons. "If they didn't fly the balloons, it would be the first time in 55 years," DePodwin said, referring to the 1971 parade, the last time weather forced the balloons to stay grounded. He cautioned that it was too early to know just how bad the storm would be, while stressing that it would likely be a blow for the parade even if it did not completely take out all the air, and that at the least it was looking likely the balloons would be lowered. "There is a significant risk that gusty winds ahead of the nor'easter cause the parade balloons to at least be lower than usual, and it's possible they are not able to fly at all," DePodwin said. "This scenario is most likely should the storm move a bit more quickly up the coast. If it's slower, the wind may intensify after the parade is over." The key piece of the forecast, he said, was the exact speed of the nor'easter as it comes up the coast — and by Wednesday evening, the answer was clear enough for the parade to be moved.

The storm track made the decision harder, not easier. "This is becoming VERY concerning," PIX 11 senior meteorologist Mike Masco warned on X on Monday, posting that all major forecast models, including their AI counterparts, were showing a powerful area of low pressure tracking west of New York City on Thanksgiving Day. "This storm track is especially concerning for the balloons," he wrote. A low-pressure system tracking west of the city puts New York on the storm's windiest side, with strong southerly winds funnelling up the coast, and the tall buildings of Manhattan can create a wind-tunnel effect as gusts funnel between the skyscrapers, potentially making conditions more dangerous for balloon handlers. Other experts predicted gusts could reach 40 miles per hour, far above the threshold for Thursday's parade. AccuWeather expert meteorologist Tyler Roys said in an email to USA TODAY that the last time weather kept all of the parade's giant balloons on the ground was in 1971, and that this year the upper end of the wind forecast puts that possibility back in play for the 100th parade. Macy's officials did not immediately respond to a request for comment on how concerned they were, or what precautionary measures were being put in place, when the New York Post asked on Monday — and by Wednesday, the precautionary measure was the postponement itself.

The 1971 grounding is the parade's own benchmark for bad weather, and it is worth remembering what happened that year. The balloons were kept on the ground because of high winds, and the parade went ahead without them — a parade, but not the parade people had come to see. The parade has never been cancelled due to weather in its 100-year history, and the balloons have not been grounded since 1971. What has happened now is different from both of those things: the parade has not been cancelled outright, but it has been moved, and the 100th anniversary parade — the one that was supposed to be the biggest and most celebratory in the event's history, with a record number of inflatables — will now take place on a Saturday, two days after Thanksgiving, in the storm's wake. For the millions of people who watch the parade on television every Thanksgiving morning, and for the crowds who line the route in Manhattan, the postponement changes the shape of the holiday itself. The parade is the opening ceremony of Thanksgiving in America, the thing that plays in the background while the turkey goes into the oven, and this year the background will be empty — or, more precisely, it will be filled with weather coverage.

A storm for 250 million people

The nor'easter is not only a New York story. Wednesday, November 25, is marked by sweeping severe weather disruptions across the country, with a powerful dual-coast weather system disrupting holiday travel and major events across the nation. On the East Coast and in the Midwest, the storm is bringing heavy rain and wind gusts up to 70 miles per hour along the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast coasts, raising alarms for widespread power outages and Superstorm Sandy-level coastal flooding in parts of New Jersey and Delaware Bay. In California, an atmospheric river combined with El Niño conditions has triggered record-breaking king tide flooding, pushing San Francisco tidal gauges to a historic 8.23 feet. A major coastal storm threatens heavy rain, strong winds and possible snow across more than 20 states from Florida to Maine during Thanksgiving week, according to FOX Weather, and the sheer scale of it is what makes the number so striking: some 250 million Americans — roughly three out of every four people in the country — are in the path of this weather system.

The snow is the other half of the storm, and it is hitting the middle of the country. A snowstorm is targeting the St. Louis area on November 25 and Thanksgiving Day, and some spots could see more than a half-foot of accumulation, according to AccuWeather, with accumulating snow forecast from far southeastern Iowa to around St. Louis to western Tennessee from Wednesday evening through Thanksgiving Day. The combination — snow in the Midwest, rain and wind on the East Coast, king tides on the West Coast — means that almost every form of severe weather the United States produces is happening at once, on the busiest travel week of the year. Thanksgiving travel was already expected to be enormous, with tens of millions of Americans travelling for the holiday, and the storm is now colliding with that movement in every direction: on the roads, where rain and snow will make driving dangerous; in the air, where wind and weather will delay flights; and in the cities, where flooding and power outages will disrupt the holiday itself.

The air travel picture was already difficult before the storm arrived. US travellers were facing long airport lines and bad weather in the run-up to Thanksgiving, and on Tuesday the Federal Aviation Administration said an outage with a ground radar system was delaying flights into Chicago O'Hare International Airport, one of the busiest airports in the world, and could force a halt to arriving flights. The radar issue is separate from the storm, but it is landing on top of it, and it is a reminder of how little slack there is in the system on Thanksgiving week. On the roads, the picture is mixed: gas prices have fallen for Thanksgiving travel, but they are still higher than last year, according to reports on Wednesday, which means drivers are paying more than they did a year ago even as the price at the pump comes down. The storm will make the driving itself harder, with heavy rain across the Southeast and Mid-Atlantic, snow in the Midwest, and high winds along the coast — and the advice from meteorologists and emergency officials is the same in every state: if you do not have to travel through the worst of it, do not.

Power, trees and the danger after the wind

The wind is the most immediate danger, and the warnings are unusually blunt. The National Weather Service warned on Monday evening that tree damage and power outages will be possible from the storm on Thanksgiving Day, and the forecasts put the strongest gusts along the mid-Atlantic coast and inland corridors on Thursday, with wind gusts of 50-plus miles per hour forecast along the coast and 40-plus miles per hour gusts inland into the Interstate-95 corridor through at least Friday. A high wind watch was in effect for all of Thanksgiving Day in New York City, and the National Weather Service in New Jersey issued a high wind watch for the coastal strip from late Wednesday night through late Thursday night. The Weather Prediction Center said power outages and tree damage are possible as wind gusts of 50-plus miles per hour are forecast along the coast, and the concern is not abstract: in a storm like this, trees come down on power lines, on cars and on houses, and the outages that follow can last for days, in cold weather, over a holiday weekend when repair crews are themselves stretched thin.

The flooding is the longer-lasting danger. The National Weather Service in New Jersey said the highest high tide is forecast for Thursday morning, November 26, with widespread moderate to major coastal flooding expected along the Atlantic coasts of New Jersey and Delaware, and Delaware Bay, and that widespread minor to moderate tidal flooding is expected more broadly. High tides will result in seawater flooding low-lying infrastructure, numerous road closures and coastal erosion, primarily on November 25 and Thanksgiving Day, according to USA TODAY's reporting on the storm, and the Weather Prediction Center said significant flooding and beach erosion are expected from Georgia and the Carolinas up through the Northeast. Beach erosion is not a minor matter: in a storm that pushes the sea over dunes and sea walls, the damage to beaches, boardwalks and coastal roads can take months to repair, and the flooding of low-lying streets can strand residents and cut off neighbourhoods. The storm surge, combined with heavy rain falling on ground that is already saturated, means that the water has nowhere to go — and in the low-lying coastal communities of New Jersey, Delaware and the Carolinas, that is a familiar and frightening combination.

There is also the question of what comes after the storm. The parade will go ahead on Saturday, at 8:30 a.m. ET, according to the retailer's announcement — but Saturday's weather, the state of the route, and the condition of the balloons after two days of storm will all matter, and the parade's organisers will be watching the forecast as closely as anyone. The storm itself will move on, but its effects — the flooding, the erosion, the power outages, the travel disruption — will linger into the weekend, and for the millions of Americans who were planning to travel home for Thanksgiving, the storm has already changed the holiday. Some will have left early, some will be delayed, and some will not travel at all. The storm is a reminder, delivered on the busiest travel week of the year, that the weather does not respect the calendar — and that even a 100-year-old tradition, one that has survived wars, recessions and a pandemic, can be moved by a nor'easter.

What to watch next

The first thing to watch is the storm itself, and how closely the reality matches the forecast. The nor'easter's exact track will determine whether the worst winds hit New York City on Thursday morning, as forecast, or whether the storm slows down and the worst of it arrives after the parade would have ended. The forecast models have been consistent for days, and the decision to postpone the parade suggests that the organisers and the city no longer believe the optimistic version of the forecast. The second thing to watch is the flooding, particularly along the New Jersey and Delaware coasts, where the highest high tide on Thursday morning is expected to bring widespread moderate to major coastal flooding. The third thing to watch is the power grid, and how many customers lose power as the wind brings down trees and lines across the Mid-Atlantic and Northeast. The fourth thing to watch is the travel system, and whether the combination of the storm and the radar outage at O'Hare produces the kind of cascading delays that can strand travellers for days.

The fifth thing to watch is Saturday, and whether the 100th parade — postponed, not cancelled — can still be the celebration it was meant to be. The balloons will be there, the floats will be there, the marching bands will be there, and the record 100 inflatables will be there, if the weather allows. But the parade will not be on Thanksgiving morning, and it will not be the thing that opens the holiday for the millions of people who watch it from their living rooms. That is the real measure of what this storm has done: it has moved a tradition that has never been moved before, and it has done it on the day that tradition matters most. The storm will pass, the parade will march on Saturday, and Thanksgiving will go ahead, as it always does. But Thanksgiving 2026 will be remembered as the year the weather won — the year the nor'easter came, the balloons stayed on the ground, and the 100th Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade waited two days to happen.

The storm is still developing, and the forecast will keep changing. The National Weather Service, the Weather Prediction Center and AccuWeather will update their forecasts through the night, and the picture on Thursday morning may be better — or worse — than the one described here. What is certain is that the storm is real, that it is large, and that it is arriving at the worst possible time. Two hundred and fifty million Americans are in its path. The parade has already been moved. The rest of Thanksgiving is now a matter of watching the sky, and hoping that the forecasters are wrong about the worst of it — and right about the timing of the best of it, on Saturday morning, when the balloons finally fly.
