The Pacifica Municipal Pier in September 2026, photographed two months before part of it collapsed into the Pacific Ocean on Wednesday, November 25, 2026. Photo: E mln e via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

A section of California's Pacifica Pier collapsed into the Pacific Ocean on Wednesday morning, November 25, 2026, torn apart by enormous waves and the highest tides of the year in a dramatic scene captured on video — and watched in disbelief by the crowds who had gathered to see the surf. The 1,140-foot pier, a beloved fishing spot south of San Francisco that had stood over the ocean since 1973, gave way shortly after 11 a.m. local time, when a wave as tall as the pier's own platform smashed into its L-shaped end and sent a section of concrete tumbling into the sea. "Oh my god, it's gone. Oh my god," a bystander could be heard saying in the video, as the structure that had survived 53 years of Pacific storms finally surrendered to the ocean.

No one was on the pier when it collapsed. That, officials were quick to stress, was a matter of luck and of foresight: the city had closed the pier in June after finding cracks in its structure, and it had remained shut ever since. But the collapse was still a shock — a visible, visceral symbol of the power of the ocean, and a warning about what the combination of king tides, El Niño and a changing climate can do to the California coast. The pier was not the only casualty of the week. Up and down the coast, from Oregon to San Diego, record-high water levels flooded homes and streets, closed roads, forced evacuation warnings and left officials warning residents to stay away from the water through Thanksgiving.

The video of the collapse, shared widely on social media, shows the moment with terrible clarity. A massive wave rolls in from the Pacific, rises to the height of the pier's platform, and slams into the structure. For a moment, the pier seems to hold. Then a section of it — the L-shaped end that jutted out into the ocean — begins to give way, slowly at first, and then all at once, tumbling into the surf in a cascade of concrete and spray. The footage was captured by NBC Bay Area and shared by Colin McCarthy, who runs the popular US_Stormwatch account on X that tracks extreme weather events. Within hours, it had been seen by millions.

A pier that had been dying for months

The Pacifica Municipal Pier — formally the Rev. Herschel Harkins Memorial Pier — was first constructed in 1973, and for more than half a century it was one of the most popular fishing piers on the California coast. At 1,140 feet long (about 350 metres), it stretched far out into the Pacific, offering anglers access to deep water and visitors a panoramic view of the coastline. It was, by any measure, an icon: a concrete landmark that generations of Bay Area families had walked, fished from and photographed.

But the pier had been in trouble for some time. The city of Pacifica closed it most recently on June 4, 2026, "due to observed cracking, separation, and displacement of the concrete walkway and structural elements near the pier abutment," according to a city statement. The closure was not the first — the pier had faced multiple closures in recent years, as seawater weakened its concrete structure — but it was the most serious. The damage was structural, not cosmetic, and the city concluded that the pier was no longer safe for public use.

On November 13, less than two weeks before the collapse, the city said it had executed a contract with the Dutra Group to replace 300 boulders that had been moved from south of the pier up to the abutment, in an effort to prevent further damage. The work was a stopgap, not a solution. And earlier in the year, city officials had begun dismantling parts of the pier, including the decades-old Chit Chat Cafe, a small restaurant that had long been part of the pier's character. The pier was, in effect, being taken apart piece by piece, even before the ocean finished the job.

Experts said this week's conditions were more dangerous than normal. The pier had been weakened by years of exposure to salt water and storms, and the combination of king tides and massive surf that arrived on Wednesday was more than the damaged structure could withstand. The wave that destroyed it was, by one account, as tall as the pier's own platform — a wall of water that hit with the force of a freight train. For a structure that was already cracked and displaced, there was no chance.

What are king tides — and why are they so dangerous?

The force that destroyed the Pacifica Pier has a name that sounds almost benign: king tides. The term is a non-scientific one, used to describe the highest seasonal tides of the year — the tides that occur when the gravitational pull of the moon and the sun align to raise water levels to their annual peak. They are predictable, they happen every year, and they are, in themselves, a natural phenomenon. But this year, they arrived with reinforcements.

The king tides that hit California this week were exacerbated by the El Niño weather pattern, which warms the waters of the eastern Pacific and raises sea levels along the American west coast, and by human-fuelled climate change, which has been raising global sea levels for decades. The result was a combination of high tides and large surf that pushed water levels to record heights in several locations — and turned a predictable annual event into a dangerous one.

The numbers tell the story. On Wednesday, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) announced that the fishing community of Charleston, Oregon, had notched its highest-ever coastal water level, roughly 1.12 metres above the mean high water level. In San Francisco, king tides pushed water levels above eight feet on Tuesday, setting a November record. Sea levels were about 1.5 feet above normal in San Diego, and more than a foot higher than normal in Monterey, according to the Scripps Institution of Oceanography. And the waves themselves were enormous: the biggest, reaching 20 to 30 feet, were expected to arrive from Tuesday, November 24, from Point Conception in Santa Barbara County and northward through November 26.

The full Beaver Moon on Tuesday, November 24, contributed to the elevated tides, according to AccuWeather, and the higher-than-normal tides were expected to persist for several days around the full moon, extending coastal flooding concerns through Thanksgiving. In Northern California, the large waves were forecast to coincide with extreme high tides on Wednesday, November 25, and Thursday, November 26, while Southern California was expected to see its largest waves coincide with the high tide on Thursday, November 26 — Thanksgiving Day itself.

"Coastal flooding can occur even when it is not raining, so sunshine does not necessarily mean waterfront roads will stay dry," Alyssa Glenny, a meteorologist with AccuWeather, said in a written statement. It was a warning aimed at the millions of Americans travelling for Thanksgiving — a reminder that the danger on the coast was not rain, but water: water pushed ashore by tides and waves, flooding roads and homes in places where the sun was shining and the sky was clear.

Flooding from Oregon to San Diego

The Pacifica Pier was the most dramatic casualty of the king tides, but it was far from the only one. Up and down the California coast, and as far north as Oregon, the high water flooded communities, closed roads and forced officials to issue warnings that lasted through Thanksgiving week.

In Pacifica itself, the waves and high tides flooded beachfront homes and neighbourhoods, closing roads and creating hazardous conditions for residents. The water pushed over a seawall and into streets that had never flooded before, and officials warned the public to stay away from the water and from waterside infrastructure. The city was under a high surf warning until midnight on Wednesday night, and under a coastal flood warning until 4 p.m. on Thursday — Thanksgiving Day.

North of San Francisco, Marin County officials issued an evacuation warning for residents of beachfront properties in Stinson Beach, saying that large waves expected through the afternoon could send water and debris into roads and homes. The warning was lifted later on Wednesday, but authorities advised people to stay away from the beach as large waves continued to pound the area. High tides had already flooded several communities along the California coast, and in the Bay Area, homes and streets in the coastal Marin County cities of Mill Valley, Larkspur, Sausalito and Corte Madera were under water.

In San Francisco, Mayor Daniel Lurie announced on Wednesday that his city had made it through the worst of the week's surge, with flooding and waves up to 9 metres — nearly 30 feet — but he warned of more king tides to come. The National Weather Service issued a coastal flood warning for the Pacific Coast and the bay shorelines along San Francisco Bay and San Pablo Bay through 4 p.m. on Wednesday, and the warnings were expected to remain in effect through 4 p.m. on Thursday.

The flooding was not confined to Northern California. From Humboldt County to San Diego, coastal communities braced for flooding, beach erosion and dangerous surf. The effects of the king tides and a series of Pacific storms were expected to spread south throughout the week, potentially affecting coastal roads and beachfront properties across the state. The message from officials, repeated up and down the coast, was the same: stay away from the water.

Bay Area Representative Kevin Mullin took to X to warn residents directly. "Sad to see a portion of the Pacifica Pier collapse as El Niño and king tides pummel our coastline. This is no joke – these huge waves and tides can be extremely dangerous. Please stay safe – avoid beaches and areas expected to flood," he wrote. It was a message that captured the mood of the week: sadness at the loss of a landmark, and fear at the power of the forces that had destroyed it.

A Thanksgiving under water

The timing of the king tides could hardly have been worse. They arrived in the middle of Thanksgiving week — one of the busiest travel periods of the year in the United States, when tens of millions of Americans take to the roads and the skies to spend the holiday with family. The high water added a new hazard to a week that was already complicated by storms on the East Coast, where a nor'easter was threatening to disrupt travel for as many as 80 million people and had already forced the postponement of the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York for the first time in its 100-year history.

On the West Coast, the danger was different but no less real. The king tides did not need rain to cause damage. They pushed water ashore on sunny days, flooding roads that drivers assumed were safe, and they created hazardous beach conditions that caught visitors by surprise. The National Weather Service's coastal flood warning for the San Francisco Bay Area was in effect through Thursday afternoon, and officials warned that the high tides would continue to pose a risk through the holiday.

For the residents of Pacifica, the Thanksgiving holiday will be marked by the loss of a landmark. The pier was more than a fishing spot: it was a part of the city's identity, a place where generations of families had spent summer days and where the community had gathered to watch the sunset over the Pacific. Its collapse, captured on video and seen around the world, is a reminder of how quickly a familiar landscape can change — and of how vulnerable coastal communities are to the forces of the ocean.

What the collapse tells us about the California coast

The destruction of the Pacifica Pier is, in one sense, a local story: a damaged pier, in a small city south of San Francisco, finally giving way to the sea. But it is also a story about something much larger — about the future of the California coast, and about the forces that are reshaping it.

The pier was built in 1973, in an era when sea levels were lower, storms were less intense and the idea of a concrete structure standing in the Pacific for half a century seemed reasonable. Fifty-three years later, the ocean has changed. Sea levels have risen, driven by human-fuelled climate change. El Niño events, which raise water levels along the west coast, have become more intense. And the king tides — the highest tides of the year — are now higher than they used to be, because they are riding on top of a higher baseline sea level.

The result is a coast that is increasingly vulnerable. Piers, seawalls, roads and homes that were built for the ocean of the 1970s are now facing the ocean of the 2020s — and the ocean is winning. The Pacifica Pier is not the first piece of California coastal infrastructure to be destroyed by the sea, and it will not be the last. The question facing coastal communities is not whether the ocean will continue to rise and the storms to intensify, but how they will adapt: which structures to reinforce, which to abandon, and how to protect the people who live and work along the shore.

For Pacifica, the immediate question is what happens to the pier now. The city had already closed it, and had begun dismantling parts of it. The collapse has, in effect, made the decision for them: the L-shaped end of the pier is gone, and the remaining structure will need to be assessed before any decision is made about its future. Some residents will hope for a rebuild. Others will argue that the ocean has spoken, and that the money would be better spent on protecting homes and roads. Either way, the pier that stood for 53 years is gone — and the video of its final moments will be watched for years to come, as a record of the day the Pacific took back a piece of the California coast.

Staying safe as the tides continue

The danger has not passed. The king tides are expected to continue through Thursday, with the largest waves in Southern California coinciding with the high tide on Thanksgiving Day. The National Weather Service has warned that rising tides will bring water and waves farther up the beach than normal, restricting escape from beaches and coastal areas, and officials across the state have urged residents and visitors to stay away from the water.

The advice is simple, but it bears repeating: do not walk on piers, seawalls or beaches during high surf and king tides. Do not drive through flooded roads. Do not assume that sunshine means safety. The water that destroyed the Pacifica Pier did not need a storm to do its work — it needed only a high tide, a big wave and a structure that was already weakened. The coast is beautiful, and it is dangerous, and this week it has shown both faces at once.

The Pacifica Pier survived 53 years of Pacific storms. It survived earthquakes, El Niños and decades of salt water eating at its concrete. What it could not survive was a week in which the highest tides of the year met the largest waves of the season, on top of a sea level that is higher than it has ever been. The video of its collapse is a spectacle — but it is also a warning. The ocean is rising. The tides are getting higher. And the coast, as the people of Pacifica learned on Wednesday morning, is not as solid as it looks.

California's coast has been warning us for years

The Pacifica Pier is not the first piece of California's coastline to be claimed by the ocean, and the king tides of November 2026 are not the first to cause damage. The state's 840 miles of coastline have been battered by storms, tides and rising seas for decades, and the damage has been accelerating. Seawalls have crumbled, cliffs have collapsed, roads have been undermined and homes have fallen into the sea — from the cliffs of Big Sur to the beaches of Southern California. What makes this week's events different is not that they happened, but how clearly they were captured: a video of a pier collapsing, in broad daylight, in front of a crowd, shared around the world within hours.

Pacifica itself has been on the front line of coastal erosion for years. The city sits on a stretch of coast south of San Francisco where the land meets the Pacific with little protection, and where the combination of high tides, big surf and rising sea levels has been eating away at the shoreline. The pier's repeated closures in recent years were a symptom of that erosion — the seawater weakening the concrete, the cracks appearing in the walkway, the displacement of the structural elements near the abutment. The city knew the pier was in trouble. It closed it in June, it hired the Dutra Group to replace the boulders at the abutment, and it began dismantling parts of the structure. But the ocean moved faster than the repairs.

The king tides are a reminder that the threat to the coast is not only about storms. The highest tides of the year arrive on a predictable schedule, driven by the alignment of the moon and the sun, and they have always been part of life on the California coast. What has changed is the baseline: sea levels are higher than they were when the Pacifica Pier was built in 1973, and they are higher than they were even a decade ago. A king tide that would have been a nuisance in 1973 is a flood in 2026. A wave that would have splashed harmlessly against the pier's platform in the past now reaches high enough to tear the structure apart. The tides have not changed — the ocean has risen to meet them.

That is the lesson of the Pacifica Pier, and it is a lesson that applies to every coastal community in California and beyond. The infrastructure of the coast — the piers, the seawalls, the roads, the homes — was built for a sea level that no longer exists. As the sea continues to rise, driven by human-fuelled climate change, the gap between what was built and what the ocean now demands will only grow. Some structures will be reinforced. Some will be abandoned. And some, like the Pacifica Pier, will be taken by the sea before anyone has decided what to do with them.

What happens to the pier now

The immediate future of the Pacifica Pier is uncertain. The city will need to assess the remaining structure before deciding whether any part of it can be saved, and the assessment will have to wait until the seas calm and engineers can safely inspect the damage. The L-shaped end of the pier — the section that jutted out into the deepest water, and the section that collapsed — is gone, and the debris is now in the ocean, where it will need to be dealt with. The remaining structure, including the abutment where the cracking and displacement were first observed in June, will need to be examined for further damage.

The longer-term question is whether the pier should be rebuilt at all. A rebuild would be expensive, and it would face the same forces that destroyed the original: rising seas, intensifying El Niño events and king tides that are higher every year. The city has already spent money on stopgap measures — the Dutra Group contract to replace 300 boulders, the dismantling of the Chit Chat Cafe — and the collapse has shown the limits of that approach. Some residents will argue that the pier is worth saving, that it is part of the city's identity and that it should be rebuilt stronger and higher. Others will argue that the money would be better spent on protecting homes, roads and the seawall — on adapting to the ocean rather than fighting it.

That debate will play out in the coming months, in city council meetings and in the community. For now, the people of Pacifica are left with the video — the wave, the platform, the slow collapse into the surf — and with the knowledge that a landmark they had known for 53 years is gone. The pier was built in 1973, when the ocean was lower and the future seemed more certain. It survived for more than half a century. And then, on a Wednesday morning in November, in front of a crowd that had gathered to watch the surf, it fell into the sea.
