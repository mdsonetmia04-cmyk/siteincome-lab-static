The Trump administration tried to break up OPEC. According to a Reuters report published on Friday, 27 November 2026, and based on interviews with ten people familiar with the matter, the United States encouraged Venezuela and Iraq to consider leaving the cartel earlier this year, and prevented Venezuela from paying its annual membership dues to the group. The moves targeted two of OPEC's five founding members, and they mark the most direct American challenge to the organisation that President Donald Trump has long accused of keeping oil prices artificially high.

It did not work. Both countries stayed. But the attempt itself — and the story of how it was stopped — reveals more about the new politics of oil than any price chart. A president who has spent years attacking OPEC from the outside decided, in his second term, to try to dismantle it from the inside, by peeling away its members one by one. He was stopped, in the end, not by OPEC itself, but by Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, who intervened personally to persuade the Trump administration not to press the issue further.

The report, published by Reuters from London, Baghdad and Houston, is the first full account of a campaign that was, until now, entirely secret. It describes a sustained American effort, over months, to convince two of the cartel's founding members that their future lay outside it. It describes a president who, in August, said publicly that it was up to Venezuela whether to leave OPEC — a remark that, at the time, seemed like a throwaway line, and now reads like a signal. And it describes an oil cartel that, for all its power, discovered that its greatest threat in 2026 was not a rival producer or a collapsing price, but the President of the United States.

Why Washington wanted OPEC broken

The Trump administration's motives, according to the people who spoke to Reuters, were straightforward. American officials were pushing for greater oil production and investment in both Venezuela and Iraq, and some worried that OPEC production limits could eventually constrain those ambitions. The logic is simple: OPEC exists to limit how much oil its members produce, so that prices stay high. If Venezuela and Iraq leave OPEC, they can pump as much as they want, American companies can invest without worrying about a cartel quota, and global supply rises, pushing prices down.

For a president who has made cheap petrol a central promise — and who has accused OPEC, for years, of ripping off American consumers — the appeal is obvious. Lower oil prices mean lower petrol prices, lower inflation, and a political win. Higher production in Venezuela and Iraq means more work for American oil companies, more investment, and more American influence in two countries where Washington has spent decades trying to increase its leverage. Breaking OPEC, or even weakening it by removing two members, would be a victory on every front at once.

The timing was also deliberate. In Venezuela, the United States seized President Nicolás Maduro in January 2026, in an operation that removed the man who had ruled the country for more than a decade and installed a government that Washington could work with. After the seizure, the State Department opened discussions with Venezuelan officials in Washington on whether the country should leave OPEC, five people familiar with the matter told Reuters. The message was clear: the old regime is gone, the new one owes us, and the price of our support is leaving the cartel.

In Iraq, the approach was different but the goal was the same. American officials held talks this summer with Iraqi Prime Minister Ali Faleh al-Zaidi, encouraging Iraq's departure from OPEC as American oil majors Exxon Mobil and Chevron planned major new investments in the country, two people familiar with the matter said. Iraq is OPEC's second-biggest producer, and its departure would have been a body blow to the cartel. The promise, implicit or explicit, was that American investment would follow an Iraqi exit — that the world's biggest oil companies would pour money into a country that was no longer bound by a quota.

The pressure on Venezuela went further than words. The Trump administration prevented Venezuela from paying its annual membership dues to OPEC, according to the Reuters report. It is a small detail, but a telling one: the United States was not only encouraging Venezuela to leave, it was actively blocking the country from fulfilling its obligations as a member, as if to make the membership itself impossible to sustain. OPEC, for its part, has the discretion to disregard unpaid membership fees, and is not planning to expel Venezuela over the issue, two OPEC sources told Reuters. The cartel, in other words, refused to take the bait.

The Saudi intervention

The American pressure on Venezuela went far enough that Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman intervened this summer, persuading the Trump administration not to press the issue further, two people familiar with the matter said. It is the most important sentence in the whole report, and it deserves to be read carefully.

Mohammed bin Salman is the de facto leader of OPEC, the ruler of its largest producer, and the man whose decisions set the oil price for the world. He is also one of President Trump's closest partners in the Middle East — the leader Trump has courted, defended and done business with for years. That he chose to intervene against the American president, on behalf of the cartel, tells you how seriously Saudi Arabia took the threat. This was not a diplomatic demarche or a press statement. It was a personal intervention by the most powerful man in the oil world, telling the most powerful man in the world to stop.

Why did it work? Because Saudi Arabia has something the United States needs, and both sides know it. The kingdom is the only country in the world with significant spare oil production capacity — the ability to raise or lower output by millions of barrels a day, at will. It is the swing producer, the country that decides, in the end, what oil costs. A president who wants low petrol prices cannot afford a war with the man who controls the tap. And Mohammed bin Salman knows it. His intervention was not a plea. It was a reminder of who holds the leverage.

There is also a deeper history. Saudi Arabia and the United States have been partners in oil for eighty years, since the first American oilmen arrived in the desert kingdom in the 1930s. But the partnership has always been transactional, and it has always had limits. The Saudis will sell oil, buy weapons, and invest in America. They will not, it turns out, let an American president dismantle the organisation that gives them their power. OPEC is not just a cartel to Riyadh. It is the instrument through which Saudi Arabia exercises influence over the world economy, and the crown prince was not going to let it be broken up by a president who, however friendly, did not understand what he was breaking.

The intervention worked. The Trump administration stopped pressing Venezuela to leave. In August, Trump said publicly that it was up to Venezuela whether to leave OPEC — a retreat, dressed up as indifference. The campaign against the cartel's founding members was over, at least for now.

Iraq: the minister who said no

In Iraq, the story ended differently, but with the same result. After his discussions with American officials, Prime Minister Ali Faleh al-Zaidi raised the possibility of leaving OPEC at a government meeting this summer, two people familiar with the matter said. He had been encouraged by Washington, and he brought the idea to his own cabinet. What happened next is a small masterclass in how a bureaucracy can defeat a prime minister.

Iraq's oil ministry pushed back, persuading al-Zaidi to keep Iraq in the group and consider seeking a higher production quota instead, the people said. The ministry's argument was, in essence, that Iraq did not need to leave OPEC to pump more oil — it needed to negotiate a better quota within it. Leaving would mean giving up the protection of the cartel, the relationships with other producers, and the influence that comes with being the second-biggest member, all for the sake of a freedom that Iraq could largely obtain by bargaining harder. The prime minister, who had been persuaded by Washington, was persuaded back by his own ministry. He stayed.

But the issue is not dead. The Reuters report notes that it could come up again, since some elements of Iraq's ruling coalition are favourable to leaving OPEC and OPEC+, which they view as dominated by Saudi Arabia and Russia. That is a real grievance, and it is not new. Iraq has long believed that its quota — the amount of oil it is allowed to produce — is too low for a country with its reserves and its needs. And the timing of the next test is imminent: ahead of the 29 November meeting of the cartel to assess 2027 capacity and quotas, Iraq is refusing to accept an OPEC+ assessment that says the country can sustainably produce less than 5 million barrels per day. The quota fight, in other words, is already under way, and it will be fought at the meeting that begins on Saturday.

For Exxon Mobil and Chevron, the American majors planning major new investments in Iraq, the outcome matters enormously. Their investments depend on how much oil Iraq is allowed to produce, and on whether the country remains inside a system that limits output. An Iraq outside OPEC would be a freer market for them. An Iraq inside OPEC, with a higher quota, would be the next best thing. Either way, the American companies are now part of the politics of OPEC in a way they have not been for decades — not as outsiders, but as players whose investment plans are a factor in whether a founding member stays or goes.

What the attempt reveals

The Trump administration's campaign against OPEC failed, but it revealed three things that matter far beyond the oil market. The first is that the president is willing to use American power — including the seizure of a foreign head of state, and the blocking of a country's membership dues — to pursue a goal that previous presidents treated as untouchable. OPEC has been criticised by American presidents for sixty years. No president has ever tried to dismantle it from the inside. Trump did, and he did it in his first year back in office.

The second is that the limits on American power are real, and they are set by the countries America depends on. Saudi Arabia stopped the campaign against Venezuela, not because it loves OPEC in the abstract, but because it has the power to do so. Iraq's oil ministry stopped the campaign against Iraq, not because it loves the cartel, but because it had a better argument. In both cases, the American pressure ran into a wall — and the wall held. That is a lesson that will be remembered the next time Washington decides to remake the world.

The third is that OPEC itself, for all the predictions of its demise, is still standing. The cartel has been declared dead more times than any institution in the world economy. It survived the shale revolution, the pandemic, the energy transition, and the rise of American oil production. It has now survived a direct attempt by the President of the United States to break it up. The meeting on 29 November, to set quotas for 2027, will be watched more closely than usual — not because of the numbers, which will be argued over as always, but because of what the meeting represents: a cartel that was supposed to be broken, meeting as if nothing had happened.

One American official, quoted by Reuters, offered a different version of events. The official said that it was possible lower-level United States officials had raised the question of leaving OPEC with Venezuela's government earlier in 2026, but that "it has not been among the issues raised by high-level officials". It is the kind of denial that denies nothing — a concession that the question was raised, wrapped in a claim that the people who raised it were not important enough to matter. The ten people who spoke to Reuters, and the detail of their accounts — the summer intervention, the government meeting in Baghdad, the blocked dues payment — tell a different story. The question was raised. It was raised at the highest levels. And it was raised because the president wanted it raised.

OPEC survived this time. But the attempt has changed the cartel's politics in a way that will not be undone. Venezuela and Iraq now know that Washington wants them out. Saudi Arabia now knows that it had to intervene personally to stop it. And the next time an American president decides that the oil cartel is the problem, the cartel will remember that, in 2026, the most powerful country on earth tried to break it — and failed.

OPEC: 66 years of keeping the price up

OPEC was founded in 1960, in Baghdad, by five countries — Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela — that had grown tired of watching foreign oil companies set the price of their own oil. The idea was simple: if the producers acted together, limiting how much each one pumped, they could keep the price high and keep the profits at home. It worked, spectacularly. In 1973, OPEC's Arab members cut off oil to the United States and its allies, and the price of crude quadrupled in months. The oil embargo remade the world economy, and it made OPEC the most powerful cartel in history.

The cartel has been in decline, by some measures, for years. The United States, once OPEC's biggest customer, is now the world's largest oil producer, thanks to the shale revolution. The energy transition is slowly eating into demand. And members have left before: Qatar quit in 2019, after 57 years, to focus on natural gas. Indonesia was suspended. Ecuador left in 2020. Each departure was presented as a national decision, not an American plot. But no member has ever been pushed out by the United States — and no American president has ever tried to recruit members away, one by one, in secret.

That is what makes the Trump campaign different. It was not a public argument about prices, the kind of argument Trump has made for years on social media. It was a covert operation, conducted through the State Department and through private meetings, aimed at the cartel's founding members — the countries whose membership is the source of OPEC's legitimacy. If Venezuela and Iraq had left, OPEC would not have collapsed overnight. But it would have lost two of its five founders, its second-biggest producer, and the claim that it speaks for the oil-producing world. The damage would have been existential, and the precedent — that the United States could pick off members at will — would have been worse than the departures themselves.

The cartel's defenders would say that OPEC's power was never really about the number of members. It was about Saudi Arabia's spare capacity — the millions of barrels a day that the kingdom can turn on or off at will — and about the willingness of the members to act together when it matters. On that view, losing Venezuela and Iraq would have hurt, but it would not have broken the cartel, because the cartel's real power has always been in Riyadh. The events of the summer of 2026 support that view: when the threat came, it was Riyadh that stopped it, and the man who stopped it was the crown prince. OPEC's power, in the end, is Saudi power, and Saudi power is intact.

The oil price in 2026 — and the meeting on Saturday

The campaign against OPEC unfolded against a strange backdrop in the oil market. Oil is on track for its first weekly gain this month, according to the daily briefing of 27 November, as attacks on energy infrastructure rattle traders — yet the cartel faces tough choices as a surplus builds. The paradox is the story of oil in 2026: prices are being pushed up by war and sabotage, and pushed down by a flood of new supply. The surplus is the problem that OPEC cannot solve by itself, because the surplus is being created, in large part, by countries that are not in OPEC at all — above all, the United States.

That is the deeper context of the American campaign. The United States is now the world's biggest oil producer, and it has no interest in a cartel that limits supply and keeps prices high. Every barrel that Venezuela or Iraq pumps outside OPEC is a barrel that pushes the price down, and every dollar the price falls is a dollar saved by American drivers and a point scored by an American president who promised cheap petrol. The campaign was not really about Venezuela or Iraq. It was about the price of oil, and about who gets to set it.

The next test comes on Saturday, 29 November, when OPEC meets to assess 2027 capacity and quotas. Iraq is already refusing to accept an OPEC+ assessment that says the country can sustainably produce less than 5 million barrels per day — a dispute that goes to the heart of the quota system, and that will be fought in the open, in Vienna, in front of the world's press. The meeting will set the numbers for next year. But it will also answer a question that has hung over the cartel since the Reuters report landed: is OPEC still a cartel that can discipline its members, or is it a club whose members are already looking for the exit?

The answer will not come in a single meeting. But the direction of travel is clear. Iraq wants a higher quota, and it has American oil majors standing behind it. Venezuela has a new government that owes its existence, in part, to Washington. Saudi Arabia has shown that it will intervene to protect the cartel — but it has also shown that intervention was necessary, which means the threat was real. OPEC survived 2026. Whether it survives the next attempt is a question that will be answered not in Vienna, but in Washington, in Baghdad, and in Caracas.
