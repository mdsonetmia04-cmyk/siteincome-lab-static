TITLE: South Africa's Top Court Just Saved Ramaphosa — Impeachment Report Thrown Out After 4 Years LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A South African court set aside the 2022 Phala Phala impeachment report in full, ending the inquiry into President Cyril Ramaphosa after four years ---BODY---

President Cyril Ramaphosa of South Africa. The Western Cape High Court has set aside the report that threatened him with impeachment, ending a saga that has shadowed his presidency for more than four years. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0.

President Cyril Ramaphosa has survived the biggest threat of his political career. On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Western Cape High Court in Cape Town declared unlawful — in its entirety — the independent panel report that found he may have committed a serious violation of the law over the theft of foreign currency from his Phala Phala game farm. The unanimous judgment by a full bench of three judges set aside not only the report itself but every step Parliament had taken on the basis of it, bringing the impeachment proceedings against the president to an end and closing, at least for now, a scandal that has cast a shadow over South African politics for more than four years.

The ruling was the most significant legal victory of Ramaphosa's presidency, and it arrived at a moment of acute vulnerability for him and for his party, the African National Congress. The impeachment process it killed would have left his fate in the hands of politicians, including opponents who wanted him removed from office. Instead, the judges found that the panel which produced the report had gone beyond its lawful remit, asked itself the wrong questions and made several material errors of law. The report, the court said, could not stand — and with it, the entire impeachment process fell away.

But the judgment is not the end of the story. The court did not decide whether Ramaphosa was guilty of the allegations against him. It decided only that the procedure followed by the panel was unlawful. The ruling can be appealed, and if a higher court takes a different view, the report — and the impeachment process built on it — could be revived. For now, though, the president has won the battle that mattered most, and the political reprieve it gives him is real, even if the scandal itself is far from over.

The judgment: a report that "cannot stand"

The judgment was delivered by a full bench of the Western Cape High Court, consisting of Judge President Nolwazi Mabindla-Boqwana and Judges Mark Sher and Ncumisa Mayosi. It was unanimous. Reading a summary of the judgment in court on Wednesday, Mabindla-Boqwana said the independent panel, chaired by former Chief Justice Sandile Ngcobo, had committed several material errors of law that led to the report being declared unlawful in its entirety.

"The report of the panel cannot stand; the panel fundamentally misconstrued its functions, the nature of the inquiry it was to conduct, and the recommendation it was required to make to the speaker. It went beyond its lawful remit, asked itself the wrong questions and made several material errors of law," Mabindla-Boqwana said.

The court's reasoning went to the heart of what the panel had been asked to do. Under Parliament's rules, the panel's job was to decide whether there was sufficient evidence that the president had a case to answer — a specific legal test, set out in the parliamentary impeachment rules. The court found that the panel had instead applied a different and lower test: whether there was a prima facie case to answer. That may sound like a technical distinction, but it mattered enormously. Applying the lower standard, the court found, allowed the panel to find that the president had to answer to an impeachment committee on charges which it would otherwise have found, if properly weighed on all the available evidence, to not be sufficiently established.

"Applying 'a prima facie case to answer' standard allowed the panel to find that the President had to answer to an impeachment committee on charges with [which] it would otherwise have found, if properly weighed on all the available evidence, to not be sufficiently established," the judgment said. In other words, the panel's approach allowed it to ignore or gloss over the deficiencies of what was before it — and that, the court found, was unlawful.

The court also found that the errors made by the panel led to the parliamentary impeachment process, which was reliant on the report, also falling away. "As a result, the process it engaged in and the conclusions it came to were not rationally related to the object of Rule [parliament rule], or the power and duty it was required to exercise in terms thereof, the court found that the report could not stand," Mabindla-Boqwana said. The court declared the report unlawful and set it aside in its entirety. "Any steps taken by the National Assembly pursuant to the report are reviewed, declared unlawful, and set aside," she said.

The judgment put a stop to the work of the impeachment committee chaired by Makashule Gana, which had been set up to probe the veracity of the charges against the president and whether their seriousness amounted to serious misconduct — the grounds the Constitution provides for the removal of a president. Following the court judgment, Gana said the committee was seeking legal advice from Parliament's legal service. National Assembly Speaker Thoko Didiza could decide simply to issue a statement saying Parliament has disestablished the impeachment committee in line with the judgment. As the judgment stands, the president will no longer have to appear before the much-anticipated inquiry, which would have left his fate in office decided by politicians.

Crucially, the high court, in its judgment, did not decide whether the president was guilty of the allegations of misconduct, but rather whether the procedure followed by the panel in considering the case was in line with the law. That distinction is central to understanding what the ruling does and does not mean. It is a victory on process, not a finding on the facts. Ramaphosa's lawyers had argued that the panel had based its recommendation on hearsay, had not considered his submissions, and had failed to show he acted in bad faith, as was constitutionally required. The court, in effect, found in favour of those arguments — without ever ruling on whether the underlying allegations were true.

The robbery that started everything: 9 February 2020

To understand why this judgment matters so much, it is necessary to go back to the beginning — to a burglary at a game farm in Limpopo, in the early hours of a Sunday morning more than six years ago, that nobody outside a small circle of people knew about for more than two years.

Phala Phala is President Cyril Ramaphosa's private game farm near Bela-Bela in the Waterberg region of Limpopo province. The farm breeds and sells game, including buffalo, and it is also where Ramaphosa keeps a private residence. Nothing about the farm was public knowledge in political terms until June 2022 — more than two years after the robbery that started everything.

On the night of February 9, 2020, at around 10 p.m., burglars broke into the president's house on the farm. They had first broken into a neighbouring property that evening, stealing only cushions, before hitting Phala Phala. The cash was hidden in a sofa in a spare bedroom of the main house. The farm's acting manager, Sylvester Ndlovu, later testified that he had first put the money in a safe, then moved it to the main house because it had cameras, lined it on top of the sofa cushions, replaced the cushions and placed Ramaphosa's golf bags on the couch to hide it further.

The amount of money stolen is at the heart of the scandal, and it has been disputed from the start. Ramaphosa says the cash was 580,000 US dollars, paid by a Sudanese businessman, Hazim Mustafa, on December 25, 2019, to Ndlovu for 20 buffaloes — 17 females and three males at 400,000 rand each, a total of 8 million rand. Mustafa wanted to pay cash because he had the 580,000 dollars on him. Ndlovu gave him an acknowledgment of receipt, not an invoice, put the money in the safe, and told Ramaphosa about it on December 26, when Ramaphosa stopped at the farm on his way to Cape Town. The court accepted evidence from the farm's former acting manager, who testified that a buyer paid him 580,000 dollars in cash for the buffaloes on December 25, 2019. The manager said Ramaphosa instructed him the following day to wait for the general manager before dealing with the money. He later moved the cash from a safe to a sofa in the president's house because too many people had access to the safe.

Arthur Fraser, the former head of the State Security Agency and a close ally of former President Jacob Zuma, alleged something very different: that around 4 million US dollars — equivalent to 60 million rand — of undeclared foreign currency had been stolen from the farm. That figure, if true, would have suggested a far larger and more suspicious sum of cash sitting undeclared at the president's private residence. Subsequent reports, and the criminal trial that followed, indicated that the amount stolen was 580,000 dollars — Ramaphosa's figure, and the figure the courts, the trial, the Reserve Bank and most reporting now use. At the time it was worth roughly 8 million to 8.75 million rand.

Ramaphosa reported the theft to Major-General Wally Rhoode, the head of the Presidential Protection Service, instead of to a police station. The theft stayed secret for more than two years.

How a burglary became a constitutional crisis

The theft became public on June 1, 2022, when Arthur Fraser walked into the Rosebank police station in Johannesburg with his lawyer and lodged a criminal complaint against Ramaphosa. Fraser accused the president of defeating the ends of justice by committing breaches of the Prevention of Organised Crime Act and the Prevention of Corrupt Activities Act of 2004. His complaint stated that a total of 4 million US dollars, stashed inside a couch at Ramaphosa's home at the Phala Phala game farm in the Waterberg, Limpopo, was stolen on or about February 9, 2020. Fraser's complaint also alleged that suspects supposedly involved in the theft were kidnapped and interrogated at the farm and were paid off not to tell anyone about the incident.

Sources close to the president stated that whilst a theft did take place at the farm, the amount of money involved was less than Fraser had claimed, and the money was all legally obtained following the sale of Ankole cattle — a different account of the origin of the funds from the buffalo sale described in later evidence, but consistent on the central point that the money was the legitimate proceeds of the farm's business.

The complaint detonated in South African politics. Here was a sitting president accused of concealing a theft of foreign currency from his own farm, of failing to report a crime, of allowing suspects to be kidnapped and paid off, and of possibly laundering money. The scandal acquired a name — "Farmgate" — and it became the single most damaging allegation of Ramaphosa's presidency, a presidency that had been built, in large part, on a promise to clean up the corruption of the Zuma years.

The political process that followed was set out in the Constitution. Section 89 of the Constitution allows Parliament to remove a president on the grounds of a serious violation of the Constitution or the law, serious misconduct, or inability to perform the functions of office. Parliament's rules provide for an independent panel to assess whether there is sufficient evidence that the president has a case to answer. If the panel finds that there is, Parliament sets up an impeachment committee to investigate, and the National Assembly then votes on whether to remove the president.

In November 2022, the independent panel, chaired by former Chief Justice Sandile Ngcobo, delivered its report. It found that Ramaphosa may have committed a serious violation of the law and the Constitution — specifically, that he may have breached the law requiring any person who holds a position of authority to report the theft of 100,000 rand or more to the police, and that he may have committed a serious violation of the Constitution. The panel's finding was enough, under the rules, to trigger the next stage of the process.

But Parliament, in December 2022, voted not to adopt the report. The African National Congress, which held a majority in the National Assembly, voted as a bloc against proceeding, and the impeachment process stopped there — or so it seemed. The Economic Freedom Fighters, the party that had driven the impeachment effort, challenged the vote in court, arguing that Parliament had acted unlawfully in rejecting the panel's findings.

That challenge wound its way through the courts for more than three years. Then, in May 2026, the Constitutional Court set aside the December 2022 vote and the parliamentary rule that allowed it, and ordered Parliament to set up an impeachment committee. The ruling revived the presidential impeachment process — and it did so at a moment when Ramaphosa's political position was weaker than it had been in 2022. The impeachment committee, chaired by Makashule Gana, began its work, and the prospect of the president having to appear before a parliamentary inquiry — with his fate decided by politicians, including his opponents — became real.

Ramaphosa responded by launching his own legal challenge. In May 2026, he brought a review of the Section 89 independent panel report itself, asking the court to declare it invalid. When the impeachment committee declined his request to pause proceedings, he successfully obtained an interdict in July 2026 against public impeachment hearings. Everything, in effect, was put on hold pending the outcome of the review — the review that was decided on Wednesday.

The criminal trial: two convictions, and a sentencing

While the political and constitutional drama played out in Parliament and the courts, a separate criminal case was moving through the justice system — the case against the men accused of the burglary itself. That case reached its conclusion in the weeks before the High Court judgment, and it supplied important corroboration for Ramaphosa's account of the money.

In the Modimolle Regional Court, regional magistrate Peter Manthata found Imanuwela David guilty of conspiracy to commit housebreaking with intent to steal, housebreaking with intent to steal, theft and money laundering, and found Froliana Joseph, a former Phala Phala worker, guilty of conspiracy to commit housebreaking with intent to steal. A third accused, Ndilinasho Joseph, was acquitted of all charges. The court found that David and Froliana Joseph had conspired to break into the president's house and steal the money hidden in the couch, and that David had then spent the proceeds on cars, property and luxury goods.

The evidence in the trial was detailed and, at times, extraordinary. Manthata found that David had bought a Volkswagen Golf 7 and other cars with US dollars. A salesman at a Benoni car dealership testified that David came in, asked for a Volkswagen Golf 7 and put three packs of US dollars in a drawer. The dealership's manager testified that this was on February 10, 2020, the morning after the break-in. Manthata said there were no fingerprints or usable video footage showing who broke into the president's house, but he found that cellphone records provided sufficient evidence linking David and Froliana Joseph to the robbery. "Considering the evidence in its totality, the only inference I can draw under the circumstances is that the communication between the accused one and two was about the dollar hidden under the couch at the house in the Phala Phala farm. I therefore find that the two conspired about the breaking in and a stealing of the money in the said house," he said.

Manthata said it was common cause that David travelled from Cape Town on February 7, 2020, was in Bela Bela on February 8, and that the house was broken into on February 9. He said David then had money for cars, a 500,000 rand Mercedes-Benz for his girlfriend, 250,000 rand teeth grills and property. The state's financial evidence showed that more than 9.5 million rand was deposited into David's accounts between February 3, 2020, and May 31, 2022. The trial began in September 2025, with the state calling more than 20 witnesses, at least four of whom were declared hostile after their evidence in court departed from their police statements.

The two people convicted in connection with the theft of 580,000 dollars from the farm were due to be sentenced in the Modimolle Regional Court, with pre-sentencing proceedings expected to continue in November 2026. Their convictions mattered for the political story as well as the criminal one: they established, in a court of law, that the amount stolen was 580,000 dollars, not the 4 million dollars alleged by Fraser — and that the money was, as Ramaphosa had said, the proceeds of a buffalo sale.

What the judgment means — and what it does not

The immediate consequence of Wednesday's judgment is clear: the impeachment committee has ceased to exist, and the impeachment process against the president is at an end — unless the judgment is appealed. The ruling hands Ramaphosa a political reprieve by removing the finding that had exposed him to possible impeachment at a vulnerable moment for him and for the African National Congress. As one South African newspaper put it, he has dodged the impeachment bullet, and the judgment marks the biggest blow to Parliament's impeachment process since the process began.

But the judgment's limits are just as important as its consequences. The High Court did not decide whether the president was guilty of the allegations of misconduct. It decided only that the panel's process was unlawful. The full reasons for the judgment are expected, but the court did not say when they would be released. And the ruling can be appealed — by the Economic Freedom Fighters, by the African Transformation Movement, whose leader Vuyolwethu Zungula was at the Western Cape High Court on November 25, or by Parliament itself. If an appeal succeeds, the report could be upheld, and the impeachment process could be revived.

Even without an appeal, the political damage of the saga is not undone by a court ruling. Wednesday's judgment has provided legal clarity that the Section 89 independent panel report was unlawful, although that may change on appeal. Yet the ruling could never deliver political clarity in a saga that has cast a shadow over Ramaphosa for more than four years. The questions that the scandal raised — about the cash in the sofa, about why the theft was not reported to the police, about who knew what and when — have been answered in part by the criminal trial and by the investigations that cleared the president, but they have not been answered to everyone's satisfaction, and they will continue to be asked by his opponents.

Ramaphosa's political troubles, in other words, linger even after his legal victory. The ANC, his party, is in a weaker position than it has ever been: it lost its outright majority in the 2024 election and now governs in a coalition, the Government of National Unity, in which its partners include parties that have been sharply critical of the president. Within the ANC itself, the Phala Phala scandal has been used by rival factions as a weapon against him. A court ruling that the impeachment process was unlawful does not remove those factions, and it does not restore the authority that the scandal cost him.

There is also the question of what the judgment means for the impeachment process itself. The court's finding that the panel fundamentally misconceived its functions raises serious questions about how Parliament designed and operated the Section 89 process — and about whether the process, as it stands, is capable of fairly assessing a president's conduct. The Constitutional Court's May 2026 ruling had already found fault with the parliamentary rule that allowed the December 2022 vote. Wednesday's judgment adds a second layer of criticism, this time directed at the panel itself. Together, the two rulings suggest that South Africa's mechanism for holding a president to account through Parliament is in need of fundamental reform — a reform that Parliament has so far shown little appetite for undertaking.

A presidency defined by survival

Cyril Ramaphosa became president of South Africa in February 2018, promising to root out the corruption of the Jacob Zuma years and to restore the integrity of the country's institutions. He was, in many ways, the anti-Zuma: a former trade union leader and businessman, a veteran of the anti-apartheid struggle, a man whose reputation was built on negotiation and institution-building rather than patronage and plunder. The Phala Phala scandal, when it broke in June 2022, struck directly at that reputation. It was not just a burglary at a farm. It was an allegation that the man who had promised to clean up South Africa had himself concealed a crime, hidden cash in a sofa, and failed to report a theft to the police.

That the allegation came from Arthur Fraser, a close ally of Zuma, was not lost on anyone. The scandal was, from the start, entangled with the factional war inside the ANC between Ramaphosa's supporters and the supporters of his predecessor. Fraser's complaint, the panel's report, the parliamentary vote, the Constitutional Court challenge, the impeachment committee — each step in the saga was also a move in that factional war, and each was watched by a country that has grown weary of seeing its politics consumed by the personal battles of its leaders.

Ramaphosa's survival, then, is not just a legal story. It is a political one, and it is a story that will shape the rest of his presidency. He has now survived the biggest threat to his career, and he has done so in court rather than in Parliament — a fact that will not be lost on his opponents, who will argue that he was saved by a technicality rather than exonerated on the merits. His supporters will argue, with equal force, that the process against him was flawed from the start, that the panel exceeded its powers, and that the court's judgment vindicates him.

Both arguments contain a measure of truth. The court found that the panel's process was unlawful — and that is a finding about the panel, not about the president. But it is also true that the investigations that looked at the substance of the allegations — the Reserve Bank's inquiry into the foreign currency, the criminal trial that established the amount and origin of the money, the Public Protector's report — did not find that Ramaphosa had committed a crime. The legal position, as of November 25, 2026, is that the president has been cleared by the institutions that examined the facts, and that the one process that found against him has been declared unlawful.

What happens next will depend, in the first instance, on whether the judgment is appealed. If it is, the case will go to a higher court — the Supreme Court of Appeal, or the Constitutional Court — and the saga will continue for months or years more. If it is not, the impeachment process is over, and Ramaphosa will be free to serve out the remainder of his term without the threat of removal hanging over him. Either way, the judgment of November 25, 2026, will be remembered as the day the Phala Phala scandal — the burglary, the sofa, the 580,000 dollars, the four years of legal and political warfare — finally met its match in a courtroom in Cape Town, and lost.
