TITLE: Britain's Benefits System Just Went Dark — Millions Locked Out in 'Serious Cyber Incident' LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A serious cyber incident has forced Britain's Department for Work and Pensions offline, leaving millions unable to claim benefits or pensions online. ---BODY---

Pat McFadden, the Work and Pensions Secretary, in 2024. He called the incident "serious" but said regular benefit and pension payments would continue as normal. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (Open Government Licence).

Britain's benefits system has gone dark. On Thursday night, 26 November 2026, the Department for Work and Pensions — the largest government department in the United Kingdom, responsible for the money that millions of people depend on to live — took its online services offline after detecting a cyber incident in its systems. By Friday morning, people trying to sign in to claim Universal Credit, check their state pension or apply for disability support were met with a message telling them to try again later. New claims could not be made. Existing claims could not be managed. And Downing Street could not say whether the country had been attacked.

"It's a serious cyber incident. I can't say more at this stage about what caused it," said Pat McFadden, the Work and Pensions Secretary. "But we did the right thing by taking our systems down in order to protect the public, in order to protect their personal information, and we made sure that regular payments could continue unaffected."

The reassurance about payments is the one piece of good news in the story. The department has said repeatedly that regular benefit and pension payments are not affected and will be paid as normal, and that people who cannot attend appointments or update their circumstances because of the outage will not be sanctioned. But the scale of what has happened is enormous. The DWP administers the welfare state for one of the world's largest economies: Universal Credit, the state pension, Personal Independence Payments for disabled people, pension credit, child maintenance. It employs 96,890 people. It pays out hundreds of billions of pounds a year. And on Friday morning, its front door was closed.

Downing Street said an investigation was under way to establish what was behind the incident, but it could not rule out a malicious attack. The prime minister's spokesperson said it was a "live and ongoing incident" and that there was "currently no evidence of data loss", adding: "We're working as quickly as possible to establish the facts." The department is working with GCHQ's National Cyber Security Centre and the Government Cyber Coordination Centre, the body that coordinates responses to cyber threats against the government, to investigate and restore services.

What happened — and when

The incident began, as far as the public record shows, on Thursday, 26 November. Unusual activity was detected in the DWP's systems during the day, and at around 9 p.m. on Thursday evening the department took its online services down as a precaution. People began reporting problems accessing DWP services late on Thursday night, with reports on Downdetector, the website that monitors the availability of online services, peaking just before 9 a.m. on Friday morning.

On Friday morning, the DWP published a short statement on the government's website. "We have taken some DWP online services offline as a precaution after we identified a cyber incident affecting our systems," it said. "We are working closely with the National Cyber Security Centre and Government Cyber Coordination Centre to investigate and to bring services back online as quickly as possible and to minimise any disruption. Regular benefit and pension payments are not affected and will be paid as normal."

Almost all public-facing DWP services were taken down, including the Universal Credit online portals, pension credit and the Tell Us Once service — the service that allows a bereaved family to inform multiple government departments at the same time when somebody dies. The DWP's websites for people claiming pensions, Universal Credit and Personal Independence Payments were affected. Visitors were advised to try again later, and given a list of telephone contacts.

The department has not disclosed the nature of the incident. It has not said what the suspicious activity was, how it was detected, or how long the systems will be offline. The disruption is expected to last for most of the weekend, according to reports, with telephone lines being set up to help people who need to make new benefit claims. A Whitehall source said the affected systems would remain offline while they were "scrubbed", but the government did not anticipate weeks of disruption on the scale of last year's cyberattack on Marks & Spencer, which shut down the retailer's online operations for weeks and cost it hundreds of millions of pounds.

It is understood that the incident did not involve ransomware, and that no money was moved. But the government has declined to rule out a hack, and the investigation is still at an early stage. "The investigation is ongoing. I'm not going to get ahead of it," the prime minister's spokeswoman said. "There's no other evidence that a malicious actor has targeted any other Government departments."

Who is affected — and what they should do

The people affected by the outage are, by definition, the people who can least afford a disruption to their income. Universal Credit is claimed by millions of people, including working people on low incomes, people who are unemployed, and people who cannot work because of illness or disability. Personal Independence Payments support disabled people with the extra costs of living with a disability. Pension credit tops up the incomes of the poorest pensioners. The state pension is the main income of millions of retired people.

None of those payments has stopped. The DWP has been emphatic on that point, and it matters: the payment systems are separate from the online portals that have been taken down, and the money will keep flowing. What has stopped is the ability to do anything online: to make a new claim, to sign in to an existing account, to report a change of circumstances, to search for a job through the government's service. For someone who has just lost a job, or just become ill, or just had a baby, the inability to make a new claim online is a serious problem — and the telephone lines that have been set up as an alternative will be under enormous pressure.

The department has said that people who cannot attend appointments or update their circumstances because of the outage will not be sanctioned — will not lose their benefits for failing to do something the system made impossible. That is an important protection, and it is one the department has had to spell out explicitly, because the sanctions regime is one of the most feared parts of the British benefits system.

The DWP has also warned claimants to be on their guard against fraud. "We will never message you to ask for bank details or ask you to click on links," the department said in its statement, and repeated the warning on social media. Cyber incidents are routinely followed by phishing attempts — fake messages, pretending to be from the department, designed to steal bank details from people who are already anxious about their money. The warning is a standard one, but it is also a sign of how seriously the department is taking the risk to the personal information it holds.

That information is the real prize. The DWP holds the personal details of tens of millions of people: names, addresses, dates of birth, bank account details, medical information, employment histories. It is one of the largest repositories of personal data in the country. The government's statement that there is "currently no evidence of data loss" is carefully worded — it is not a guarantee that no data was taken, only that no loss has been found so far. The investigation, by the National Cyber Security Centre and the Government Cyber Coordination Centre, will have to establish whether data was accessed, and if so, whose.

A warning that came weeks ago

The incident comes weeks after the National Audit Office — the body that scrutinises government spending — warned that there was a "high" risk of the department suffering a cybersecurity breach that could disrupt its services or compromise its data. The warning, delivered in a report on the DWP's digital systems, will now be read very differently. A high risk, identified by the government's own auditors, has become a live incident, and the questions will follow: what was done about the warning, how much was spent on fixing it, and whether the department was prepared.

The DWP is not a small, obscure agency. It is the largest department in Whitehall, with nearly 97,000 staff, and it runs the systems that pay the state pension to more than 13 million people and Universal Credit to millions more. Its computer systems are among the oldest and most complex in government, built up over decades, running programmes that were written when the welfare state looked very different. Modernising them has been a project of many years, and many billions of pounds, and it is still not finished.

The incident is also the latest in a long line of cyber incidents to hit British public services and businesses. Last year, the retailer Marks & Spencer was hit by a cyberattack that shut down its online operations for weeks. The National Health Service has been hit repeatedly, most seriously by the WannaCry ransomware attack in 2017, which shut down hospitals across the country. And in the past week alone, the world's news agency Agence France-Presse was hit by a cyberattack that took its systems offline, the United States Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network suffered a breach, and hackers were reported to have broken into the US Air Force's main cloud computing platform. The DWP incident is part of a pattern — and the pattern is getting worse.

What the investigators will be looking for

The investigation, led by the National Cyber Security Centre — part of GCHQ, Britain's signals intelligence agency — and the Government Cyber Coordination Centre, will have several questions to answer. The first is what happened: was this a ransomware attack, a data theft, a state-sponsored intrusion, or something else entirely? The second is how it happened: which system was breached, and how did the attackers get in? The third is what was taken: was any personal data accessed or stolen, and if so, whose? And the fourth is how long it will take to fix: when will the services come back online, and what will it take to make them safe?

The answers will determine the political consequences. If the incident turns out to be a minor intrusion, quickly contained, with no data lost, the government will be able to present it as a success — a system that detected a threat and shut itself down before any harm was done. If it turns out to be a serious breach, with data stolen or systems damaged, the questions about the National Audit Office's warning, and about the state of the DWP's systems, will become very sharp indeed.

The attribution question — who did it — will be the most sensitive of all. Britain's cyber defences are aimed principally at hostile states, and the list of suspects in any attack on a government department is short: Russia, China, Iran, North Korea, and the criminal gangs that operate, often with state tolerance, from within their borders. The government has said nothing about attribution, and it is unlikely to do so until the investigation is much further advanced. But the timing — in the middle of a war in Ukraine, a confrontation with Iran in the Gulf, and a global competition over technology — will fuel speculation, whether or not it is justified.

There is also the question of what "taken offline as a precaution" actually means. In cybersecurity, taking a system offline is the nuclear option: it stops the attack, but it also stops the service. The fact that the DWP chose to take almost all of its public-facing services down, rather than isolating a single system, suggests that the incident was serious enough to justify the disruption — or that the department could not be sure how far the intrusion had spread. Either way, it is a measure of how seriously the department took what it found.

The weekend ahead

For the millions of people who depend on the DWP, the practical question is simple: when will the services come back? The government has not given a date, and the disruption is expected to last for most of the weekend. Telephone lines have been set up for people who need to make new claims, and the department has said its staff will support anyone who calls their usual benefit line. But the telephone system was not designed to replace the online system, and it will be tested by the volume of calls.

The department has also said that it will never message claimants to ask for bank details, or ask them to click on links — a warning that bears repeating, because the days after a cyber incident are when fraudsters are most active, and the people they target are often the most vulnerable. Anyone who receives a message claiming to be from the DWP, asking for bank details or offering to restore access, should treat it as a scam.

For the government, the weekend will be spent in the investigation: establishing what happened, scrubbing the affected systems, and bringing them back online as quickly — and as safely — as possible. McFadden's words on Friday were careful, and they will be tested in the coming days: "We did the right thing by taking our systems down in order to protect the public, in order to protect their personal information." If the investigation shows that no data was lost, he will be proved right. If it shows otherwise, the incident will become something else entirely: not a precaution, but a breach — and the largest government department in Britain will have to explain how it happened.

Britain has been here before

The DWP is not the first British institution to be brought down by a cyber incident, and it will not be the last. The most serious attack on a British public service was the WannaCry ransomware attack of May 2017, which shut down computer systems in hospitals across the National Health Service, forced the cancellation of thousands of appointments and operations, and cost the NHS an estimated £92 million. The attack, later attributed to North Korea, exploited a vulnerability in old versions of Microsoft Windows that had not been patched — a failure of basic cyber hygiene in one of the largest organisations in the world.

Since then, the attacks have kept coming. In 2023, the Electoral Commission revealed that hostile actors had accessed its systems and the personal data of tens of millions of voters. In 2024, the Ministry of Defence's payroll system was breached, exposing the details of serving military personnel. Last year, Marks & Spencer, one of Britain's best-known retailers, was hit by a cyberattack that shut down its online operations for weeks and cost it hundreds of millions of pounds. The British Library, the guardian of the nation's written heritage, was hit by a ransomware attack in 2023 that took its catalogue offline for months and cost millions to repair.

Each incident has produced the same pattern: a statement, an investigation, a promise to learn lessons, and then, months later, another incident. The National Audit Office's warning about the DWP, delivered weeks before this week's incident, is part of the same pattern — a warning given, and then overtaken by events. The question is whether this time will be different, and whether the government will treat the cybersecurity of the systems that pay the nation's benefits with the same seriousness it treats the cybersecurity of its weapons.

There is a reason the DWP is such an attractive target. It is not just the money — although the hundreds of billions of pounds that flow through its systems every year would be a prize for any criminal. It is the data. The DWP holds the personal details of almost everyone in the country who has ever claimed a benefit, drawn a pension, or reported a change of circumstances. For a hostile state, that data is intelligence. For a criminal gang, it is the raw material of fraud on an industrial scale. And for the people whose data it is, a breach is not an abstract risk: it is the possibility that their bank details, their medical records, and their home addresses end up in the hands of strangers.

What to do if you need the DWP this weekend

For anyone who needs to contact the DWP while the online services are down, the department's advice is to call their usual benefit line, where staff will support them. All benefit helpline numbers can be found on GOV.UK, the government's website, which has not been affected by the incident. New claims can be started by telephone, and existing claimants can report changes of circumstances the same way — although waiting times are likely to be long, given the volume of people who would normally use the online service.

There are three things every claimant should remember. First, your payments will continue as normal — the money is not affected, even though the website is. Second, you will not be sanctioned for failing to do something online that the outage made impossible — the department has said so explicitly. And third, the DWP will never message you to ask for your bank details, or ask you to click on a link. Any message that does so is a scam, and it should be deleted, not answered. If you are unsure whether a message is genuine, call the DWP on a number you already know, not on a number given in the message.

For pensioners, the position is simpler: the state pension is paid automatically, and the outage does not affect it. For people making a new claim for Universal Credit or Personal Independence Payments, the position is harder: the claim cannot be made online, and the telephone route will be slow. The department has said it is working to bring services back online as quickly as possible, and that it will update the public as the investigation progresses. Until then, the telephone is the only way in.

What this means for Britain's digital state

The DWP incident is a reminder of a fact that governments prefer not to dwell on: the modern state runs on computers, and computers can be attacked. The welfare state, the health service, the tax system, the courts — all of them depend on digital systems that were built over decades, that hold the personal data of millions of people, and that are under constant attack from criminals and hostile states. The DWP spends hundreds of billions of pounds a year, and it does so through systems that, as the National Audit Office warned, carry a high risk of breach.

That risk is not going away. The Government Cyber Action Plan, backed by more than £210 million, is an attempt to address it, and the National Cyber Security Centre and the Government Cyber Coordination Centre are the institutions built to respond to it. But the DWP incident shows the limits of that effort: even the largest department, with the most to lose, can be forced to shut down its services by an incident it detected only on Thursday.

The question for the coming weeks is whether this incident becomes a turning point — the moment when the government finally treats the cybersecurity of its own systems with the urgency it treats the cybersecurity of the military — or whether it becomes another incident in a lengthening list, investigated, reported on, and then forgotten until the next one. The people who depend on the DWP will not forget it. For them, the benefits system going dark is not a story about cybersecurity. It is a story about whether the state will be there when they need it — and on Friday morning, for millions of people, it was not.
