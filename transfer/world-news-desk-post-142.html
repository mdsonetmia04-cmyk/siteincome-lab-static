For seven straight days, the Earth was more than two degrees hotter than it was before the industrial age — and it has never done that before. From 18 to 24 November 2026, the global average temperature stayed above 2°C over the pre-industrial baseline every single day, the European Union's Copernicus Climate Change Service confirmed on 26 and 27 November. It is the first seven-day stretch above that line in recorded history. And on Monday 23 November, the planet set a record that may stand for years: the largest daily temperature anomaly ever measured, at 2.25°C above the average for that day between 1850 and 1900.

The numbers are stark, and they are worth reading twice. Before this November, the world had crossed the 2°C line on just six individual days in all of recorded history — two in November 2023 and four in February 2024. This month alone, ten days have crossed it. Fifteen days in total have now crossed the threshold, and two-thirds of them happened in a single month. The first day the planet ever went past 2°C was 17 November 2023. Three years later, it has done it for a full week.

"For the first time, the world was more than two degrees warmer than in the pre-industrial era for seven consecutive days," said Samantha Burgess, the strategic lead for climate at Copernicus. Her next sentence matters just as much as the first: a week of such temperatures, she stressed, does not mean that long-term global warming has crossed the 2°C threshold set under the Paris Agreement. That target is measured over decades, not days. But the fact that the caveat is needed at all — that scientists must now explain why a week above 2°C does not mean the limit is broken — tells its own story about how fast the planet is changing.

The week the record fell

The streak ran from Wednesday 18 November to Tuesday 24 November. Every day in that span, the global average surface air temperature sat more than 2°C above the corresponding daily average for the pre-industrial period of 1850 to 1900 — the baseline scientists use to measure how much humans have warmed the planet since the age of coal and steam began.

The peak came on Monday 23 November, when the gap widened to 2.25°C, the highest single-day reading on record. Three days earlier, on Friday 20 November, the anomaly had already reached 2.12°C, according to preliminary Copernicus data reported by Scientific American — itself a record at the time, and the first time the planet had ever measured more than 2.1°C above its pre-industrial average on a single day. Measured against the more recent 1991–2020 average, the 23 November temperature was 1.34°C above normal for the time of year — again, the largest positive gap on record, breaking the previous mark of 1.15°C set in November 2023 by almost two-tenths of a degree. On a planetary average, two-tenths of a degree is an enormous jump.

The heat was not confined to the global average. In South Korea, daytime highs climbed to between 15 and 21°C on 27 November, far above the seasonal norm of 8 to 15°C, according to the Korea Meteorological Administration, as the country experienced summer-like warmth at the start of winter. In Peru and Ecuador, El Niño-driven floods have killed 34 people and affected 47,000. Across the United States, the Thanksgiving storm that knocked out power to more than 900,000 people was part of the same restless, overheated atmosphere. The global average is an abstraction; the floods, the blackouts and the unseasonable heat are what it feels like on the ground.

How rare is this, really?

To understand how extraordinary a seven-day streak above 2°C is, it helps to look at the history of the measurement. The Copernicus record of daily global temperatures stretches back decades, built from satellite observations, weather stations, ships and buoys, all combined into a single daily figure for the whole planet. In that entire record, the 2°C line had been crossed on only six days before this month — and never on two days in a row for long, let alone seven.

The first crossing came on 17 November 2023, a date that climate scientists remember the way astronomers remember a comet: the first time the daily global average had ever been measured more than 2°C above the pre-industrial baseline. Two days in that November crossed the line. In February 2024, four more days did. Then, for more than two and a half years, the line held — until this November, when it was crossed ten times in a single month, including seven days in a row.

The acceleration is the story. Six days in all of history before November 2026; ten days in one month; seven in a single week. The planet is not creeping towards the 2°C line. It is arriving at it in bursts, each burst longer and hotter than the last. Scientists expect the bursts to keep coming. Zachary Labe, a scientist at Climate Central, a climate research non-profit, told Scientific American that more days above 2°C are likely over the next couple of months, possibly through March 2027, as the current El Niño builds towards its peak — though he added that he still expects such days to be the exception rather than the rule, for now.

The word "for now" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. Every record in this story was set within the last three years. The first crossing, the first four-day run, the first week-long run, the largest single-day anomaly — all of them happened since November 2023. A planet that had never crossed 2°C in a day now crosses it for a week at a time. The question is no longer whether the line will be crossed regularly. It is when.

What is driving the heat

Two forces are behind the record week, and scientists are clear about both of them. The first is the long-term warming caused by greenhouse gases: the concentration of heat-trapping gases in the atmosphere is at a record high, the product of more than a century and a half of burning coal, oil and gas. That is the background warming that makes every record possible. The second is El Niño, the natural climate pattern in the Pacific that periodically releases vast amounts of stored ocean heat into the atmosphere — and the current El Niño is, according to Scientific American, likely the strongest in the past 1,000 years.

El Niño works like this. Normally, trade winds push warm water across the Pacific towards Asia, and cold water wells up off South America. During El Niño, the winds weaken, the warm water sloshes back east, and the ocean gives up its heat to the air. The result is a surge in global temperatures that typically lasts a year or more, peaking months after the pattern begins. The World Meteorological Organization expects the current El Niño to strengthen and peak towards the end of 2026 — which means the hottest days may still be ahead of us.

The combination is what makes this event so powerful. El Niño alone, in a cooler world, would not have pushed the planet past 2°C for a week. Greenhouse warming alone, without El Niño, would not have done it either — at least, not yet. Together, they have produced the hottest week in human history. And because the greenhouse gases are not going away, each future El Niño will start from a warmer baseline than the last, and push the planet further past the line.

There is one piece of genuinely hopeful news in the data. Recent research suggests that global emissions may have peaked in 2026 — that the world may finally have reached the top of the curve, and that the amount of greenhouse gas humanity pumps into the sky each year may now start to fall. If that turns out to be true, it would be a turning point of historic importance. But a peak in emissions is not a fall in temperatures. The gases already in the air will keep warming the planet for decades, and the records will keep coming while they do.

What this does not mean — and what it does

The most important sentence in this story may be the one Samantha Burgess has had to repeat all week: a week above 2°C does not mean the Paris Agreement's 2°C limit has been breached. The agreement, signed by nearly every country on Earth in 2015, aims to hold long-term global warming to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C. That target is measured over decades — typically a twenty- or thirty-year average — not over a single week, or a single month, or even a single year.

The distinction matters, and it is worth understanding properly. A daily temperature anomaly is a snapshot: it compares one day's global average with the average for that same day of the year in the pre-industrial period. It is affected by the weather — by El Niño, by the seasons, by where the storms happen to be. A long-term average smooths all of that out and asks a different question: how much warmer has the planet become, on average, over a generation? By that measure, the world is currently around 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels, not 2°C. The week above 2°C is a spike, not a new normal — yet.

But the word "yet" matters too. The spike is a preview of the future, and the preview is arriving faster than anyone expected. The world is currently on track to surpass 1.5°C of long-term warming and to overshoot that threshold as early as 2030, according to analysis published alongside the record. That is four years away. The 2°C line, which the Paris Agreement treats as the absolute ceiling — the point beyond which the risks of catastrophic climate change rise sharply — is no longer a distant prospect measured in centuries. It is a line the planet now crosses for a week at a time, in bursts, three years after it first crossed it for a single day.

Think of it this way. A single day above 2°C in 2023 was a warning. Four days in February 2024 was a louder warning. Seven days in a row in November 2026, with a record anomaly of 2.25°C, is the warning becoming a pattern. The pattern is not the same as a permanent breach of the Paris target. But it is the shape of things to come, and the shape is arriving on schedule — or ahead of it.

A planet already living with the consequences

The record week did not happen in a vacuum. It happened in a world that is already living with the consequences of a warmer planet, and the news of the same few days reads like a catalogue of them. In Peru and Ecuador, El Niño floods have killed 34 people and left 47,000 affected, as rivers burst their banks and hillsides gave way. In South Korea, winter arrived with temperatures more suited to late spring, daytime highs of 15 to 21°C against a seasonal norm of 8 to 15°C. In the United States, a Thanksgiving storm knocked out power to more than 900,000 people, flooding streets and closing roads on one of the busiest travel weekends of the year.

None of these events can be blamed on a single week of heat, and scientists are careful not to do so. But all of them are the kind of event that a warmer atmosphere makes more likely and more severe: heavier rain, because warm air holds more water; stronger storms, because there is more energy in the system; longer heatwaves, because the baseline has shifted. The global average temperature is a number. The floods in Peru, the blackouts in America and the spring-like winter in Korea are what the number means.

And then there is the timing. The record week coincided, almost to the day, with the collapse of the COP31 climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, which ended on 22 November without an overarching climate pact. Countries could not agree on language about moving away from fossil fuels, or on climate finance — the money that poorer nations need to adapt to a warming world and to build clean energy of their own. The talks had already been the subject of an earlier report on this site; their failure is now part of the same story as the record heat. The planet set its hottest week on record in the same days that the world's governments failed to agree on what to do about it.

The irony was not lost on the scientists watching the numbers come in. Climate scientists began noticing the new temperature anomalies just as the negotiations in Turkey were falling apart, according to analysis in The Conversation. The conference halls in Antalya were debating the future while the future was arriving, day by day, in the Copernicus data.

What happens next

The short-term forecast is clear enough. The El Niño that drove the record week is expected to strengthen and peak towards the end of 2026, according to the World Meteorological Organization. That means more days above 2°C are likely in the coming months — Zachary Labe of Climate Central expects them to continue possibly through March 2027 — and the records set in November may not survive the winter. This year, 2026, may well be the hottest year on record. And 2027, with El Niño still in force and greenhouse gases still accumulating, is likely to be even hotter.

The longer-term picture depends on what the world does with the fact that emissions may have peaked. If global emissions have indeed reached their highest point in 2026, as recent research suggests, then the world has turned a corner — but the corner is only the beginning of the descent. Emissions must fall, and fall fast, and keep falling for decades, before temperatures stop rising. The gases already in the atmosphere guarantee further warming; the only question is how much further, and how fast.

The Paris Agreement's 1.5°C target, which the world is on track to overshoot as early as 2030, was designed to avoid the worst consequences of warming: the loss of coral reefs, the drowning of low-lying islands, the collapse of ice sheets, the kind of heatwaves that kill tens of thousands. Overshooting it does not mean the game is over — the agreement allows for overshoot and return, if emissions fall fast enough afterwards — but every year of delay makes the return harder, and every record week makes the scale of the task clearer.

There is also a simpler, more human question: how many more record weeks will it take before the numbers stop being abstract? The first day above 2°C was a curiosity for most people. A week above 2°C is news. A month above it, or a season, would be something else entirely — a world in which the ceiling the Paris Agreement set as the limit of safety has become the weather. Nobody knows when that will happen. But the direction of travel is now unmistakable, and the speed of it is the real shock of this story: three years from the first day to the first week.

Why this week will be remembered

History is full of records that seemed important at the time and faded. This one is unlikely to fade, because it marks a threshold — not a legal threshold, and not a scientific one in the strict sense, but a psychological one. For as long as the 2°C line had never been crossed, it was possible to believe that it was far away, a problem for the next generation. The week of 18 to 24 November 2026 ended that belief. The line has now been crossed for seven days in a row, and the largest single-day anomaly ever measured — 2.25°C — is on the books.

The numbers to remember are simple. Seven days. Two point two five degrees. Ten days in one month. Six days in all of history before that. A first crossing in November 2023, a week-long crossing in November 2026. An El Niño likely the strongest in a thousand years, riding on top of record greenhouse gas concentrations. A climate conference that collapsed without a deal, in the same week the planet set its record.

None of this means the Paris Agreement has failed, or that the 2°C limit is already broken. It means the opposite: the limit is still there, still measurable, still achievable in the long-term sense — and the planet is now showing us, week by week, what happens if we miss it. The week the Earth spent more than 2°C hotter than the world before industry is a warning written in the planet's own temperature. The question, as ever, is whether anyone is listening.

One final number puts the week in perspective. The global average temperature on 23 November was 1.34°C above the 1991–2020 average for that date — the largest gap ever recorded against the modern baseline, beating the previous record of 1.15°C set in November 2023. Against the pre-industrial baseline, the gap was 2.25°C. Two baselines, two records, one day. That is what a planet in the grip of the strongest El Niño in a thousand years, heated by record greenhouse gases, looks like from space — and from the ground, it looks like floods in Peru, blackouts in America, and a Korean winter that feels like spring. The numbers are the story, and the story is still being written.
