TITLE: Argentina Just Banned Its Central Bank From Printing Money — Milei's 141-0 Vote Ends 91 Years of Peso Printing LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Argentina's Congress voted 141-0 to bar the central bank from financing the government, enshrining Milei's inflation fight in law. ---BODY---

Javier Milei, President of Argentina. Congress has voted to bar the central bank from printing money to pay the government's bills — the centrepiece of his fight against inflation, now written into law. Photo: Official presidential portrait, via Wikimedia Commons.

Argentina has just done something no country in modern history has tried quite this way. On Thursday, November 26, 2026, the country's Congress voted to make it illegal for the central bank to print money to pay the government's bills. The vote in the Chamber of Deputies was 141 to zero, with 99 abstentions — a result so lopsided that not a single deputy was willing to stand up and vote against it. Under the new law, the central bank's "primary and fundamental mission" is now, in the words of the charter itself, to preserve the value of the peso. It may no longer lend to any level of government. It may no longer finance the Treasury. The printing press that has funded Argentine governments for generations has been switched off by law.

For President Javier Milei, the libertarian economist who won the presidency in 2023 promising to take a chainsaw to the state, this is the victory he has been building toward since the day he took office. His office celebrated the vote with a statement declaring that the law puts an end to "91 years of plunder, expropriation, impunity and institutional decay" — a reference to the central bank's founding in 1935 and the nine decades of money printing that followed. It is the most radical central bank reform attempted by any major economy in living memory, and it arrives at a moment when Argentina's economy, by the government's own preliminary figures, has likely slipped back into recession.

The stakes could hardly be higher. Argentina has spent much of the last century as the world's great cautionary tale about what happens when a government pays its bills by printing money. Inflation has destroyed savings, wiped out wages and driven the country into repeated crisis. Milei was elected on a promise to end that cycle, and he did it with a programme of spending cuts so severe that his supporters call it shock therapy and his critics call it cruelty. Now he has moved to make the change permanent — not by decree, not by policy, but by rewriting the charter of the central bank itself so that no future government can quietly switch the presses back on.

The vote: 141 to zero

The numbers tell the story of how completely Milei has reshaped Argentine politics. The Chamber of Deputies voted 141 to zero in favour of the new central bank charter, with 99 deputies abstaining. Not one vote was cast against the bill. The abstentions matter — they came from the opposition benches, from deputies who would not vote for Milei's reform but would not vote against it either. In a country where the central bank has been a political football for decades, the refusal of a single deputy to defend money printing on the record is a measure of how far the argument has moved.

The bill had already passed the Senate, but not in the form Milei wanted. Senators removed language the president had pressed for, which would have required a two-thirds vote in both chambers of Congress to remove central bank governors — a provision designed to insulate the bank's leadership from political pressure. The Senate's version keeps the power to appoint and remove bank leaders in the hands of a simple majority, which means a future government with a Senate majority could still replace the bank's leadership. Milei lost that fight, and his allies made no secret of their disappointment. But the core of the bill — the ban on financing the government — survived intact, and the Chamber of Deputies gave it final approval on Thursday.

The new charter is blunt. It defines the central bank's mission as preserving the value of the peso, and it bars the bank from lending to any level of government or otherwise financing the Treasury. That sounds technical. It is not. For decades, Argentine governments have covered their deficits by borrowing from the central bank, which created the money to lend them — a polite description of printing pesos and hoping the consequences would arrive after the next election. The consequences always arrived. Inflation, which was running above 200 percent a year when Milei took office in December 2023, was the direct result of a system in which the government could always pay for things by creating money.

Milei's office framed the vote as a historical break. "This marks the end of nearly two decades of political manipulation at the Central Bank," the presidency said in a statement celebrating the Senate's passage of the bill. The longer version of the argument, in the statement that followed the deputies' vote, went further: 91 years of plunder, expropriation, impunity and institutional decay, ended in a single afternoon. The rhetoric is Milei's — grand, absolute, and deliberately provocative. The law behind it is real, and it will be very hard to undo.

From abolishing the bank to reforming it

There is an irony at the heart of this reform that Milei himself would be the first to acknowledge. As a candidate in 2023, he did not promise to reform the central bank. He promised to abolish it. His campaign symbol was a chainsaw, and the central bank was one of the first institutions he said he would cut. He promised to replace the peso with the US dollar — to dollarise the economy entirely and take the power to print money away from Argentine politicians forever.

He did not do that. Once in office, Milei discovered what every radical discovers: abolishing a central bank is easy to promise and extraordinarily difficult to do. Dollarisation would have required reserves Argentina did not have, and it would have left the country with no lender of last resort and no monetary policy of its own. Instead of abolition, Milei chose the next most radical thing available: a central bank that is legally forbidden from doing the one thing Argentine central banks have always done. The institution survives. Its power to fund the government does not.

The shift is characteristic of Milei's presidency. The chainsaw rhetoric has remained, but the governing has been more pragmatic than the campaign suggested. He cut spending savagely — slashing subsidies, freezing public works, shrinking the state — and inflation fell from triple digits to far lower levels. But he kept the peso, kept the central bank, and kept the exchange rate controls he once denounced. The new charter is the same pattern at a larger scale: the institution is preserved, but its purpose is rewritten so completely that it is, in substance, a different bank.

Whether the reform works depends on a question the law cannot answer. A central bank that cannot lend to the government is only as strong as the government that respects the law. Argentina's history is full of laws that were rewritten when they became inconvenient. The charter can be changed by a future Congress, and the bank's governors can still be replaced by a Senate majority. Milei's reform makes money printing illegal. It does not make it impossible. The difference between those two things is the difference between a law and a constitution, and Milei knows it — which is why he fought, and lost, the battle for the two-thirds removal rule.

A recession, an election, and a gamble

The timing of the vote is not an accident. Preliminary figures released in the days before the vote showed that Argentina had likely slipped into recession, less than a year before Milei seeks a second four-year term. The next presidential election is scheduled for October 24, 2027, and Milei is expected to seek re-election. A recession in the year before an election is the worst possible news for an incumbent, and it is the direct consequence of the austerity that brought inflation down. Milei cut spending to stop the printing presses, and the economy contracted as the money dried up.

That is the gamble at the heart of the Milei project, and it has always been explicit. The president's argument is that Argentina's decades of crisis were caused by the state spending money it did not have, and that the only way out was a painful contraction followed by genuine, sustainable growth. His supporters point to the fall in inflation as proof that the medicine is working. His critics point to the recession, to rising poverty, and to the human cost of the cuts, and argue that the medicine is killing the patient. Both sides are, in their way, right. Inflation has fallen dramatically from the triple-digit levels of 2023. The economy has shrunk. The question Argentine voters will answer in October 2027 is whether the trade was worth it.

The central bank vote is part of that answer. By locking the reform into law, Milei is telling voters that the change is permanent — that even if he loses, the printing press stays off. He is also telling investors the same thing, and investors matter: Argentina needs foreign capital, and foreign capital needs to believe that the peso will hold its value. A central bank charter that forbids monetary financing is the strongest signal a government can send that it is serious about price stability. Whether the signal is believed depends on whether the law survives contact with Argentine politics.

The recession complicates everything. A government that cannot borrow from its own central bank must borrow from the market, or cut spending, or raise taxes. In a recession, all three are painful. Markets charge Argentina punitive rates because of its history of default — the country has defaulted on its sovereign debt nine times, more than almost any other nation. Cutting spending further in a recession deepens the contraction. Raising taxes in a recession strangles growth. The new charter removes the easiest option, which is the entire point. But it does not remove the need to pay the bills.

The other bill: suspending the primaries

The central bank vote was not the only piece of Milei's agenda to move on Thursday. The Senate also advanced a bill to reform Argentina's elections by suspending the country's primary elections ahead of the 2027 presidential race. Unlike the central bank reform, that bill is not final: it still needs to pass the Chamber of Deputies. But its advance on the same day as the central bank vote shows how much of Milei's programme is now moving through Congress at once.

The primaries, known as the PASO, are open simultaneous primaries in which all parties choose their candidates on the same day — a system that has shaped Argentine politics for more than a decade. Suspending them would change the dynamics of the 2027 race significantly, removing a stage at which outsider candidates have historically built momentum. Critics say the suspension is self-serving, designed to smooth Milei's path to re-election. Supporters say the primaries are expensive, wasteful and unnecessary. The debate will continue in the Chamber of Deputies, where the bill's fate is not yet certain.

The two bills together show a president who is using his political capital while he has it. Milei's coalition, La Libertad Avanza, does not hold a majority in either chamber of Congress, and every major reform has required negotiation with opposition parties. The 141-0 vote on the central bank, with 99 abstentions, is the product of that negotiation — a bill that the opposition would not vote for but would not vote against. The primaries bill will be a harder test, because it touches the electoral rules under which every deputy in the chamber will fight the next election.

What the world is watching

Argentina's experiment is being watched far beyond Buenos Aires. For economists, the question is whether a country can legislate its way out of a century of monetary indiscipline. Central bank independence is a standard feature of modern economies — the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England are all insulated from direct government financing. But most of those banks were built independent from the start. Argentina is trying to do it in reverse: to take a bank that has been the government's piggy bank for 91 years and turn it into a guardian of the currency by changing its charter. No country has attempted a transformation quite this stark, and no country has done it while in recession.

For the rest of Latin America, the vote is a test of Milei's model. The Argentine president has become the most closely watched leader in the region — admired by free-market reformers across the continent, reviled by the Latin American left, and studied by governments that face their own inflation problems. If the central bank reform holds and the economy recovers, Milei's approach will be copied. If the economy stalls and a future government rewrites the charter, it will be cited as proof that radical reform cannot survive democracy.

For ordinary Argentines, the question is simpler and more urgent: will their money hold its value? The peso has been one of the world's least reliable currencies for as long as most Argentines have been alive. Salaries that lose value between paydays, savings that evaporate, prices that change between the shelf and the till — these are the lived experiences that made Milei's message resonate. The new charter promises that the institution responsible for that history will no longer be allowed to repeat it. It is a promise written into law. Whether it is kept will be decided not in Congress, but in the markets, in the supermarkets, and in the votes of October 2027.

Milei's chainsaw is still running. On Thursday it cut through the central bank's charter, and the sound of it was heard around the world.

How Argentina got here: 91 years of the printing press

To understand why a vote about a central bank charter has electrified Argentina, you have to understand what the Banco Central de la República Argentina has actually been for most of its life. Founded in 1935, the bank was created to bring order to a monetary system that had been chaotic since the Great Depression. Within a generation, it had become something else entirely: the instrument through which Argentine governments paid for promises they could not afford.

The pattern was set in the middle of the twentieth century, when governments discovered that the central bank could create pesos on demand and that the political rewards of spending arrived long before the economic consequences. The Peronist governments of the 1940s and 1950s expanded the money supply to fund industrialisation and social programmes. The military governments that followed did the same to fund their own projects. By the late 1980s the system had collapsed into hyperinflation: in 1989, prices rose by more than 3,000 percent in a single year, wiping out savings and forcing a president from office early.

The 1990s brought a radical experiment in the opposite direction. Under President Carlos Menem, Argentina pegged the peso one-to-one to the US dollar under the convertibility system, effectively abolishing its own monetary policy. The peg ended inflation, but it also ended the government's ability to respond to economic shocks, and when the system collapsed in 2001, Argentina defaulted on roughly 95 billion dollars of sovereign debt — the largest default in history at the time — and the peso lost most of its value in weeks.

The governments that followed returned to the printing press. The Kirchner administrations of the 2000s and 2010s used central bank reserves to fund spending, including a controversial 2010 decision to use reserves to pay foreign debt that led to a standoff with the bank's own president. The government of Alberto Fernández, which governed from 2019 to 2023, printed money on a vast scale during the pandemic and afterwards, and inflation climbed past 200 percent a year. Each government inherited the consequences of the last one's printing and added its own. Milei's description of "91 years of plunder" is partisan rhetoric, but the history it describes is real: for nine decades, the central bank has been the place Argentine governments went when they ran out of money.

That is what makes Thursday's vote different from an ordinary piece of legislation. Every previous reform of the bank left the essential mechanism intact: the government could always, in a crisis, ask the bank to create money. The new charter removes that option. It is the first time in the bank's 91-year history that the law has forbidden the thing the bank was most often used for.

The IMF, the dollar and the world beyond Buenos Aires

Argentina's reform does not happen in a vacuum. The country is operating under a 20 billion dollar programme with the International Monetary Fund, agreed in April 2025, which requires the government to rebuild reserves and maintain fiscal discipline. The IMF has been Argentina's most important external creditor for decades, and its programmes have always included conditions on central bank financing. What Milei has done is to go further than the IMF asked — to write the prohibition into domestic law rather than merely accepting it as a programme condition.

The United States has also backed Milei's experiment directly. The US Treasury extended a 20 billion dollar currency swap line to Argentina in 2025, a show of support from Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent that was widely seen as an endorsement of Milei's programme by the Trump administration. The swap line gave Argentina's central bank access to dollars at a moment when its own reserves were under strain, and it signalled to markets that Washington regarded Milei's reforms as worth protecting. Thursday's vote strengthens that signal: a central bank that cannot print money is a central bank whose currency is more credible, and credibility is the currency in which Argentina trades with the rest of the world.

But the reform also creates a tension that will define the next year. Argentina's government still needs to finance itself, and with the central bank closed as a source of funds, it must borrow from the market at rates that reflect nine decades of default risk — or cut spending further, or raise taxes. In a recession, none of those options is painless. The IMF programme provides a cushion, and the US swap line provides a backstop, but neither can substitute for the basic arithmetic of a government that must live within its means. Milei has removed the escape hatch. Now he has to prove that the house can stand without it.

What to watch

The first test is the Senate's version of the charter in practice: whether the bank's leadership, removable by a simple Senate majority, can resist political pressure when the next budget is tight. The second is the economy itself — whether Argentina climbs out of recession before the October 2027 election, or whether the contraction deepens and turns voters against the reform. The third is the primaries bill in the Chamber of Deputies, which will show whether Milei's 141-0 moment was a one-off or the start of a legislative run. And the fourth is the peso: if the currency holds its value through 2027, the law will have done its job. If it does not, no charter in the world will save it.
