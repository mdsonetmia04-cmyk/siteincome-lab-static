Taraneh Rahimi is 21 years old, and she cannot walk properly. In recent weeks, her legs have been beaten so badly in prison that she struggles to stand, and an ear infection caused by an untreated head injury has affected her balance. Her cousin, who has spoken to the BBC, says she loves horses and would like to set up her own breeding centre one day. Instead, she is on death row in Iran, sentenced to die for taking part in a protest.

Taraneh is one of at least nine women sentenced to death in Iran this year, according to Mai Sato, the United Nations Special Rapporteur on human rights in Iran. Six of them were sentenced in connection with the anti-government protests that swept the country in January. Three more face execution on other charges, including blasphemy and espionage. The numbers, reported by BBC Persian in an investigation broadcast on 26 November 2026, are the highest in decades. In the entire decade before 2025, the number of women sentenced to death in Iran in any single year never exceeded three. This year, it is nine — and the year is not over.

The figure for men is much higher. But the sentencing of women protesters and dissidents to death in these numbers is new, and it is deliberate. Amnesty International's Iran researcher, Raha Bahreini, told the BBC that the number of women facing death sentences had reached levels not seen in decades, and described a growing pattern of politically motivated executions. The Iranian embassy in London told the BBC that claims of an increase in the use of the death penalty in protest-related cases were misleading. The evidence, gathered by the United Nations, by human rights monitors and by the families themselves, says otherwise.

The twins from Isfahan

Taraneh Rahimi and her twin sister Romina were arrested in late January, two weeks after the end of the protests that began in late December over economic grievances and grew to become Iran's largest against the clerical establishment since the 1979 Islamic Revolution. They had joined the demonstrations in the Isfahan region, where one member of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps was killed. Their cousin says the family did not find out where the sisters were being held for more than three months.

On 31 August, Taraneh was sentenced to death. Court documents have not been published, but the BBC has seen part of a sentencing document which says she was convicted of moharebeh — waging war against God — a capital offence under Iran's Islamic Penal Code. Romina was sentenced to 25 years in jail for the lesser charge of aiding and abetting moharebeh, according to Sato, the United Nations rapporteur. Sato says the sisters were part of a case in which ten people were sentenced to death, but that there was more to the story than the verdicts suggest.

According to sources familiar with the case, the twins' lawyers have submitted a complaint alleging that the sisters were tortured and sexually abused during interrogation — and that the investigation into that complaint is being overseen by an official they accuse of carrying out the torture. It is a detail that captures, in miniature, the system the women are caught in: the people who investigate the abuse are the people accused of committing it, and the courts that are supposed to protect the accused are the ones sending them to the gallows.

The physical evidence of what happened to Taraneh is visible in her own body. Her legs were beaten until she could not walk properly. A head injury, left untreated, became an ear infection that destroyed her balance. She is 21 years old. She loves horses. She wanted to breed them. The Iranian state has decided that she should die for marching in the street.

The other women on death row

Taraneh Rahimi is the face of the story because her family has spoken, and because the BBC has documented her case in detail. But she is not alone. The other women facing execution include a civil engineer, a young woman condemned for a social media post, and a 27-year-old convicted on charges that human rights groups say are a pretext for punishing dissent.

Mahboubeh Shabani, 33, a civil engineer, was sentenced to death in September on espionage charges after helping transport wounded protesters. Her photograph has been shared with human rights groups, and voice messages from her and other women on death row contain a mixture of desperation and defiance. She is not accused of carrying a weapon, or of attacking anyone. She is accused of helping the injured — of doing, in the middle of a crackdown, what a decent person does when people are bleeding in the street.

Najmeh Amini, 23, was condemned to death for blasphemy over content she posted on social media. A post — words on a screen — is the whole of her offence, and the sentence is death. Mozhdeh Hashemi Bazargani, 27, is currently on death row following convictions for espionage and moharebeh. The charges, in each case, are the ones the Iranian judiciary reaches for when it wants to kill a dissident and call it law: waging war against God, corruption on earth, espionage, blasphemy. They are charges that require no evidence of violence, no victim, no weapon — only the state's say-so that the accused is an enemy.

The scale of the crackdown extends far beyond these nine women. According to figures cited in the BBC report, at least 39 people have already been executed in connection with the January protests, based on United Nations data. The United States-based Human Rights Activists News Agency, known as HRANA, says it has identified more than 7,000 people killed in the crackdown that followed the protests, most of them protesters, but believes the true figure is much higher. HRANA also reported that 2,003 people were executed in Iran in the year up to October 2026, including 47 women. That is a country executing, on average, more than five people every single day.

Norway-based Iran Human Rights, in an urgent assessment on 16 November, confirmed that at least 121 demonstrators from the January uprising — including six women and five juvenile offenders — currently face death sentences across 15 provinces. The same monitors recorded 691 executions in the first ten months of 2026 alone. The numbers are so large that they risk becoming abstract. They are not abstract. Each one is a person, with a family, with a name, with a last meal and a last night. Taraneh Rahimi is one of them. There are 120 more, at least, waiting behind her.

The protests that changed everything

To understand why Iran is executing women in numbers not seen for decades, it is necessary to understand what happened in January. The protests began in late December over economic grievances — the price of bread, the collapse of the currency, the daily humiliation of an economy that no longer works for ordinary people — and spread to become the largest anti-government uprising since the 1979 Islamic Revolution. They were not a single demonstration in a single city. They were a national revolt, in dozens of cities, in every province, by people who had decided that the system could not be reformed and had to go.

The government answered with force on a scale that shocked even seasoned observers of Iran. HRANA's count of more than 7,000 killed makes the January crackdown one of the deadliest episodes of state violence in the country's modern history. The dead were mostly protesters — young people, workers, students, mothers and fathers — killed by security forces in the streets. The wounded were carried to hospitals by people like Mahboubeh Shabani, who is now on death row for doing so. The arrested were held incommunicado for months, tortured, and tried in courts where the verdict was written before the hearing began.

The Iranian government accuses the United States and Israel of fomenting the unrest, blaming foreign hands for a revolt that began over the price of bread. It is a claim the government has made after every protest for forty-seven years, and it has never been true. The January protests were Iranian, and they were about Iran: about an economy in ruins, about a generation with no future, about women who have spent their lives being told what to wear, where to go and what to say, and who decided, in January, that they had had enough.

That is why the death sentences for women matter so much, and why they are not an accident. The women of Iran have been at the forefront of every protest movement in the country's recent history, from the Woman, Life, Freedom uprising of 2022 to the January revolt of 2026. They are the ones the regime fears most, because they are the ones who have the least to lose and the most to gain. Executing them is not a judicial act. It is a message, sent in the cruellest language the state knows: if you march, we will kill you, and we will call it the law.

The world watches — and waits

The BBC Persian investigation, broadcast on 26 November, is the most detailed account yet of what is happening to the women on Iran's death row. It is also a test of the world's response. The United Nations rapporteur has documented the sentences. Amnesty International has described the pattern. The families have spoken, at enormous personal risk. The question is what happens next — whether the international community treats nine death sentences for women as the emergency they are, or whether they join the long list of Iranian atrocities that the world has noted, condemned and then forgotten.

There are precedents for pressure working, and precedents for it failing. International attention has, in the past, delayed executions in Iran, and in some cases stopped them altogether. But it has never stopped the machine. The execution rate — 2,003 people in ten months, 47 of them women — is the highest in years, and it is rising, not falling. The regime has calculated that the cost of killing its own people is lower than the cost of letting them live and protest. Every execution that passes without consequence confirms that calculation.

The women on death row are not waiting for the world to save them. Their voice messages, as reported by the BBC, contain desperation and defiance in equal measure. They are appealing to the international community because there is no one else left to appeal to. Their lawyers have complained of torture, and the complaints are investigated by the torturers. Their families waited three months to learn where they were held. Their trials were held behind closed doors, on charges that require no proof of violence. There is no court left in Iran that will save them. There is only the world outside.

Taraneh Rahimi loves horses and wanted to breed them. She is 21 years old, and she can barely walk. She was beaten in prison, and she is going to be executed for marching in the street. That is the story. It is not complicated. It is not ambiguous. And it is happening now, while the world decides whether to notice.

The machinery of death

The death sentences handed to the nine women were not the product of a fair trial. They were the product of a system designed to produce them. In Iran, protesters are tried in Revolutionary Courts, where the judges are clerics or loyalists, the hearings are often held behind closed doors, the accused frequently have no access to a lawyer of their own choosing, and the verdict is, in many cases, written before the hearing begins. The charges — moharebeh, waging war against God; efsad-e-fel-arz, corruption on earth; espionage; blasphemy — are elastic enough to cover almost anything, and serious enough to carry the death penalty. A woman who marched in the street can be charged with waging war against God. A woman who carried a wounded protester to hospital can be charged with espionage. A woman who posted her opinion online can be charged with blasphemy. The law, in these courts, is not a shield. It is a weapon.

The confessions that support the verdicts are, in many cases, extracted by force. Human rights monitors have documented, for years, a pattern of torture in Iranian detention: beatings, sexual abuse, sleep deprivation, threats against family members, and forced confessions recorded on camera and broadcast on state television. The twins' lawyers have alleged that Taraneh and Romina Rahimi were tortured and sexually abused during interrogation. The complaint they submitted is being investigated by an official the lawyers accuse of carrying out the torture. It is a closed loop, and it is deliberate: a system that investigates itself will never find itself guilty.

The speed of the process is part of the cruelty. Taraneh Rahimi was arrested in late January and sentenced to death on 31 August — seven months from arrest to a death sentence, in a case where her family did not know where she was held for the first three months. In that time, she was beaten until she could not walk, and a head injury was left untreated until it became an infection that destroyed her balance. The physical damage is not incidental to the process. It is the process. The state breaks the body first, and then it breaks the life.

There is also the question of what the sentences are for. The January protests were, by every account, the largest since the revolution. The government's response was not to address the grievances — the economy, the corruption, the repression — but to kill the people who raised them. The death sentences are the judicial half of that response, and they serve the same purpose as the bullets in the street: to make the cost of protest so high that no one dares to try again. The women are targeted not despite being women, but because they are women — because the regime knows that the women's movement is the engine of the opposition, and because it believes that executing women will terrify the movement into silence.

The women who came before

The nine women on death row in 2026 are not the first Iranian women to face the state's violence, and they will not be the last. Their story begins, in its modern form, in September 2022, when Mahsa Amini, a 22-year-old Kurdish woman, died in the custody of the morality police after being arrested for wearing her headscarf improperly. Her death set off the Woman, Life, Freedom uprising — months of protests led by women and girls, who burned their headscarves in the street and demanded an end to the system that had killed her. The state answered with bullets, with mass arrests, and with executions. Hundreds were killed. Thousands were jailed. And the movement, though driven underground, never died.

In 2023, the European Parliament awarded the Sakharov Prize for Freedom of Thought to Mahsa Amini and the Iranian women's protest movement — a recognition that the women of Iran had become, in the eyes of the world, the face of the fight for freedom. The award changed nothing on the ground. The morality police returned. The arrests continued. The executions went on. But it established something important: the world was watching, and the women knew it.

The January 2026 protests were, in many ways, the child of the 2022 uprising. The same generation, the same grievances, the same courage — and a state that had learned, from 2022, that it needed to be more ruthless, not less. In 2022, the crackdown killed hundreds. In January 2026, it killed more than 7,000, according to HRANA's count. In 2022, the death sentences for women were rare. In 2026, there are nine. The escalation is not an accident. It is a policy, and it is getting worse.

The women on death row know this history. They know what happened to Mahsa Amini, and they know what happened to the women who marched after her. They marched anyway. That is the part of the story that the Iranian state cannot understand, and cannot defeat: the women know the price, and they pay it. Taraneh Rahimi knew that marching in Isfahan could get her arrested. She marched. Mahboubeh Shabani knew that carrying the wounded could get her charged with espionage. She carried them. Najmeh Amini knew that posting her opinion could get her charged with blasphemy. She posted it. The state calls this waging war against God. The rest of the world calls it courage.

What the numbers hide

The statistics in this story are so large that they threaten to swallow the people inside them. Two thousand and three executions in ten months. Forty-seven of them women. Seven thousand killed in the crackdown. One hundred and twenty-one demonstrators on death row across fifteen provinces. Six hundred and ninety-one executions in the first ten months of the year. Each number is accurate, each is sourced to monitors who count the dead with care, and each is, in a sense, a lie — because a number cannot convey what it means to be one of them.

Consider the number 47: the women executed in Iran in the year to October 2026, according to HRANA. Forty-seven women, each with a name, a family, a last night. Consider the number 121: the demonstrators facing death sentences, including five juvenile offenders — children, sentenced to die for protesting. Consider the number 7,000: the people killed in the January crackdown, most of them protesters, with the true figure believed to be much higher. The monitors count what they can verify. The dead who were buried in secret, the bodies that were never returned to families, the people who vanished into the prison system and never came out — those are not in the numbers. The real toll is higher, and it will never be fully known.

There is one more number that matters, and it is the smallest one in this story: three. In the entire decade before 2025, the number of women sentenced to death in Iran in any single year never exceeded three. This year, it is nine. The tripling of that number is not a statistical quirk. It is a decision — a decision made by judges, by prosecutors, by the men who run the Revolutionary Courts and the men who run the country — to use the death penalty against women as a tool of political control. The decision was made deliberately, and it can be unmade. The question is whether anyone with the power to unmake it will try.
