TITLE: The Hunger Games Just Passed $175 Million in a Week — and an $85 Million Thanksgiving Weekend Is Still to Come LABELS: Entertainment SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Sunrise on the Reaping took $15.1M on Wednesday and $11.6M on Thanksgiving Day, passing $175M domestic as it heads for an $85M holiday weekend. ---BODY---

Joseph Zada, who plays the young Haymitch Abernathy in The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, at the film's world premiere in New York. The prequel has passed $175 million at the North American box office in its first week. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC0).

The Hunger Games just ate Thanksgiving. Lionsgate's prequel, Sunrise on the Reaping, took $15.1 million on Wednesday and $11.6 million on Thanksgiving Day itself, and it is now projected to collect about $85 million across the five-day holiday stretch — a haul that would push its North American total to roughly $233 million by Sunday night, just nine days after it opened. Two brand-new studio rivals opened against it this week. Both were beaten by a distance. The turkey, the football and the shopping could wait. America went to the movies instead — and it went to see Haymitch Abernathy.

The numbers, reported by Variety, Deadline and The Numbers, tell a story of a film that has not slowed down for a single day since it arrived. It opened on 20 November with $119.3 million over its first three days, the fifth-biggest debut of the year. It set a new Tuesday record for Lionsgate and for the franchise, taking $15.98 million on 24 November. It led Wednesday with $15.1 million from 4,018 theatres, led Thanksgiving Day with $11.6 million, and by the end of Thursday its domestic running total stood at $174,931,438 — just under $175 million, according to The Numbers, which TheWrap rounded to the same figure. The five-day projection of $85 million, if it holds, would make this the franchise's second-biggest Thanksgiving performance ever, behind only 2013's Catching Fire — and it would put the film past $233 million at home before its second weekend is even over.

That is a remarkable run for a franchise that many people had written off. The last Hunger Games film, The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes, finished its entire domestic run at $166 million. Sunrise on the Reaping passed that total in its first week. It has already done so with a reported production budget of about $150 million, which means the film is now within touching distance of covering its costs from North American ticket sales alone, before a single dollar of international gross, streaming, or home video is counted. And the international numbers are strong too: the film opened to $226.1 million worldwide, and its global total stood at about $253 million after five days, according to Deadline and The Hollywood Reporter.

The Wednesday and Thursday numbers

The holiday frame runs from Wednesday, 25 November, through Sunday, 29 November, and the first two days of it already belong to the film. On Wednesday, Sunrise on the Reaping took $15.1 million — $15.15 million, according to The Numbers — from 4,018 theatres, a figure that Variety reported as the clear lead of the day. On Thanksgiving Day itself, when cinemas traditionally quieten as families sit down to dinner, it took an estimated $11.6 million, again first by a wide margin. The two days together added about $26.7 million to a domestic total that had already reached $148.1 million by the end of Tuesday, and took it to $174.9 million by Thursday night.

The Friday-to-Sunday portion of the five-day window is projected at about $58 million, according to Deadline — a drop of roughly 51 percent from the opening weekend, which is a normal second-weekend decline for a film of this size, and a healthy one for a film in its second week. Variety's $85 million five-day projection, TheWrap's matching figure and Deadline's modelling all point to the same conclusion: the film will win the holiday by a landslide, and its domestic total will be around $233 million when the weekend's studio estimates arrive on Sunday, 29 November, with the actual figures following on Monday, 30 November.

Those projections come with the usual caveats, and they are worth stating plainly. The $85 million is a forecast, built from partial grosses, advance ticket sales, historical patterns and expected holiday traffic — not a final result. A storm, a regional closure or an unexpected surge for a family film could move the final ranking and the totals. But the forecast is built on two days of hard actuals that support it, and every trade publication that tracks the box office has landed in the same place. As of Friday, the responsible conclusion is that Sunrise on the Reaping is on course to win Thanksgiving comfortably, and that the only real question left is how far past $85 million it lands.

The two rivals who never had a chance

Thanksgiving week is one of the biggest moviegoing weekends of the year, and the studios knew it. Two new films opened wide against Sunrise on the Reaping, and both were beaten by a distance that tells its own story about the state of the box office.

Universal's Focker In-Law, the fourth film in the Meet the Parents franchise and the one carrying Robert De Niro back to the comedy that made the series famous, took $5.3 million on Wednesday and $7.7 million on Thanksgiving Day. It is projected to take about $40 million across the five-day frame — a respectable number for a comedy, but less than half of what the Hunger Games prequel is expected to do, and below the $40–45 million forecasts it was tracking before the holiday began. Disney's Hexed, the studio's new animated feature, took $7.6 million on Wednesday — including $2.1 million from Tuesday previews — and $4.5 million on Thanksgiving Day, and is projected at about $34 million for the five days, also below its pre-holiday tracking. Both films, in other words, opened softer than expected, and both were overshadowed by a film in its second week.

The gap between first place and the rest is the story of this Thanksgiving. Sunrise on the Reaping is projected to take roughly double what either newcomer will manage, and its Wednesday gross alone — $15.1 million — was roughly double the two newcomers' Wednesday grosses combined. For Universal and Disney, the consolation is that both films have long runs ahead of them: family audiences and comedy audiences tend to turn up over weeks rather than days, and the Christmas corridor is still to come. But the headline of the holiday belongs to Lionsgate, and it is not close.

There is a third film worth noting in the holiday frame, even though it is not playing the same game. Cliff Booth, the Brad Pitt film that follows his Once Upon a Time in Hollywood character, is playing only in Imax theatres for now, and it stays Imax-only until it begins streaming on Netflix on 23 December. Its presence in the Imax numbers is a reminder that the holiday box office is not one race but several, and that the premium-screen audience is a market of its own.

Why this film worked when the last one did not

The success of Sunrise on the Reaping is not an accident, and it is not just a matter of a famous title. The film is built on the single most beloved character in the Hunger Games universe who had never had his own story — Haymitch Abernathy, the drunken, brilliant, broken mentor who guided Katniss Everdeen and Peeta Mellark through the arena in the original films, played in those films by Woody Harrelson. The prequel, adapted from Suzanne Collins's novel of the same name, published in March 2025, goes back 24 years to tell the story of the 50th Hunger Games — the Second Quarter Quell, in which twice as many tributes were sent into the arena — and of the 16-year-old Haymitch who won them, played here by Joseph Zada, with Whitney Peak as Lenore Dove and Mckenna Grace as Maysilee Donner.

That premise gave the film two things its predecessor did not have. The first is a character the audience already loves, in the story they have always wanted to see: how Haymitch won, what it cost him, and why he became the man Katniss met decades later. The second is a story with genuine stakes that do not depend on knowing the outcome. Everyone who has read the books or seen the films knows that Haymitch survives the 50th Games. The question the film has to answer is not whether he lives but what living costs — and the novel's answer, that the Capitol's revenge on a victor who outsmarted it is to take everyone he loves, is the darkest material in the franchise, and the most emotionally powerful.

The film is directed by Francis Lawrence, who has directed every Hunger Games film since Catching Fire, and that continuity matters. Lawrence knows this world, its visual language and its rhythms, and the prequel looks and feels like a Hunger Games film rather than a spin-off wearing the franchise's clothes. The audience response reflects that: the film holds a 94 percent audience score on Rotten Tomatoes, according to the holiday box-office roundups, a figure that speaks to the word of mouth that has carried it through its first week. Good word of mouth is the difference between a big opening and a big run, and this film has it.

There is also a simpler explanation, and it is the one the box office usually rewards: the film is, by the standards of a franchise that has always been about spectacle and emotion in equal measure, a very good night at the cinema. The arena sequences are large and brutal, the love story is real, and the ending is devastating in the way the best Hunger Games stories are devastating — because the victory is real and the price of it is unbearable. Audiences who came for the spectacle stayed for the story, and they told their friends.

A franchise that refused to die

The Hunger Games is now one of the longest-running franchises in modern Hollywood, and its history is a study in how a series can survive its own ending. The original four films, released between 2012 and 2015, took more than $3 billion worldwide, with Catching Fire — the 2013 sequel that set the franchise's Thanksgiving record — standing as the biggest of them. When the series ended, the conventional wisdom was that it was over. Then Collins returned with The Ballad of Songbirds & Snakes in 2020, a prequel about a young Coriolanus Snow, and Lionsgate turned it into a film in 2023 that took $166 million at home and $349 million worldwide — a solid result, but a clear step down from the original series, and one that left the question of whether the franchise had anything left to say.

Sunrise on the Reaping answers that question. Its $119.3 million opening weekend was the fifth-biggest debut of the year, and it is already the highest-grossing Hunger Games film since Mockingjay — Part 2 closed the original series in 2015. Its Tuesday record — $15.98 million on 24 November, a new Tuesday high for both Lionsgate and the franchise — showed that the audience was not just turning up on the weekend but returning on weekdays, the sign of a film with genuine momentum rather than a front-loaded opening. And its second-weekend projection of about $58 million for Friday-to-Sunday suggests a hold that would be the envy of most blockbusters.

The comparison with Catching Fire is the one that matters most. That film, released in November 2013, took $158.1 million over its opening weekend and set the standard for what a Hunger Games film could do at Thanksgiving. Sunrise on the Reaping is not going to match that opening, and it does not need to. What it has done is prove that the franchise can still open a film to more than $100 million, still dominate a holiday weekend, and still produce a film that audiences love — thirteen years after the first film and eleven years after the original series ended. In an era when franchises are routinely declared dead after a single soft opening, that is a considerable achievement.

What the Thanksgiving numbers say about the whole box office

The success of one film should not hide the wider picture, and the wider picture this Thanksgiving is mixed. The overall holiday frame is tracking at $210 million to $215 million across all releases, according to Variety — below last year's $290 million, and far below the 2024 record of $423 million, a year when the Thanksgiving corridor was lifted by a once-in-a-generation combination of releases. The Thanksgiving box office, in other words, is down sharply from the last two years, even as its biggest film is thriving.

There are reasons for that, and they are not all about the films. The 2024 record was driven by a pair of films that appealed to almost everyone, and by a calendar that put the holiday in the best possible place. This year's frame has one dominant film and two newcomers that underperformed their tracking, and the rest of the market is thin. The weather did not help either: the same nor'easter that knocked out power to hundreds of thousands of homes across the East Coast over Thanksgiving, and that forced the postponement of the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, will have kept some audiences at home, at least on the Thursday.

But the concentration of the box office in a single film is itself a story. When one film takes roughly 40 percent of the entire holiday frame, as Sunrise on the Reaping is projected to do, it means the audience is choosing with unusual clarity. They are not spreading themselves across five films. They are going to see one film, and they are going in large numbers. That is good news for Lionsgate, and it is a reminder for the rest of the industry that a film with a clear premise, a beloved character and strong word of mouth can still cut through a crowded calendar — and through a storm.

It is also a reminder of how much the box office has changed since the last time a Hunger Games film dominated Thanksgiving. In 2013, the audience for Catching Fire was overwhelmingly young, and the film's success was driven by teenagers and young adults. In 2026, the audience for Sunrise on the Reaping includes the people who were teenagers in 2013 and are now in their late twenties, bringing their own children, and the parents who took them to the first films and are now returning for the prequel. The franchise has grown up with its audience, and the audience has brought the franchise with it. That is the rarest thing in Hollywood: a series that does not just survive its audience growing older, but grows older with it.

What happens next

The immediate future of the film is a matter of days. Studio estimates for the full five-day weekend arrive on Sunday, 29 November, and the actual figures — the ones that count — follow on Monday, 30 November. Those numbers will settle the question of whether the $85 million projection was right, and by how much. If the film lands at or above $85 million, its domestic total will be around $233 million, and it will be on course for a final North American total well past $300 million. If it lands below, the shortfall will be measured in single-digit millions, and the film will still be one of the year's biggest hits.

Beyond the weekend, the film's run will be shaped by the calendar. December is traditionally a strong month for the box office, with the Christmas corridor offering a second wave of family audiences, and a film that has already proved its word of mouth is well placed to benefit. The awards season will also matter: a film that is a commercial success and a critical one, with a young star in Joseph Zada who has announced himself to the world in this role, is the kind of film that attracts attention from the guilds and the academies, and attention of that kind extends a run.

For Lionsgate, the stakes are larger than one film. The studio has had two major worldwide opening performances this year, and Sunrise on the Reaping is the bigger of them. Its success validates the decision to keep the franchise alive, and it raises the obvious question of what comes next. Collins has written two prequels now, and both have been filmed. Whether she writes a third, and whether Lionsgate films it, will depend in part on how this film finishes its run — and on whether the appetite that has produced $175 million in a week is still there when the next story is ready to be told.

The young star at the centre of it all

For all the talk of numbers and records, the human story of this Thanksgiving belongs to Joseph Zada, the 21-year-old Australian actor who plays Haymitch — and who, a year ago, was barely known outside casting circles. Zada was chosen from a wide search to carry a film that rests almost entirely on his shoulders, and to do so in the shadow of Woody Harrelson's beloved performance as the older Haymitch. That is a near-impossible assignment for an established star, let alone a newcomer. The box office suggests he has pulled it off: audiences are not just turning up for the title, they are returning for a performance, and the word of mouth that has carried the film through its first week is built, more than anything, on the belief that Zada makes the young Haymitch feel like the same man Harrelson played — clever, reckless, funny, and already carrying the weight of what the arena will do to him.

Zada's casting is also a reminder of how the franchise finds its stars. Jennifer Lawrence was 21 when the first Hunger Games film made her a global star. Josh Hutcherson was 19. The series has always bet on young actors, and it has always been rewarded for it. Zada, who appears at the film's world premiere in New York in the photograph above, now faces the same trajectory: a single film that changes everything, a character that will follow him for the rest of his career, and an audience that already regards him as Haymitch. The $175 million his film has taken in a week is, in part, a measure of how completely that gamble has paid off.

For now, the numbers speak for themselves. A film that opened to $119.3 million, set a Tuesday record, led Wednesday with $15.1 million, led Thanksgiving Day with $11.6 million, passed $175 million in a week, and is heading for $85 million over the holiday weekend has done something that very few films manage: it has made Thanksgiving its own. The turkey is eaten, the parade is over, and the shopping is done. The Hunger Games won the weekend. And it is not finished yet.
