TITLE: The Army Is About to Detonate 9,700 Pounds of Explosives in Iowa — and a Whole Town Has to Leave LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The US Army will detonate 9,700 pounds of unstable lead azide in Iowa on Nov 25. Middletown must evacuate as toxic lead dust could drift 40 miles. ---BODY---

The entrance sign of the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, where the US Army plans to detonate nearly 10,000 pounds of degraded lead azide on Wednesday, November 25, 2026 — the day before Thanksgiving. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

The United States Army is about to deliberately blow up nearly 10,000 pounds of unstable explosives in rural Iowa — and it is doing it the day before Thanksgiving. At 9 a.m. Central time on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Army plans to detonate 68 deteriorating barrels of lead azide, a highly sensitive primary explosive, stored inside earth-covered magazines at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, a small farming town about 150 miles southwest of Des Moines. The stockpile, which weighs more than 9,700 pounds in net explosive weight, has degraded and crystallised to the point where the Army says it can no longer be moved, stored, or left alone — and where the risk of it blowing up by itself is now greater than the risk of blowing it up on purpose. Every resident of Middletown has been told to leave. People living within five miles of the plant have been urged to evacuate, people living five to 20 miles away have been told to stay indoors with their windows shut, and Army projections released on Tuesday show that toxic lead dust from the blast could settle on surfaces as far as 40 miles to the southwest, reaching from southeastern Iowa into northern Missouri. It is, by any measure, an extraordinary operation: the deliberate detonation of the nation's entire stockpile of a chemical so unstable that the Army cannot transport it, carried out in the open air, over homes, farms and livestock, on the eve of America's biggest family holiday.

The operation has been days in the making and has already been delayed, fought over in court, and argued about in public for more than a week. Environmental groups sued to stop it. A federal appeals court had to reinstate the Environmental Protection Agency permit that allows it. President Donald Trump weighed in, saying he wanted agreement from Iowa's Democratic governor-elect before allowing it to go ahead. And on Tuesday, the day before the blast, residents across three counties received an imminent-threat alert on their mobile phones, telling them the detonation was coming and what they needed to do to protect themselves. The Army, for its part, insists there is no safer option — that the lead azide has become so degraded that cold weather alone could set it off, and that a controlled, mitigated detonation, carried out on the Army's terms, with hours of preparation and monitoring, is far preferable to an uncontrolled, spontaneous explosion in the middle of the night, with no warning at all. "There is consensus across the experts across the country that a controlled, mitigated detonation of the lead azide here at Iowa Army Ammunition Plant is far preferable to an uncontrolled, spontaneous detonation of that substance," the Army's acting secretary, Adam Telle, told reporters on Tuesday. The question now is whether the Army can keep the blast, and the toxic lead dust it will throw into the sky, under control — and what happens to the people, the land and the livestock underneath the plume if it cannot.

What is lead azide — and why it cannot be moved

Lead azide is not a household name, but it is one of the most sensitive explosives in the military's inventory, and that sensitivity is precisely the problem. It is a primary explosive — a chemical used to set off other, larger explosives — which means it is designed to detonate easily, from heat, friction, shock or even a sharp knock. In its fresh, properly stored form, it can be handled safely by trained personnel under controlled conditions. But lead azide degrades over time, and as it degrades it crystallises, forming sharp, unstable crystals that are far more sensitive than the original material. A drum of crystallised lead azide can, in the worst case, detonate from a change in temperature, from being jostled, or from nothing at all. That is the state of the stockpile in Middletown: 68 plastic barrels, stored in earth-covered magazines at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant, that a routine safety inspection found to be severely degraded and crystallised. The Army says the material is now too unstable to be moved — that loading the barrels onto a truck, or even shifting them within the plant, could set them off — and too unstable to be left where it is, because the onset of cold weather could do the same. The only option the Army says it has left is to destroy the material where it sits, in a controlled detonation, before it destroys itself.

The stockpile did not arrive in Middletown by accident. After lead azide was deemed largely obsolete years ago, the Army gathered unused stockpiles from military installations around the country and brought them to the Iowa plant in 2009, to be stored and regularly monitored in one place. The plant, which has made munitions for the US military for decades, was the logical home for material that nobody wanted but nobody could safely get rid of. For 17 years, that arrangement worked: the barrels sat in their magazines, the Army inspected them, and nothing happened. But time, as it always does with unstable chemistry, caught up with the stockpile. The inspections that found the degradation were routine — the kind of safety check the Army carries out as a matter of course — and what they found was a stockpile that had crossed the line from old to dangerous. The Army's decision to detonate it in place, rather than to attempt to move it, remove it, or treat it chemically, reflects how far that line has been crossed: when the material is too sensitive to transport, the blast has to happen where the material is, and the people around it have to get out of the way.

The scale of what is about to be detonated is hard to grasp in the abstract, so it is worth putting it in concrete terms. Nearly 10,000 pounds of net explosive weight — 4,500 kilograms — is the equivalent of several tonnes of high explosive, stored in 68 separate drums. The detonation will not be a single, tidy explosion. It will be a series of blasts, designed to destroy the material in place, and it will throw a plume of dust and debris into the air above the plant. That dust is the heart of the public safety problem, because lead azide is, as its name suggests, a compound of lead — and lead, once it is in the air as fine dust, is a poison. It settles on soil, on crops, on rooftops, on playground equipment, on the coats of livestock. It can be inhaled, ingested, or absorbed through contact with contaminated surfaces. And it is most dangerous to the people who are most vulnerable to it: pregnant women, children under six, and people with chronic illness. That is why the Army's warnings on Tuesday singled those groups out by name, and why the evacuation advice was framed not as a precaution but as an instruction.

The evacuation: a whole town told to leave

The evacuation plan the Army and the Federal Emergency Management Agency announced on Tuesday is sweeping, and it is built around a simple geography: a cone, extending southwest from the plant, in the direction the wind is expected to carry the plume. Every resident of Middletown itself — the town that hosts the plant — has been told to leave, along with everyone living within a five-mile cone extending southwest of the facility. The Federal Emergency Management Agency said on Tuesday that residents within five miles of the blast may be evacuated, and the Army said there was a recommended evacuation zone for all residents of Middletown and the five-mile cone, beginning on Tuesday, November 24. For people living five to 20 miles from the plant, the advice is different but no less serious: stay indoors, keep windows and doors closed, and do not go outside while the plume is in the air. And the Army's own projections show that the dust could travel further still — settling on surfaces as far as 40 miles southwest of the blast site, through southeastern Iowa and into northern Missouri. Some officials have said the plume could travel 100 miles or more. The Army said it would release updated projection maps hourly, beginning six hours before the scheduled explosion, so that residents could see, in near real time, where the dust was expected to go.

The practical support for the evacuation is substantial, and it reflects how seriously the federal government is taking the operation. The Federal Emergency Management Agency is providing short-term hotel accommodation, funded by the federal government, through Friday, November 27, with flexibility and extensions available on request, as well as food, water and emergency transport for people who cannot travel on their own. Affected residents can register for help online at DisasterAssistance.gov or by calling the Federal Emergency Management Agency's helpline, 24 hours a day, on 1-800-621-3362. The state of Iowa has opened an emergency operations centre, and Governor Kim Reynolds has issued a disaster emergency proclamation for Des Moines, Lee and Van Buren counties, a step that unlocks additional resources for the affected areas. The Federal Aviation Administration has issued a temporary flight restriction over the area around the plant, running from Tuesday through 12:52 p.m. Central time on Wednesday, to keep aircraft clear of the blast and the plume. And on Tuesday, residents across the area received an imminent-threat alert on their mobile phones from the Federal Emergency Management Agency, telling them the detonation would take place at 9 a.m. on Wednesday and advising people within five miles to seek evacuation or relocation assistance, and people five to 20 miles away to stay indoors.

The advice for people who cannot leave, or who live outside the evacuation zone, is detailed and specific, and it reads like the instructions for a chemical spill rather than a fireworks display. Stay indoors with windows and doors closed. Set heating and cooling systems to recirculate air rather than drawing in outside air. Consider replacing a furnace filter with a new one before the detonation, rated MERV 11 or higher if possible. Avoid the area altogether if you can. And after the blast, wait for officials to determine that it is safe before going outside, opening windows, or cleaning surfaces that may have collected lead dust. The acting secretary of the Army was blunt about who should be most careful: "Individuals that are in that directly affected area... if they are women who are pregnant, children under the age of 6, or children in general, and individuals who may have chronic disease, you definitely want to be out of that area. I would evacuate that area that is signaled in the model." It is not the language of a routine safety briefing. It is the language of a government that knows it is about to do something dangerous, and is trying, in public, to make sure nobody is hurt by it.

A week of delays, lawsuits and political wrangling

The detonation was not supposed to happen this way, on this day, with this much public alarm — and the week that led up to it explains why the operation has become a national story rather than a routine piece of military housekeeping. The plan was announced to the public only five days before its originally scheduled date, and the short notice, combined with the scale of the blast and the toxicity of the dust, drew immediate criticism from the community, from elected officials and from environmental groups. The Environmental Protection Agency had given the Army a permit to detonate the material starting on November 13, but environmental groups filed suit in federal court and were granted a temporary stay of that permit, and the Army said it would hold off on the detonation until November 23. On Monday, a federal appeals court cleared the way for the operation to proceed, reinstating the permit and removing the legal obstacle. President Donald Trump then weighed in, saying he wanted agreement from Iowa's Democratic governor-elect before allowing the detonation to continue — an intervention that added a political dimension to what was already a legal and environmental dispute, and that underscored how unusual it is for a peacetime military operation on American soil to become a matter of presidential attention.

The Army's argument throughout has been that the delay itself was part of the danger. Army officials have said that cold weather could cause the degraded lead azide to explode spontaneously before a planned detonation could be carried out, and that every day the stockpile sat in its magazines was a day closer to an accident that nobody could control. That argument, and the appeals court's decision to reinstate the permit, carried the day — but it did not resolve the underlying tension, which is that the Army is asking a community to accept a risk it did not choose, on a timetable it did not set, for a problem it did not create. The residents of Middletown did not ask for the nation's stockpile of obsolete explosives to be stored in their town, and they did not ask for it to be blown up over their homes the day before Thanksgiving. The Army's position is that it has no choice, and that the alternative is worse. The community's position, as expressed by its elected officials and by the environmental groups that sued, is that the Army should have found a safer way, or a safer place, or given people more time. Both positions are understandable. Only one of them is going to happen at 9 a.m. on Wednesday.

The political wrangling has also exposed a fault line that runs through the whole operation: the question of who is responsible for the risk, and who gets to decide what level of risk is acceptable. The Army says the decision is a technical one, made by experts, on the basis of the chemistry and the modelling. The environmental groups that sued say it is a public health decision, and that the public — the people who will breathe the air and live on the land under the plume — should have had more of a say in it. The federal appeals court's decision settled the legal question, but not the political one, and the fact that the President himself intervened suggests that the political question is far from settled. What is settled is the timetable: Wednesday, 9 a.m., Middletown, Iowa, 9,700 pounds of lead azide, and a plume of lead dust that will go wherever the wind takes it.

The lead dust: what it is, and why it matters

The reason the detonation has drawn so much attention — and so much fear — is not the explosion itself, but what the explosion will put into the air. Lead azide, when it detonates, produces lead dust: fine particles of lead and lead compounds that can travel on the wind, settle on surfaces, and persist in soil for years. Lead is a neurotoxin. There is no safe level of lead exposure, and children are especially vulnerable: even low levels of lead in a child's blood can affect brain development, learning and behaviour, and the effects are irreversible. Pregnant women are at risk because lead can cross the placenta and affect the developing foetus. Adults with chronic illness, particularly kidney disease, are also more vulnerable. The Army's own modelling acknowledges this: the projections it released on Tuesday show lead dust settling on surfaces as far as 40 miles from the blast site, and the Agency's advice to stay indoors, to keep windows closed, and to change furnace filters is designed to reduce the amount of dust that gets into homes and lungs. But no amount of modelling can eliminate the uncertainty — about exactly where the wind will carry the plume, about how much dust will fall where, and about how much of it will remain in the soil and on surfaces after the air clears.

The Army says it has detailed a wide range of measures to keep the lead dust to a minimum, and officials spent Tuesday describing them to reporters. The measures include the timing of the detonation — 9 a.m., when the weather forecast is expected to be favourable for dispersing the plume away from populated areas — and the use of water, soil cover and other mitigations at the detonation site to suppress dust. The Army also said it would release updated projection maps hourly, beginning six hours before the blast, so that residents and officials could track the expected path of the plume and adjust their precautions accordingly. Whether those measures will be enough is a question that cannot be answered until after the blast, when the Army and the Environmental Protection Agency begin testing the air, the soil and the surfaces in the affected area for lead contamination. That testing, and the cleanup that may follow it, will take days, weeks or longer — and it will determine whether the operation is remembered as a controlled detonation that went according to plan, or as the day the Army covered southeastern Iowa in lead dust the day before Thanksgiving.

The long-term question is what happens to the land. Lead does not break down in soil. Once it settles, it stays, unless it is physically removed — and removing lead-contaminated soil from farmland, from gardens, from school playgrounds, is a slow, expensive and disruptive process. The Army has not said, in public, how much testing it will do, how long it will take, or who will pay for any cleanup that is needed. Those questions will be asked, loudly, in the days after the blast, by the farmers whose crops are under the plume, by the parents whose children play in the yards under it, and by the elected officials who represent them. For now, the Army's message is that the operation is necessary, that the risk of doing nothing is greater than the risk of doing it, and that the mitigations in place are the best available. The people of Middletown, and of the counties around it, will have to take that on trust — from a town that, by Wednesday morning, most of them will have left.

Thanksgiving, interrupted

There is a human dimension to the operation that the numbers do not capture, and it is the timing. The detonation is scheduled for the day before Thanksgiving — the day when millions of Americans travel to be with family, when kitchens fill with the smell of roasting turkey, when the holiday that is, more than any other, about home and togetherness, begins. For the residents of Middletown, and for the people living in the evacuation zone and the shelter-in-place area, this Thanksgiving will be different. Some will spend it in a hotel room paid for by the Federal Emergency Management Agency, waiting to hear whether it is safe to go home. Some will spend it indoors, windows shut, furnace on recirculate, listening for news of the plume. Some will spend it cleaning lead dust off surfaces they cannot see, with instructions they did not expect to receive. The Army's advice to residents — to consider avoiding the area, to stay indoors, to change furnace filters — was issued in the same week that the rest of the country was planning menus and booking flights, and the contrast is stark: while 81.5 million Americans are travelling for the holiday, a small town in Iowa is being told to leave home, not to visit family, but to get out of the way of an explosion.

That contrast is part of why the story has resonated so widely, and why it has been covered by every major news organisation in the country. It is not only a story about explosives, or about lead, or about the Army. It is a story about a community being asked to sacrifice its holiday — and, potentially, its health and its land — for a problem that was created by a decision made in 2009, in a different administration, for reasons that had nothing to do with Middletown. It is a story about the hidden costs of the military's stockpiles, and about what happens when the chemicals that were once useful become too dangerous to keep. And it is a story about the limits of control: the Army can choose the hour of the detonation, and it can model the plume, and it can tell people to leave — but it cannot control the wind, and it cannot guarantee that the dust will go where the models say it will go. That uncertainty is what the residents of Middletown are living with tonight, as they pack their bags, lock their doors, and drive away from the town they call home, the night before Thanksgiving.

What happens next

At 9 a.m. Central time on Wednesday, the Army will detonate the stockpile, and the operation will enter its second phase: monitoring, testing and, if necessary, cleanup. The Army has said it will release updated projection maps hourly in the run-up to the blast, and that it will monitor the air and the ground in the affected area afterwards. The Environmental Protection Agency, which issued the permit for the detonation, will be involved in that monitoring, as will state and local emergency management authorities. The Federal Emergency Management Agency's hotel accommodation runs through Friday, November 27, with extensions available — a timeline that suggests officials expect it to be at least several days before residents can safely return, and before the full extent of any contamination is known. The temporary flight restriction over the area runs until 12:52 p.m. on Wednesday, after which aircraft will be allowed back into the airspace above the plant. And the disaster emergency proclamation issued by Governor Reynolds for Des Moines, Lee and Van Buren counties will remain in effect, giving the state the flexibility to direct resources to the affected areas as the situation develops.

The longer-term questions will take longer to answer. How much lead dust fell, and where? How much of it remains in the soil, on crops, on rooftops, in homes? Will the farmland under the plume be safe to farm next spring? Will the children who live in the affected area need blood tests for lead exposure? Will the Army pay for the cleanup, and for the testing, and for the hotel rooms, and for the lost Thanksgiving? Those questions will be asked by the people who live under the plume, and by the journalists who cover them, and by the elected officials who represent them — and the answers will determine whether the detonation is remembered as a necessary, if painful, act of public safety, or as a failure of planning that put a community at risk. For now, the facts are these: 68 barrels, 9,700 pounds, one small town, and a plume of lead dust that will go where the wind takes it. At 9 a.m. on Wednesday, the Army will find out whether its models were right. So will the people of Middletown — from a safe distance, if the Army's advice is followed, and from their homes, if it is not.

The bottom line

The United States Army will detonate nearly 10,000 pounds of degraded, crystallised lead azide — stored in 68 deteriorating barrels at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa — at approximately 9 a.m. Central time on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the day before Thanksgiving, after a federal appeals court reinstated the Environmental Protection Agency permit for the operation and ended a legal challenge by environmental groups. The Army says the material is too unstable to move and too dangerous to leave, and that a controlled detonation is far safer than a spontaneous explosion. All residents of Middletown and everyone within a five-mile cone southwest of the plant have been told to evacuate, with federally funded hotel accommodation available through Friday, November 27; people five to 20 miles away have been advised to stay indoors with windows closed, and Army projections show lead dust could settle as far as 40 miles away, into northern Missouri. Pregnant women, children under six and people with chronic illness have been singled out for the strongest warnings. The operation, announced to the public only five days before its original date, has already drawn a lawsuit, a court stay, a presidential intervention and a disaster emergency proclamation from Iowa's governor — and it will be watched, hour by hour, by a community that has been told to leave its homes on the eve of the year's biggest family holiday, and to trust that the Army's models, and the wind, will keep them safe.
