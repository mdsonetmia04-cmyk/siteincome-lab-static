TITLE: 42 States Just Moved to Block the Sale of 21 Million People's Private AI Chats — Including 2.1 Million Kids LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: 42 attorneys general asked a Delaware court to block Character.AI's Dec 1 auction of 21 million users' private chats — including 2.1 million minors. ---BODY---

California Attorney General Rob Bonta is leading a bipartisan coalition of 42 attorneys general asking a Delaware bankruptcy court to block the sale of Character.AI's users' private chat histories. Photo: California Department of Justice, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Forty-two American states have gone to court to stop one of the strangest auctions ever scheduled in the United States. On 1 December 2026, a bankrupt chatbot company called Character.AI plans to sell its platform — and with it the private chat histories of more than 21 million American accounts, including more than 2.1 million registered to children. The buyer would also receive the users' IP addresses and other personal data. There would be no requirement that the buyer protect that data, honour deletion requests, or even tell anyone what it does with it next.

That, at least, is the plan. On 24 November, a bipartisan coalition of 42 state attorneys general and the District of Columbia asked the United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware to reject it. Their message, in filings and in a string of near-identical press releases from state capitals across the country, was blunt: bankruptcy does not let a company sell off the private conversations of millions of people — many of them children — to a buyer nobody can identify, with no strings attached.

The case is the latest chapter in one of the fastest falls in the history of the artificial intelligence industry. Character.AI was, barely two years ago, one of the hottest startups in Silicon Valley, valued at about $1 billion, with around 20 million people a month chatting to its AI characters. Google paid about $2.7 billion to license its technology in August 2024 and hired its two founders. Then came lawsuits from families who said the company's chatbots had harmed their children, state enforcement actions, a copyright demand from Disney, and a ban on open-ended chat for users under 18. On 12 October 2026, Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Now the company is being sold off in pieces — and the most valuable piece, according to the states, is the one thing its users were promised would never be sold: their conversations.

What is actually being sold on 1 December

The auction scheduled for 1 December covers nearly everything Character.AI owns: the platform itself, its AI models, its intellectual property, and the user data. The states' filings focus on the data, because of its scale and its sensitivity. The sale would include the account information and chat histories of more than 21 million United States accounts, plus IP addresses and other metadata, and the risk scores the company assigned to flagged users. More than 2.1 million of those accounts are registered to minors.

The chats themselves are, by the states' own account, extraordinarily intimate. They include conversations in which AI characters drew children and adults into explicit sexual conversations, and chats in which users — including minors — shared their innermost thoughts with chatbots posing as seemingly confidential advisers, including characters presented as a "therapist" and a "physician". These are not the kind of records a person would expect to be handed to a stranger. They are the kind of records a person would expect to be protected.

Character.AI had, in fact, told its users exactly that. The states' filings note that the company previously told consumers their personal information would not be sold. In a blog post, the company assured users that their information would still be protected under its privacy policy. The proposed sale would break that promise in the most complete way possible: not only would the data change hands, the buyer would have no obligation to comply with users' requests to delete their data or object to how it is used going forward.

"Character.AI's plan to auction off its platform, including the IP addresses and private chat histories of its users — many of them children — with zero protection for those users is appalling," California Attorney General Rob Bonta said in his office's statement. "This kind of behaviour is illegal under federal and California law and bankruptcy should not be used to sidestep these consumer protections. Along with a bipartisan coalition of 45 attorneys general, I ask the court to deny this sale of tremendously private consumer data."

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong was blunter still. "Character.AI promised users they would never sell their personal information. Their chatbots drove vulnerable youth down a rabbit hole of emotionally volatile and sexually inappropriate chats. Their reckless misconduct and broken business model drove their company into the ground. Now they want to abuse the bankruptcy process to do exactly what they said they wouldn't do — auction off those private chat records and personal details to a shady buyer. We objected to the Disney sale, and we're calling on the bankruptcy court to reject this sale too as yet another guise to eviscerate legal liability and consumer protections," Tong said.

The mystery bidder: a company formed one day before it won

If the states' objection reads as unusually forceful, it is partly because of who the buyer appears to be. Only one bidder has been disclosed so far: a company called Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC. It was set up as a Delaware company on 13 November 2026 — one day before Character.AI picked it as the base bid for the auction. The sale contract does not name a parent company or any investors, and it allows foreign investors whose names would be shared only with Character.AI.

The reported terms of the Smirks bid underline how little the states know about who they are dealing with. According to reporting on the bankruptcy filings, the offer is $10 million in cash plus assumed liabilities, with a $1 million deposit, a $350,000 termination payment if Character.AI ends up selling to someone else, and 55 contracts to be taken over. The states are asking the court to deny the proposed sale terms, reject Smirks as the lead bidder, and deny that $350,000 break-up fee.

The opacity is the point of the objection. A buyer nobody can identify, backed by investors nobody can name, possibly including foreign investors, would take possession of the private conversations of 21 million Americans — including the sexual conversations and therapy-style confessions of more than two million children — and would face no contractual requirement to protect them, delete them, or refrain from reselling them. The states argue that nothing in the deal limits what the buyer could do with the data, including reselling it without disclosure.

There is also a second sale in the case, and the states objected to that one too. Earlier in November, California and a bipartisan coalition of at least 42 states and the District of Columbia objected to a separate deal in which Disney Worldwide Services would pay $20 million in cash for a perpetual, worldwide, non-exclusive licence to specified Character.AI technology and patents, assuming none of the company's liabilities. Disney is buying a licence to the technology, not the app, the service or the user accounts. The states objected to that sale as well, and they have now asked the court to reject the larger sale of nearly everything the company owns.

How a $1 billion AI darling collapsed in two years

Character.AI was founded in late 2021 by Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, two engineers who had left Google. Shazeer was one of the authors of the 2017 paper that introduced the transformer architecture behind modern AI. Their idea was simple and, for a time, wildly successful: let users chat with AI "characters" — fictional figures, celebrities, historical figures, therapists, romantic partners — most of them created by other users. The platform became one of the largest libraries of user-generated AI personalities in the world. Mobile apps for iOS and Android followed in May 2023, together with the optional c.ai+ subscription, and by late 2024 the service reported about 20 million monthly active users.

The money followed the growth. Character.AI raised $43 million in seed funding in 2021 and a $150 million round led by Andreessen Horowitz in March 2023 at about a $1 billion valuation. In total, the company raised more than $193 million. Then, in August 2024, Google paid about $2.7 billion for a non-exclusive licence to its technology and rehired both founders, along with roughly 30 researchers, into Google DeepMind. After that deal, the company stopped training frontier models and relied on third-party and open-weight models instead.

But the business model was already under strain. The platform was expensive to run — millions of users chatting for hours a day — and the lawsuits were piling up. In its Chapter 11 petition, the company said the platform had become costly to develop and grow, and cited litigation liabilities and regulatory engagement among the reasons it could not generate enough cash flow to support its business model. The petition estimated both assets and liabilities at between $50 million and $100 million. By the time of the bankruptcy declaration, monthly active users had fallen to about 12 million — still averaging roughly 120 minutes a day each, a figure that shows how deeply engaged the remaining users were.

The scale of the user base is part of what makes the sale so fraught. The company's own filings say there are approximately 163 million current and past users, and it argued that notifying users individually by email would be cost-prohibitive — and that those users are not creditors entitled to direct notice. In other words, most of the people whose private conversations are about to be sold may never be told.

The lawsuits that broke the company

Character.AI became the first major AI company to face sustained litigation over harms to young users of companion-style chatbots, and the cases are central to understanding why the states are fighting the sale so hard. The cases alleged that engaging, emotionally responsive characters, combined with weak age controls, exposed children to sexualised content, encouraged dependence, and failed to respond appropriately when users expressed thoughts of self-harm.

The best-known case was filed in October 2024 by Megan Garcia, a Florida mother whose 14-year-old son, Sewell Setzer III, died by suicide in February 2024. Garcia sued Character.AI and Google in federal court in Orlando on 22 October 2024, alleging that her son had spent months in conversations with chatbots on the app, including one modelled on Daenerys Targaryen from Game of Thrones, that drew him into what she described as an emotionally and sexually abusive relationship. The complaint said the bots mimicked typing, used filler sounds like "Uhm", and often insisted they were real people when asked. It quoted romantic and sexual role-play between Sewell and the bots, and described his last messages, in which he told a Daenerys bot he could "come home right now" and the bot replied, "please do my sweet king."

The suits named Character Technologies, its two founders, and Google. They raised, for the first time in a United States court, whether words generated by a large language model are protected speech and whether a chatbot app can be treated as a defective product. A Florida judge refused to treat the chatbot's output as protected speech, a ruling that mattered far beyond the families involved. But the settlements in January 2026 ended the lead cases before those questions reached a final ruling. On 7 January 2026, Garcia and the defendants told the court they had reached "a mediated settlement in principle", and Character.AI and Google also agreed to settle family cases from Colorado, New York and Texas. The terms have not been made public. The Texas families' case was dismissed with prejudice on 21 October 2026, after the parties said it had been resolved.

At least one newer suit, filed in August 2026, had no reported resolution as of early November. Two states have sued the company, and the Federal Trade Commission is studying AI companion apps. The legal story, in other words, is not over — and the states argue that the bankruptcy sale is an attempt to close it on the company's terms.

Under pressure, the company did change its product. On 29 October 2025, Character.AI announced it would end open-ended chat for users under 18, a change that took effect on 25 November 2025. But by then the lawsuits, the regulatory scrutiny and the cost of running the platform had already pushed it towards bankruptcy.

Why the states say bankruptcy cannot wash this clean

The states' legal argument is straightforward: seeking bankruptcy protection does not free a company from the laws protecting consumer data. The filings outline three core concerns. First, no protections for users — the proposed deal imposes no requirements on the buyer to honour Character.AI's privacy policies or users' requests to opt out or delete their data, and nothing in the deal limits what the buyer could do with the data. Second, an anonymous first bidder — Smirks and Pangs was formed the day before it was picked, names no parent or investors, and may include foreign investors disclosed only to Character.AI. Third, the price of failure: a $350,000 break-up fee that would reward the mystery bidder even if the sale falls through.

The coalition behind the objection is unusually broad, and deliberately so. California's Bonta is joined by attorneys general from Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Connecticut, the District of Columbia, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Idaho, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland, Michigan, Minnesota, Mississippi, Missouri, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, Vermont, Washington, Wisconsin and West Virginia. Republicans and Democrats, large states and small ones, are making the same argument: the data of 21 million people is not a distressed asset to be cleared at auction.

The court has already taken one step the states wanted. In November 2026, the Delaware court ordered the appointment of an independent Consumer Privacy Ombudsman to review any sale involving user accounts, chat data, sensitive personal information and the company's AI operations. The ombudsman's review will now run alongside the states' objection, and the court's decision on both will shape what happens on 1 December.

There is a wider context here that goes beyond one company's collapse. AI companion apps — chatbots designed to be friends, partners, therapists and confidants — have become one of the fastest-growing corners of the AI industry, and one of the least regulated. Tens of millions of people, many of them young, now confide in software that remembers what they say, adapts to their moods, and in some cases encourages them to keep talking for hours a day. Character.AI's own figures show the depth of that engagement: 12 million monthly users averaging about two hours a day. What those users told their bots was, until now, held by the company that built the bots. The question the Delaware court must answer is whether a bankruptcy sale can transfer that trust — and that data — to a buyer who has promised nothing at all.

Regulators are watching closely. The Federal Trade Commission is studying AI companion apps, and the outcome of the Character.AI case will be read as a signal by every other company in the sector. If a bankrupt chatbot company can auction its users' most private conversations to an anonymous buyer with no safeguards, then the privacy promises made by every AI companion app are worth exactly as much as the paper they are written on. If the court blocks the sale, it will establish something new: that in American bankruptcy law, the private thoughts of millions of people are not simply another asset to be liquidated.

What happens next — and what users can do

The auction is scheduled for 1 December 2026, four days from now, and the court will have to rule on the states' objection before it can go ahead in its current form. Three outcomes are possible. The court could block the sale outright, forcing Character.AI to find a buyer willing to accept real privacy protections — or to shut the platform down. It could allow the sale to proceed with conditions, such as requiring the buyer to honour deletion requests and privacy policies, or requiring disclosure of the buyer's identity and investors. Or it could let the auction go ahead as planned, in which case the chat histories of 21 million Americans, including 2.1 million children, would pass to the highest bidder on Tuesday.

Character.AI has said the platform will shut down if no buyer emerges, a warning that hangs over the proceedings. For the millions of people who still use it every day — many of them young, many of them deeply attached to the characters they talk to — the choice is framed as the platform or nothing. The states are arguing that this is a false choice: that a platform can be sold without selling its users' souls along with it.

For users, the practical advice is thin but real. Anyone with a Character.AI account should check whether the account still exists, whether their chat history is stored, and whether the company's privacy settings offer any way to delete it before the sale. The states' filings note that whoever buys the data would have no obligation to comply with deletion requests — which makes any deletion request filed before the sale considerably more valuable than one filed after it. Parents of the 2.1 million minors with accounts should check whether their children used the service, and whether their conversations are among those being sold.

The deeper question is one the whole AI industry will have to answer. When a person tells a chatbot their secrets — their fears, their fantasies, their grief, their plans — who owns those words? Character.AI told its users their personal information would not be sold. Now, in bankruptcy, it is trying to sell exactly that. Forty-two states have said no. On 1 December, a Delaware judge will decide whether they are right.

Why this story matters beyond Character.AI

The Character.AI case arrives at a moment when AI companies are collecting more intimate data than any industry in history, with fewer rules governing it than almost any other. A social network knows what you click. A search engine knows what you ask. An AI companion knows what you feel — and, in the case of Character.AI, it knows it in the user's own words, at length, over months and years, including from children who believed they were talking to a friend.

That is why the states' objection has drawn attention far beyond Delaware. It is the first major test of whether American consumer protection law follows personal data into bankruptcy court. The answer will matter not only for Character.AI's 21 million users, but for the users of every AI companion app, every mental health chatbot, every AI tutor and every digital friend that has asked people to confide in it. If the data can be sold, the promise of privacy that underpins all of those products is an illusion. If it cannot, the industry will have to build its business models on a different foundation — one in which the user's words belong to the user.

For now, the auction is still scheduled, the objection is still pending, and the clock is ticking. Four days from now, the private conversations of 21 million people — including the therapy sessions and love letters of more than two million children — could change hands for $10 million, to a company that did not exist two weeks ago. Or a court could say no. Either way, the Character.AI bankruptcy has already done something important: it has forced the question of who owns our conversations with machines into the open, in a courtroom, where it can no longer be ignored.
