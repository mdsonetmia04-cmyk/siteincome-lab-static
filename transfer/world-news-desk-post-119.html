TITLE: Hackers Just Breached the US Air Force's Biggest Cloud — and Nobody Knows What They Took LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Hackers breached Cloud One, the US Air Force's flagship cloud platform, reaching its control plane. The Air Force won't say what was stolen. ---BODY---

An aerial view of the Pentagon in Arlington, Virginia. Hackers have breached Cloud One, the US Air Force's flagship cloud computing platform, and investigators have called in US Cyber Command to assess the damage. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0, mariordo59.

Hackers have breached the United States Air Force's flagship cloud computing platform, in a development that could jeopardise a vast swathe of sensitive tactical, personnel and logistics data. The intrusion at Cloud One, one of the biggest cloud platforms in the entire American military, was discovered within the past month or so during work to upgrade the system, according to three people familiar with the matter who spoke to Reuters. The discovery has led to disruption across the platform: the migration of back-office applications to Cloud One has been paused, updates to applications already running on it have been blocked, and investigators have called in US Cyber Command, the military's cyber force, to assess the damage.

What the hackers took — if they took anything — is unknown. Reuters said it could not establish the identity of the hackers, the full scope of the intrusion, or whether any data had been stolen. The Air Force, asked about the breach, said only that it "does not comment on the security posture, vulnerabilities or operational status of specific networks and systems." That silence, from a service that runs some of the most sensitive computing infrastructure in the world, is itself a measure of how serious the incident is.

The breach is the latest in a string of intrusions into American government systems to be disclosed in recent weeks. It follows the breach of the Central Intelligence Agency's and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence's unclassified systems, the cyber incident at the Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, and the hack of the FBI's National Crime Information Center database. Each of those incidents was serious on its own. Together, they describe a moment in which the digital infrastructure of the United States government is being tested — and, repeatedly, found wanting.

What Cloud One is — and why it matters

Cloud One was initiated in 2017 as the answer to a problem the Air Force had been struggling with for years: its computing was scattered across dozens of separate systems, each with its own hardware, its own software and its own security. The idea was to build a single, centralised platform — a one-stop shop for the Air Force's information technology needs — that would combine the resources of four of the biggest cloud operators in the world: Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle and Alphabet, the parent company of Google.

On that platform run scores of applications, including ones that manage personnel data and pay, logistics and maintenance — the unglamorous but essential systems that keep an air force flying. When a pilot is paid, when a spare part is ordered for a fighter jet, when a maintenance crew checks the service history of an aircraft, the data passes through systems that live on Cloud One. The platform also supports tactical applications, the software that helps commanders plan and execute operations. A breach of Cloud One is therefore not a breach of a single database. It is a breach of the shared foundation on which a large part of the Air Force's daily work rests.

That is why the discovery of the intrusion has caused such disruption. According to two of the sources who spoke to Reuters, along with a fourth person familiar with the matter, efforts to migrate back-office applications to Cloud One — a longstanding military priority — have been paused. Updates to applications already running on the platform have been blocked, leaving Air Force personnel unable to apply fixes, just as a new batch of serious software vulnerabilities has been disclosed across the technology industry. The pause is a precaution: until the Air Force knows how the hackers got in and whether they are still inside, moving more applications onto the platform, or changing the ones that are already there, risks making the problem worse.

The prime contractor for Cloud One was SAIC, the American technology company, until 2025, when Leidos took over the contract. The change of contractor is a detail, but it matters for the investigation: the people who built and maintained the platform, the security practices they followed, and the handover between the two companies will all be examined as investigators try to work out how the breach happened and how long it went undetected.

How the hackers got in — and what they reached

One source told Reuters that the cyber intruders exploited a software vulnerability to reach the control plane of Cloud One. The control plane is the part of a cloud platform that manages its underlying infrastructure: the servers, the networks, the storage, and the permissions that decide who can do what. Reaching the control plane is not the same as breaking into a single application. It is reaching the layer of the system that controls the system itself — the place from which an attacker can, in principle, see across the platform, create accounts, change configurations and access the data that flows through it.

Reuters could not identify the specific vulnerability the hackers exploited, and the Air Force has not said what it was. That gap in the public record is significant, because the vulnerability is the key to understanding the breach: whether it was a known flaw that should have been patched, a zero-day — a vulnerability nobody knew about until the attackers used it — or a weakness in the way the platform was configured. Each of those possibilities points to a different failure, and to a different lesson for the rest of the government.

What is known is the sequence of events. The intrusion was discovered within the past month or so during work to upgrade the system — which suggests that it was found by accident, in the course of routine work, rather than by a security system designed to detect it. That is a familiar pattern in major breaches: the attackers are discovered not because an alarm went off, but because someone doing unrelated work stumbles across something that should not be there. How long the intruders were inside Cloud One before they were discovered is unknown, and that is the question that will determine how much damage was done. An intruder who is found within days has had days to act. An intruder who has been inside for months has had months.

Investigators have called in Cyber Command, the United States military's cyber force, to assess the damage. The involvement of Cyber Command is a sign of the seriousness with which the breach is being treated: Cyber Command is the organisation that defends the military's networks and conducts offensive cyber operations, and its forensic teams are among the most capable in the American government. Their job now is to work out what the hackers did, what they saw, what they took, and whether they left anything behind — a back door, a hidden account, a piece of malicious software — that would let them return.

What is at stake

The data that could be at risk in the Cloud One breach is the kind of data that matters in a war. Tactical data — the information that commanders use to plan operations, to track aircraft and to coordinate with other services — is among the most sensitive material a military holds. Personnel data — the records of the men and women who serve, including their pay, their assignments and their personal details — is the kind of information that can be used for identity theft, for blackmail, or for targeting individuals. Logistics data — the records of what the Air Force has, where it is, and when it will run out — is the information an adversary needs to understand what the Air Force can and cannot do.

None of that data has been confirmed stolen. Reuters was explicit that it could not establish whether any data had been taken, and the Air Force has said nothing on the subject. But the possibility that it was is what makes the breach so serious. A hacker who reaches the control plane of a cloud platform has, in principle, access to the data that passes through it. Whether the hackers in this case used that access, and what they took if they did, is the central question of the investigation — and it may be months before there is a reliable answer.

There is also a second kind of damage, which is harder to measure but just as real. Cloud One was meant to be the future of the Air Force's computing: the platform on which the service would consolidate its systems, modernise its operations and move away from the scattered, ageing infrastructure of the past. The decision to pause the migration of back-office applications to the platform is a setback for that plan, and it will have consequences that last beyond the investigation. Every application that was supposed to move to Cloud One, and now will not, must stay where it is — on older systems, with older security, maintained at greater cost. Every update that was blocked leaves an application running with known vulnerabilities unpatched. The breach has not only damaged the platform. It has damaged the plan to modernise the Air Force's computing, and that damage will take time to repair.

The timing makes the disruption worse. The updates were blocked just as a new batch of serious software vulnerabilities was disclosed across the technology industry — the kind of vulnerabilities that, in normal circumstances, would be patched as quickly as possible. Air Force personnel who are unable to apply fixes to their applications are left running software with known holes in it, at precisely the moment when attackers around the world are looking for those holes. It is a cruel irony: the breach that was discovered during an upgrade has left the platform less able to defend itself against the next attack.

A string of breaches — and a pattern

The Cloud One breach is the latest in a series of intrusions into American government systems that have been disclosed in recent weeks, and it is worth setting them out together, because the pattern they form is the real story.

In November, it was reported that hackers had breached the unclassified systems of the Central Intelligence Agency and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, in an incident in which early signs pointed to China. The breach affected the systems that handle the agencies' unclassified work — not their most secret material, but the everyday infrastructure of American intelligence. In the same month, the Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, the agency that tracks money laundering and terrorist financing, suffered a cyber incident in which unauthorised actors gained access to a limited subset of its systems, forcing parts of the network offline. And the FBI's National Crime Information Center, the database that police officers across America query tens of millions of times a day, was hacked, with 19.3 million records exposed.

These are different systems, run by different agencies, breached — as far as anyone knows — by different attackers. But they share a common feature: they are the systems that the American government depends on to do its work, and they were all breached within weeks of each other. That is not a coincidence. It is a reflection of the scale of the threat: the attackers — whether they are state-backed groups, criminal gangs or both — are probing the government's systems constantly, and they are finding weaknesses faster than the government can fix them.

The Cloud One breach fits that pattern, but it also stands out from it. The CIA and ODNI systems were unclassified. FinCEN's breach was limited to a subset of its systems. The NCIC database, for all its importance, is a law enforcement tool. Cloud One is different: it is a military platform, run by the armed forces, holding tactical data. A breach of a military cloud is a breach of the infrastructure of war itself, and it raises questions that go beyond cybersecurity. If the Air Force's flagship cloud can be breached, what else can be? And if the attackers are still unknown, how does the Air Force know they are gone?

The questions nobody can answer yet

There are four questions at the heart of the Cloud One breach, and at the moment, none of them has an answer. The first is who did it. Reuters could not establish the identity of the hackers, and the Air Force has said nothing. The attackers could be a state-backed group — the kind of actor that has the resources and the patience to break into a military cloud — or they could be criminals, or a group whose motives are not yet clear. Until they are identified, it is impossible to know what they wanted, or whether they will come back.

The second question is how long they were inside. The breach was discovered within the past month during upgrade work, but that is when it was found, not when it began. The intruders could have been inside for weeks, or for months, and the difference matters enormously. A breach that lasted days is a contained incident. A breach that lasted months is a different kind of event altogether — one in which the attackers had time to map the platform, to understand its data, and to take what they wanted at their leisure.

The third question is what they took. The data that passes through Cloud One — tactical, personnel, logistics — is the kind of data that an adversary would value highly. But it is also possible that the attackers took nothing, or that they were discovered before they could. The investigation will need to examine the platform's logs, to trace what the attackers accessed and what left the system. That work is painstaking, and it is complicated by the fact that a sophisticated attacker will try to cover its tracks — deleting logs, disguising its traffic, and making its activity look like ordinary use.

The fourth question is whether they are really gone. A breach of the control plane is particularly dangerous in this respect, because an attacker who reaches the control plane can create hidden accounts, install back doors and change the platform's configuration in ways that persist after the initial intrusion is discovered. Cyber Command's forensic teams will be looking for exactly that: the signs that the attackers left a way back in. Until they are confident that the platform is clean, the Air Force cannot be sure that the breach is over — only that it has been found.

What happens next

The investigation is now in the hands of Cyber Command, and it will take time. Forensic analysis of a cloud platform of Cloud One's size is a major undertaking: the investigators must examine the logs of scores of applications, the configuration of the infrastructure, and the activity of every account that had access to the control plane. They must determine the vulnerability that was exploited, so that it can be closed — and so that other government cloud platforms can be checked for the same weakness. And they must determine the scope of the breach, so that the Air Force knows what was exposed and can take steps to protect the people and the operations affected.

In the meantime, the disruption continues. The migration of back-office applications to Cloud One remains paused, and updates to the applications already on the platform remain blocked. That is the right precaution — moving applications onto a platform that may still be compromised, or changing software on a platform whose integrity is in doubt, would be reckless — but it is a precaution with a cost. The Air Force's modernisation plan is on hold, its applications are running unpatched, and the people who depend on the platform are working around it.

The longer-term question is what the breach means for the American military's reliance on the cloud. Cloud One was built on a simple idea: that the Air Force's computing should be centralised, modern and run by the best cloud operators in the world. That idea has not been disproved by a single breach — centralised systems, properly defended, can be more secure than scattered ones. But the breach is a reminder that centralisation has a cost: when a single platform holds so much, a single breach exposes so much. The Air Force will need to decide, once the investigation is complete, whether Cloud One's security needs to be rebuilt from the ground up, or whether the platform's design — four operators, one control plane, scores of applications — is itself the problem.

For now, the facts are these: hackers breached the Air Force's flagship cloud platform, reaching its control plane through a software vulnerability that has not been identified. The breach was discovered within the past month during upgrade work. The migration of applications to the platform has been paused, updates have been blocked, and Cyber Command has been called in. Nobody knows who the hackers were, how long they were inside, or what they took. Those are the questions that matter, and they are the questions the investigation must answer — because until they are answered, the Air Force cannot be sure what it has lost, or whether it is safe.
