TITLE: FedEx Jet Just Ran Off the Runway at Miami Airport — 214-Minute Delays on Thanksgiving Eve LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A FedEx MD-11 overran the runway at Miami airport on Thanksgiving Eve, forcing an FAA ground stop and hours of delays for holiday travellers ---BODY---

A FedEx McDonnell Douglas MD-11 freighter, the same type of aircraft that overran the runway at Miami International Airport on Wednesday night. Photo: Peter Bakema via Wikimedia Commons, GFDL 1.2.

A FedEx cargo jet ran off the end of the runway at Miami International Airport on Wednesday night, November 25, 2026, forcing the Federal Aviation Administration to halt incoming flights on one of the busiest travel nights of the year and leaving Thanksgiving travellers facing delays of more than three and a half hours. The aircraft, a McDonnell Douglas MD-11 operating as FedEx Flight 910 from Memphis, overshot the runway at about 8:40 p.m. Eastern time and came to rest on the grass beyond the pavement, close to a public road. Both crew members on board escaped unhurt, and there was no fire. But the incident shut down almost the entire airport at the worst possible moment — the night before Thanksgiving, when more than 80 million Americans were travelling for the holiday.

The timing could hardly have been worse. Wednesday evening is traditionally the peak of the Thanksgiving travel rush, with airports across the United States handling millions of passengers heading home for the holiday. In Miami, the runway excursion turned a busy night into a chaotic one: the FAA ordered a ground stop on all flights bound for the airport, all but one of the runways were closed, and hours later the average delay for arriving flights had stretched to 214 minutes — nearly three and a half hours — according to the FAA. For thousands of travellers hoping to be home for Thanksgiving dinner, the night became a long wait in departure lounges hundreds of miles from Florida.

It was also the second time in less than three months that a cargo jet had overrun a runway at Miami International Airport. On September 6, an Amazon cargo jet overshot a different runway at the same airport, killing five people on the ground. That earlier crash, and the fact that the FedEx jet ended up close to the spot where the Amazon plane came to rest, immediately raised questions about safety at one of America's busiest airports — and about the MD-11 itself, a 30-year-old aircraft type that had only returned to service in May after a six-month grounding following a fatal crash in Louisville that killed 14 people.

What happened on Wednesday night

FedEx Flight 910 had departed from Memphis, Tennessee — from the airport now named for Frederick W. Smith, the founder of FedEx — on a routine cargo run to Miami. The aircraft was a McDonnell Douglas MD-11, a three-engine wide-body freighter that has been the workhorse of FedEx's long-haul cargo fleet for decades. There were two crew members on board and no passengers: cargo flights carry freight, not people, and on this night the only souls on the aircraft were the two pilots.

The approach to Miami took place in difficult weather. Heavy rain was falling across South Florida, and a flood watch was in effect for the area. At about 8:40 p.m., as the MD-11 touched down on Runway 26R, something went wrong. The aircraft did not stop in time. It ran past the end of the runway, across the paved overrun area and onto the grass beyond, coming to rest beyond the airport's perimeter fence near Northwest 67th Avenue, according to flight-tracking data and footage of the scene. Photographs and video from the scene showed the big tri-jet sitting on wet grass in the dark, its lights on, with emergency vehicles around it and mobile stairs deployed at its door. There was no visible damage to the front of the aircraft.

The Miami-Dade Sheriff's Office confirmed that both crew members escaped without injury, and Miami-Dade Fire Rescue reported no injuries and no transports to hospital. Emergency personnel from the Sheriff's Office and Fire Rescue reported no fire or smoke — a crucial detail, because a cargo jet carrying fuel that has left the runway is at serious risk of catching fire, and the absence of flames meant the incident, while serious, had not become a disaster.

FedEx confirmed the incident in a statement, saying: "Flight 910 from Memphis to Miami experienced an issue upon landing Wednesday evening at Miami International Airport." The company said it was coordinating with investigating officials. The FAA, which is investigating the incident, said the aircraft was a McDonnell Douglas MD-11 coming from Memphis.

The ground stop that froze Thanksgiving travel

The FAA's response was swift. At 8:16 p.m. Eastern time — before the aircraft had even come to rest, according to the sequence of advisories — the agency ordered a ground stop on all flights headed to Miami, affecting traffic from multiple air traffic control centres including Miami, Atlanta, Washington and Houston. A ground stop means aircraft bound for the affected airport are held on the ground at their departure airports rather than being allowed to take off and circle in the air. It is one of the most disruptive tools in the FAA's kit, and it is used only when an airport cannot safely accept arrivals.

The ground stop was initially scheduled to last until at least 9:45 p.m. Eastern time, with the FAA advisory giving it a 30 to 60 per cent chance of extension. In the event, the disruption lasted far longer. All landing strips at the airport except one were shut down as a result of the incident, Miami Airport said on its official account on X. As of 10:30 p.m., all runways remained closed with the exception of one that accommodates arrivals and departures, and another for outbound flights only, the airport confirmed.

The FAA's initial advisory projected average delays of 44 minutes, with potential maximum delays reaching 78 minutes. But as the night wore on and the backlog of diverted and delayed flights grew, the picture worsened dramatically. Several hours after the incident, the airport remained under a ground delay by the FAA, which reported an average wait time of 214 minutes for last-minute holiday travellers. By early Thursday, the FAA was still reporting hours-long flight delays at Miami.

Later in the evening, the airport began a partial reopening, using Runway 9R for some operations while the investigation into the excursion got under way. But for the thousands of passengers whose flights had been held, diverted or cancelled, the damage was done: Thanksgiving Eve is the one night of the year when there is almost no slack in the system, and a delay of three hours or more can mean missing the holiday altogether.

The airport recommended that passengers scheduled to fly on Wednesday night check with their airlines for the latest flight updates before travelling to the airport. "We appreciate our passengers' patience as we work to safely resume normal operations," the airport said in a statement.

A holiday already under strain

The Miami incident did not happen in isolation. Thanksgiving 2026 was already shaping up to be one of the most disrupted holiday travel periods in years, with a powerful storm system battering both coasts of the United States. Severe storms were affecting the East and West coasts ahead of Thanksgiving, with more than 80 million people expected to travel for the holiday, according to reports citing CNN. On the East Coast, the storm brought heavy rain, strong winds and coastal flooding; the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York had already been postponed to Saturday for the first time ever because of the weather, and New Jersey's governor had declared a statewide state of emergency. On the West Coast, powerful waves had destroyed part of a pier in Pacifica, south of San Francisco, and high tides had broken water-level records around San Diego.

Southwest Airlines and United Airlines had both warned Thanksgiving Day passengers about possible disruptions in the Northeast. New York Governor Kathy Hochul warned of heavy rain, strong winds and possible coastal flooding across parts of the state, with wind gusts of up to 60 miles per hour. In that context, the Miami runway excursion was not just a local incident: it was one more blow to a national aviation system already stretched to its limits by weather, volume and the sheer number of people trying to get home.

The scale of Thanksgiving travel in the United States is difficult to overstate. The holiday is the busiest travel period of the year, with tens of millions of Americans driving and flying to be with family. The Transportation Security Administration screens millions of passengers a day during the holiday week, and the Wednesday before Thanksgiving is traditionally the single busiest travel day of the year. Any disruption on that night ripples outwards: a ground stop in Miami means aircraft held in Atlanta, in Washington, in Houston, in Charlotte — and passengers in those cities missing connections, missing dinners, missing the holiday itself.

Miami's second runway overrun in three months

What made the FedEx incident particularly alarming was that it was not the first time a cargo jet had run off a runway at Miami International Airport in recent months. On September 6, 2026, an Amazon cargo jet also overran a runway at the same airport, killing five people on the ground and injuring several others. The Amazon plane had overshot a different runway from the one the FedEx jet used on Wednesday, but three of Miami's runways converge at the west end of the airport, so the FedEx plane ended up close to where the Amazon plane had careened across a road and struck a van filled with a plane cleaning crew.

The September crash was a tragedy: five people on the ground were killed when the cargo jet left the runway and struck the van. The FedEx incident, by contrast, ended with no injuries at all — a matter of luck as much as anything else, given that the aircraft came to rest near a public road, Northwest 67th Avenue, where traffic was passing. Video from the scene showed a white truck driving past in the foreground as the stricken jet sat on the grass, and witnesses and commentators noted with relief that nobody had been on the adjacent road when the plane came off the runway.

The proximity of the two incidents — same airport, same kind of event, less than three months apart — is bound to focus attention on Miami International Airport itself. Investigators will want to know whether there is something about the airport's runway layout, its drainage in heavy rain, or its operations during storms that contributed to both events. They will also want to know whether the heavy rain falling on Wednesday night played a role: standing water on a runway can cause an aircraft to aquaplane, losing braking grip entirely, and the flood watch in effect for the area suggests conditions were poor.

But it is important to be clear about what is not yet known. The cause of the runway overrun remains unclear, and no official has attributed it to weather, to pilot error, or to any mechanical fault. The FAA investigation will analyse data from the cockpit voice recorder, the flight data recorder, ground-based weather observation systems and detailed aircraft performance logs. Until that work is done, any explanation is speculation.

The MD-11's troubled year

The aircraft at the centre of Wednesday's incident has had a difficult year — and so has the type to which it belongs. The McDonnell Douglas MD-11 is a three-engine wide-body jet that first flew in 1990 and ended production in 2000. In its passenger days it was a familiar sight at airports around the world; in its later life it became a freighter, and FedEx became the world's largest operator of the type, with a fleet of dozens of the aircraft carrying parcels and freight across the globe.

On November 4, 2025, a UPS MD-11, Flight 2976, crashed shortly after take-off from Louisville International Airport in Kentucky. The aircraft's left engine separated from the wing as it lifted off, igniting a fire and sending the jet into a nearby industrial park. Fourteen people died in the crash, including the three pilots on board. The National Transportation Safety Board's preliminary report found cracks and fractures in the left wing's engine mount: the plane's engine had begun to come off from the rear and went over the wing as it caught fire, and the jet climbed only about 30 feet before coming down.

The crash led the FAA to issue an emergency airworthiness directive in mid-November 2025, grounding all MD-11s until they could be thoroughly inspected and fixed. All MD-11s flown by UPS, FedEx and Western Global Airlines were grounded, along with a few related DC-10s. The NTSB investigation focused on fatigue cracks in the pylon bulkhead's aft lug — the structural fitting that holds the engine to the wing — and found that Boeing had warned MD-11 operators as long ago as 2011 about a broken engine attachment, but had never flagged the failure as critical to flight safety. Boeing had issued a service bulletin identifying four previous separations of a spherical bearing assembly that helps hold the engine to the wing.

The grounding forced the two main operators of the MD-11 to make very different decisions. UPS chose not to repair its jets: on January 27, 2026, it announced it had retired all of its MD-11s — about 9 per cent of its fleet — and took a 137 million dollar after-tax charge to write them off. A UPS filing dated December 31, 2025, said it had owned or operated 26 MD-11s, all now retired, and the company is replacing them with Boeing 767s.

FedEx took the opposite path. Working closely with Boeing, it developed a fix centred on replacing specific bearings within the aircraft's engine pylons — a redesigned bearing for the pylon aft bulkhead, paired with a rigorous inspection of the aft bulkhead structure. The problem was traced to a bearing in the lug that holds the engine pylon to the wing, and operators must install a replacement structural part before a jet can fly again. The FAA said it approved Boeing's plan for returning MD-11s to service safely after an extensive review, and FedEx put the three-engine cargo jet back into service in May 2026. Its first flights back included a short test flight out of Memphis on May 9, followed by flights to Los Angeles and Miami on May 10. FedEx plans to keep flying the MD-11 until 2032, when newer, more efficient planes replace it. Western Global Airlines resumed MD-11 freighter flights on May 21, 2026, after the FAA approved Boeing's inspection and repair programme.

The NTSB held a two-day public hearing on the Louisville crash on May 19 and 20, 2026, but has not yet ruled on the probable cause. That means that when the FedEx jet ran off the runway in Miami on Wednesday night, the investigation into the crash that grounded the fleet was still open — and the type was still, in the eyes of many in aviation, under a cloud.

It is essential to be precise here: the Miami incident was a runway overrun on landing, not an engine separation on take-off. Nothing in the reporting so far suggests that the pylon problem that caused the Louisville crash had anything to do with what happened in Miami. The two events involve the same aircraft type, but they are different kinds of accident with, on the available evidence, different causes. The heavy rain and the flood watch are, at this stage, the most obvious factors in Miami. But the coincidence — an MD-11 in trouble at a major airport, six months after the type's return to service — is certain to be part of the conversation in the days ahead, and investigators will be under pressure to establish quickly whether Wednesday's incident was a matter of weather and runway conditions or something more.

What happens next

The FAA investigation into Flight 910 is now under way. Investigators will recover the cockpit voice recorder and the flight data recorder, which will tell them exactly what the pilots did and said in the final minutes of the flight, how fast the aircraft was travelling when it touched down, where on the runway it landed, and how hard the crew braked. They will examine the runway itself — its surface, its drainage, the amount of standing water on it — and they will review the weather data for the exact moment of the landing. They will also examine the aircraft: its brakes, its thrust reversers, its tyres, and the condition of the runway overrun area where it came to rest.

FedEx, for its part, will conduct its own internal review alongside the official investigation, and the airline will be cooperating with the FAA and the NTSB. The aircraft itself will need to be inspected and, if it is to fly again, repaired and recertified. Whether it can be towed back onto the pavement and flown out, or whether it will need more extensive work, was not immediately clear.

For Miami International Airport, the priority in the hours after the incident was restoring normal operations. The partial reopening using Runway 9R was a first step, but the full resumption of service — with all runways open and the backlog of delayed flights cleared — was expected to take time. The airport's statement thanking passengers for their patience was a familiar one, but on Thanksgiving Eve it carried a particular weight: the people waiting were not just delayed, they were at risk of missing the one holiday of the year that most Americans refuse to miss.

For the aviation industry, the incident is a reminder of how thin the margins are. A runway overrun is one of the most common kinds of aviation accident, and also one of the most dangerous: an aircraft that leaves the pavement at speed can strike buildings, vehicles, fences and people, or it can break apart. That Wednesday's incident ended with no injuries is a credit to the crew, to the emergency responders, and to a certain amount of luck. That it happened at all — at the same airport, on the same kind of event, less than three months after five people were killed — is a question that investigators, regulators and the airport itself will have to answer.

A night to remember for the wrong reasons

Thanksgiving is a holiday built around being together, and for millions of Americans the journey home is part of the ritual. The Wednesday before Thanksgiving is the night when the country's airports are at their fullest, its roads at their busiest, and its families at their most expectant. A FedEx cargo jet sliding off a runway in Miami may seem, at first glance, like a footnote to that story — a freight flight, no passengers, no injuries, no fire. But the ground stop it triggered, the hours of delays it caused, and the questions it raised about safety at one of America's busiest airports make it something more than a footnote.

It was, in the end, a night of two stories. One was the story of a crew who walked away from an aircraft that had left the runway, and of emergency responders who arrived quickly and found nobody to rescue. The other was the story of thousands of travellers who spent Thanksgiving Eve waiting — in departure lounges, on tarmacs, in cars heading to airports that could not take their flights — because a single aircraft, on a single runway, in heavy rain, did not stop in time. Both stories are true. Both will be remembered, for different reasons, by the people who lived through them. And both will be part of the investigation that now begins, as the FAA, FedEx and the airport try to work out what happened on Runway 26R — and how to make sure it does not happen again.
