TITLE: SAP Is About to Cut 10,000 Jobs — Europe's Biggest Software Company Is Rebuilding Itself for the AI Era LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: SAP is weighing at least 10,000 job cuts — 9% of its workforce — as CEO Christian Klein rebuilds Europe's biggest software firm around AI. ---BODY---

Christian Klein, chief executive of SAP. Klein is pushing to turn Europe's most valuable software company into an AI-first business — and Bloomberg reports the plan under discussion would eliminate at least 10,000 positions, about 9 per cent of the workforce. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

SAP, the German software giant that runs the back offices of much of the world's biggest companies, is preparing to cut at least 10,000 jobs as it rebuilds itself for the artificial intelligence era. The plan, reported by Bloomberg on Thursday, November 26, would wipe out at least 9 per cent of the company's global workforce of more than 110,000 people — and it would be the second time in less than three years that SAP has restructured on this scale.

The cuts have not been finalised. The number could still change after negotiations with employee representatives, according to the people familiar with the matter who spoke to Bloomberg. But the direction of travel is unmistakable: chief executive Christian Klein is pushing to turn SAP into an AI-first company, embedding artificial intelligence into its software and changing how the company develops and sells its products. Product development, engineering and sales are among the departments under review.

SAP declined to comment on whether any job cuts are planned. In a statement to Bloomberg, the company said only that it "continuously reviews its organization to ensure it remains aligned with its strategy and customer needs." That is the standard language companies use when a plan is real but not yet announced. The Financial Times, citing its own people familiar with the matter, reported that the plans have yet to be discussed with employee representatives at all — which means the works councils in Germany, where SAP is headquartered, will have their say before anything becomes final.

For the 110,000 people who work at SAP, and for the tens of thousands more whose jobs depend on the SAP ecosystem of consultants, partners and customers, this is the biggest corporate story of the week. For everyone else, it is something else: the clearest sign yet that the AI revolution has moved from the demo stage to the org chart. The companies that build AI are now restructuring themselves around it — and the jobs are going first.

What Bloomberg reported — and what it means

The Bloomberg report, published on Thursday, November 26, is specific about the scale and vague about the timing, which is exactly how these stories usually work. SAP is "preparing a plan" to eliminate at least 10,000 positions. The word "at least" matters: the final number could be higher. The restructuring would affect at least 9 per cent of the global workforce. And the total number of cuts "hasn't been finalized and might still change after labor negotiations," the people familiar with the matter said.

What is being restructured is just as important as how many jobs are going. The plan is aimed at reorganising product development and sales around AI while protecting SAP's growth in cloud software, according to the reports. In plain language: SAP wants fewer people building software the old way and selling it the old way, and more of the company's energy — and money — pointed at AI products. The departments reportedly under review are product development, engineering and sales, which together make up the core of the company.

This is not a company in trouble cutting costs to survive. SAP is Europe's most valuable technology company, and its cloud business has been growing. The restructuring is offensive, not defensive: Klein wants SAP to be an AI-first company, and he is willing to reshape the workforce to get there. That distinction matters, because it tells you this is not a one-off. When a healthy, profitable company cuts a tenth of its workforce to reposition itself, other healthy, profitable companies watch — and copy.

The timing also fits a pattern. Bloomberg noted that SAP has historically announced major restructuring programmes in late January, alongside its annual financial results. If that pattern holds, the formal announcement could come in January 2027, with the details — which roles, which countries, what severance — following after negotiations with employee representatives. Until then, the 10,000 figure is a plan, not a promise. But plans at this scale, reported by Bloomberg with multiple sources, rarely shrink to nothing.

Ten thousand jobs, twice in three years

The most striking thing about the reported plan is not the number. It is the repetition. SAP undertook a major workforce restructuring in 2024, when it announced plans to eliminate approximately 10,000 positions. Now, less than three years later, it is reportedly preparing to do it again at the same scale. Two restructurings of 10,000 roles each in under three years would mean roughly a fifth of the company's workforce turned over through restructuring alone — a remarkable churn for a company that has spent five decades selling stability to the world's biggest enterprises.

The 2024 programme was framed differently. It was presented as a restructuring that would affect around 8,000 roles, largely through voluntary leave programmes and reskilling, as SAP shifted resources toward AI and cloud. The reported 2026 plan is described in blunter terms: eliminating at least 10,000 positions. The language of "elimination" rather than "restructuring" suggests a harder edge this time — fewer voluntary exits, more outright cuts — though that will only become clear when the formal plan is published and negotiated.

There is a logic to the repetition, and it is the logic of the AI transition itself. In 2024, SAP was beginning to reposition around AI. In 2026, the repositioning is accelerating, and the company has had two years to see which roles AI can genuinely replace and which it cannot. The second restructuring is the consequence of the first one's lesson: that AI does not just change what a software company sells, it changes how many people the company needs to sell it.

It is also worth noting what has changed inside SAP in 2026. The company reorganised its executive board twice this year as part of Klein's push to promote an AI-first strategy, according to the reports. Leadership changes at the top usually precede workforce changes below. A board reshuffled twice in a year is a board being rebuilt to execute a strategy — and the workforce restructuring is the execution.

Who is Christian Klein — and what is his AI bet?

Christian Klein has been SAP's chief executive since 2020, and he has spent his tenure trying to drag a company founded in 1972 — a company built on on-premise enterprise software sold in multi-year deals — into the cloud and now into AI. He is 45 years old, a SAP lifer who joined the company as a student in 1999, and his bet is that SAP's future is not in selling software licences but in selling AI-powered business processes: software that does the work, not just records it.

The AI-first strategy Klein is pushing has three visible parts. First, embed AI capabilities into SAP's existing enterprise applications — the finance, supply chain, human resources and procurement software that runs the world's biggest companies. Second, change how SAP develops software, using AI tools to write, test and maintain code with fewer engineers. Third, change how SAP sells software, with AI handling more of the sales process and customer support that used to require large teams of people.

Each of those three parts has a workforce implication. Embedding AI into products means product teams need AI specialists more than they need traditional developers. Using AI to write code means fewer engineers per product. Using AI in sales means fewer salespeople per customer. The reported restructuring — product development, engineering and sales — maps exactly onto those three parts. This is not a random 9 per cent cut across the company. It is a targeted reshaping of the three functions AI touches most directly.

Klein's bet is also a bet on timing. SAP's cloud revenue has been growing, and the company has been telling investors that AI will accelerate that growth. But AI products take time to generate revenue at the scale of the old licence business, and in the meantime the cost base has to be reshaped. Cutting 10,000 roles is how a company funds a transition: the savings from the old workforce pay for the new AI workforce, the new AI products, and the margin investors expect while the transition is under way.

Why Germany — and Europe — is watching this so closely

SAP is not just any company. It is Germany's most valuable company by market value and Europe's most valuable technology company. It is headquartered in Walldorf, a small town in south-west Germany, and it employs tens of thousands of people in Germany alone. When SAP cuts 10,000 jobs, it is a national story in Germany, not just a business story. The works councils and trade unions that represent SAP's German employees have real power under German labour law, and any restructuring plan has to be negotiated with them. That is why the reports are careful to say the number could change after labour negotiations: in Germany, it almost certainly will.

The German context also explains why the plan has not been finalised. Under German law, a company planning large-scale redundancies must consult with employee representatives and, in many cases, negotiate a social plan covering severance, retraining and redeployment. SAP's employee representatives have not yet been formally consulted, according to the Financial Times, which means the process is still at an early stage. The 10,000 figure is the management plan; the negotiated outcome could be different — fewer forced redundancies, more voluntary departures, more retraining into AI roles.

But the German context cuts the other way too. Germany's economy has been struggling, and its industrial giants — from carmakers to chemical companies — have all been cutting jobs or warning about competitiveness. SAP cutting 10,000 roles would be the biggest single restructuring by a German technology company in years, and it would land in a political debate about whether Germany is losing its industrial edge. The timing, just before the Christmas break and ahead of annual results in January, will be watched closely in Berlin.

For Europe more broadly, SAP is a test case. The European Union has spent years trying to build a European technology champion that can compete with the American giants — Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Oracle. SAP is the closest thing Europe has. If SAP's AI-first restructuring works, it becomes the model for European tech. If it turns into a brutal round of job cuts that damages the company's engineering culture, it becomes a warning. Either way, what happens at SAP in the next few months will be studied by every large European employer.

The AI jobs question, in one company

SAP's reported plan lands in the same week as a stark warning from JP Morgan, which estimated that artificial intelligence could displace around 24 million knowledge-worker jobs worldwide over the next five years. The two stories are the same story told at different scales: JP Morgan is the forecast, SAP is the first big company acting on it. And SAP is not alone. Across the technology industry, the pattern is now familiar: a company announces an AI strategy, then announces a restructuring, then announces that the restructuring is because of AI.

The pattern is visible in the numbers. In 2024, SAP's restructuring affected around 10,000 positions. In 2025, companies across the tech industry cut tens of thousands of roles while simultaneously increasing their AI spending. In 2026, SAP is reportedly preparing to cut another 10,000. The direction is consistent: as AI tools get better at writing code, answering customer queries, drafting documents and analysing data, the companies that build those tools need fewer people to do those jobs themselves.

What makes SAP different from a pure AI company is that its customers are the world's biggest employers. When SAP embeds AI into the software that runs a carmaker's supply chain or a bank's finance department, the AI does not just change SAP's workforce. It changes the customer's workforce too. The 10,000 jobs at SAP are the first-order effect. The second-order effect — the jobs that disappear at SAP's customers when their back-office work is automated — is much larger, and much harder to count.

That is why the SAP story matters beyond SAP. It is the first time a company that sells the software of work itself has said, in effect, that AI means it needs a tenth fewer people to do its own work. The customers who buy that software will draw the obvious conclusion about their own workforces. The restructuring at SAP is not just a cost-cutting exercise. It is a signal to every SAP customer in the world: the AI transition is here, and it has a headcount number attached.

What happens next — the calendar

The next steps are procedural, but they matter. First, SAP's management will have to present the plan to employee representatives and begin formal consultations. In Germany, this is not a formality: the works council can delay, challenge and reshape the plan, and the negotiations can take months. Second, the company will have to decide the mix: how many roles are eliminated outright, how many through voluntary departures, and how many people are retrained into AI roles inside the company. Third, the formal announcement is likely to come in late January, alongside SAP's annual results, if the company's historical pattern holds.

Investors will be watching the January announcement closely. SAP's share price has been strong, and the market has rewarded the company's cloud growth and its AI positioning. A restructuring that cuts 9 per cent of the workforce is usually good for the share price in the short term — lower costs, higher margins — and the market may well welcome it. But investors will also ask whether SAP can cut that deeply without damaging the engineering talent it needs to build the AI products that justify the cuts in the first place. Cutting the people who build the future to pay for the future is a delicate operation.

Employees will be watching too, and not just at SAP. The technology industry has been through wave after wave of layoffs since 2022, and each wave has been justified by a different reason: over-hiring during the pandemic, rising interest rates, the shift to cloud, and now AI. For workers, the justifications blur into a single experience: the job that was safe last year is not safe this year. SAP's reported plan will deepen that anxiety across the German tech sector and beyond, because SAP was supposed to be the stable one — the company that sold stability to others.

There is one more group watching: SAP's customers. A restructuring of this scale raises a practical question for the companies that depend on SAP software to run their businesses. Will support get worse? Will product development slow down? Will the salespeople and consultants they know disappear? SAP will have to answer those questions while it negotiates with its own workforce. The company's answer so far — that it "continuously reviews its organization" — is not an answer at all. Customers, employees and investors will all want a real one, and they will want it in January.

The bigger picture: AI is no longer a product, it is a workforce strategy

Step back from SAP, and the story is bigger than one company. For three years, the AI debate has been about what the technology can do: write essays, generate images, pass exams, write code. The SAP story is about what the technology does to the companies that make it. AI has moved from being a product that companies sell to being a reason that companies restructure. That is a different kind of revolution, and it is happening faster than most people expected.

Consider the sequence. In 2022 and 2023, tech companies laid off hundreds of thousands of people and blamed over-hiring during the pandemic. In 2024, SAP restructured 10,000 roles and blamed the shift to AI and cloud. In 2025, the layoffs continued across the industry even as AI spending soared. In 2026, SAP is reportedly preparing to cut another 10,000 roles, explicitly as part of an AI-first restructuring. The excuse has become the strategy. AI is no longer the reason companies give after the fact. It is the reason they plan in advance.

The JP Morgan report published this week puts a number on where this leads. If half of potential enterprise AI spending goes towards replacing jobs that would otherwise have been filled, and AI costs about half as much as the workers it replaces, the bank estimates that around 24 million knowledge-worker jobs worldwide could be displaced over five years. SAP's 10,000 is a rounding error in that number. But it is a rounding error with a name, a face, and a headquarters in Walldorf — and that makes it real in a way that 24 million never can be.

The optimistic version of this story is that AI creates more jobs than it destroys, and that the people SAP lets go will find better jobs building, selling or using AI. The pessimistic version is that the transition is too fast, the new jobs require skills the old workers do not have, and the gains flow to shareholders while the costs fall on employees. The honest version is that nobody knows yet, and SAP's restructuring will be one of the first large-scale tests. What happens to those 10,000 people — how many are retrained, how many find new work, how many are simply let go — will tell us more about the AI economy than any forecast.

What to watch in the coming weeks

There are five things to watch as this story develops. First, whether SAP confirms the plan or continues to decline comment. A formal announcement in January would confirm the scale; silence would suggest the plan is being reshaped by negotiations. Second, the negotiated number. German labour law gives employee representatives real leverage, and the final figure could be lower than 10,000 — or the same number reached through voluntary departures rather than forced cuts.

Third, the mix of roles. If the cuts fall mainly on sales and support, SAP is betting that AI can sell and serve customers. If they fall mainly on engineering, SAP is betting that AI can write the software. Which functions are hit will tell you what Klein really believes AI can do. Fourth, the retraining offer. SAP's 2024 restructuring included reskilling programmes. Whether the 2026 plan offers the same — and whether employees take it up — will determine whether this is a transition or simply a cull.

Fifth, and most importantly, whether other companies follow. SAP is the first European tech giant to restructure at this scale explicitly around AI. It will not be the last. The companies that watch SAP's share price, its employee reaction and its customer response in January will decide whether to do the same. In that sense, the 10,000 jobs at SAP are not just 10,000 jobs. They are the opening move in the AI restructuring of the European economy — and the rest of the industry is watching to see how it plays out.

SAP built its business on a promise: that its software would make the world's biggest companies more efficient. Now the company is applying that promise to itself. The question is whether the efficiency it finds is worth the people it loses along the way. For 10,000 SAP employees and their families, that question is not abstract. It is the Christmas they are about to have, and the January they are about to face.
