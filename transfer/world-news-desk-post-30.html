TITLE: 169 Pages, 17 Items Seized, Zero Charges — Unsealed Warrant Files Expose the Decades-Long Probe Into Ilhan Omar LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Unsealed files reveal a federal probe into Rep. Ilhan Omar stretching back to 1980 — 169 pages, a 67-page affidavit still sealed, no charges yet. ---BODY---

Representative Ilhan Omar in her official House portrait. Federal prosecutors say they believe she committed crimes "over decades" — but no charges have been filed. Photo: United States House of Representatives, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

A federal judge has unsealed more than 160 pages of heavily redacted court records from the search of Representative Ilhan Omar's Washington, D.C., home — and the files offer the clearest look yet at a federal investigation into a sitting member of Congress that prosecutors say stretches back to 1980. The documents, released on Monday night, November 23, 2026, by Chief U.S. District Judge James E. Boasberg, confirm that investigators are examining alleged immigration fraud spanning decades, that agents seized 17 items from the Minnesota Democrat's home on October 12, and that a 67-page affidavit at the heart of the case remains sealed. What the files do not contain is any criminal charge. Omar, who won re-election to a fifth term in the House just weeks after the search, has denied all wrongdoing, and prosecutors describe themselves as still "in the throes of a pre-indictment investigation." For a probe that has already consumed the capital's attention for six weeks, the unsealing raises as many questions as it answers — about what the government believes, about what it can prove, and about what happens next to one of the most prominent members of Congress.

The release came after a lawsuit by a coalition of 15 news organisations — led by The New York Times — and the watchdog group Judicial Watch, which asked the court to make the warrant records public. Judge Boasberg ordered the files released on Monday night, despite a last-minute motion filed the same day by Jeanine Pirro, the U.S. Attorney for the District of Columbia, seeking to extend the sealing deadline. Roughly half of the nearly 170 pages released are mostly or entirely blacked out. But the unredacted portions expose several striking details about the Justice Department's probe, and the language prosecutors chose is unusually blunt. In one filing, the government wrote: "The United States believes ILHAN has committed [redacted] crimes over decades — beginning long before she was ever in Congress." In another, prosecutors said the case had "anything to do" with nothing in Omar's professional life — a point the government has made repeatedly, and one that matters enormously to the politics of the case.

The investigation, according to people familiar with the probe, is being built jointly by prosecutors in the D.C. U.S. Attorney's Office, led by Pirro, and the Minnesota U.S. Attorney's Office. It focuses on possible immigration fraud committed by Omar or by family members who fled war-torn Somalia in the 1990s — and on a set of questions about her personal history that have followed her for years: the year she was born, the circumstances of her father's naturalisation, and a marriage that ended in divorce in 2017. None of it, prosecutors insist, concerns her work in the House. Associate Deputy Attorney General Alessandra Serano made that explicit in a court filing, writing that nothing in the search warrant affidavit, the briefing, or the oral argument "indicates that the United States's case has anything to do with ILHAN's professional life." That distinction is the government's answer to the charge, made by Omar's supporters, that the investigation is political. It is also, critics note, a distinction that does not make the case any less explosive.

What happened on October 12

The search itself took place on October 12, 2026, when agents from Homeland Security Investigations — the investigative arm of the Department of Homeland Security — executed a judicially authorised search warrant at Omar's Washington, D.C., residence. The warrant had been applied for on October 2, and a judge granted approval a week later. In a statement at the time, the department said only that "Homeland Security Investigations executed a judicially authorized search warrant secured by the Department of Justice" and that "this enforcement action is part of an ongoing federal investigation." The warrant was initially sealed by a federal judge in early October, who cited "reasonable grounds to believe the disclosure will result in flight from prosecution, destruction of or tampering with evidence, intimidation of potential witnesses, and serious jeopardy to the investigation."

The unsealed records now show what the agents were authorised to take: papers, records, photographs, documents and any other physical objects, along with thumb drives, external hard drives, computers, other electronic devices, and all cellular phones registered or used by Omar. Omar herself later described what was actually removed. In an October interview on MS NOW's "All In with Chris Hayes," she said the federal agents "took 17 items" from her home, including several cellphones and notebooks. Her passport was seized, along with her computers. DNA evidence was also obtained, according to the same account. Other items on the list, as described in later reporting, included old phones belonging to her and a daughter, a Somali-language children's book, and a vintage camera gifted to her husband. The final inventory of seized property remains sealed — one of the many parts of the file that the public still cannot see.

The scale of the unsealing is itself a story. The released collection spans roughly 169 pages, and about half of it is redacted to the point of being unreadable. The 67-page affidavit in support of the search warrant — the document that would lay out, in the government's own words, why it believed a crime had been committed and why a search of a congresswoman's home was justified — remains entirely under seal. Judge Boasberg agreed with prosecutors at a court hearing last Tuesday that the "vast majority" of entries related to the case should stay sealed. What was released, then, is a fragment: enough to establish the scope and the timeframe of the investigation, but not enough to judge its strength. That is the tension at the heart of this story. The government has said, in writing, that it believes a sitting member of Congress committed crimes over decades. It has not said, in public, what evidence supports that belief.

"1980 to the present"

The most striking detail in the unsealed files is the timeframe. Court briefs show that federal prosecutors applied for the search warrant seeking all "records and information that constitute fruits, contraband, evidence, and instrumentalities of violations of [REDACTED]" occurring between "1980 to the present." That window stretches back even before Omar's listed birth year — reported as 1981 or October 1982; she does not have a birth certificate. The breadth of the range is extraordinary. It covers her entire life, her family's flight from Somalia, their years as refugees, their arrival in the United States, and her rise from a community organiser in Minneapolis to one of the most recognisable figures in American politics.

Investigators have examined, among other things, whether Omar or her father intentionally misstated her birth date on immigration documents. The question matters because of a specific legal consequence: if her birth year was recorded as 1982 rather than 1981, it would have affected her eligibility for citizenship as a minor when her father naturalised in 2000. That is the kind of detail that sounds technical — a one-year discrepancy on a form, filed decades ago, by a family that had fled a civil war — but that can carry enormous legal weight in an immigration fraud case. Prosecutors are also said to be examining her father's naturalisation file and the age entries within it, and whether information was omitted or misrepresented. The files do not say what the investigators have concluded. They say only that these are among the questions being asked.

The investigation is also revisiting the circumstances of Omar's 2009 marriage to Ahmed Nur Said Elmi, a British national. Public records show that Omar legally married Elmi in February 2009 in Eden Prairie, Minnesota. The pair separated in 2011 and divorced in December 2017. Elmi sought to convert a marriage-based green card from conditional to permanent status, then withdrew the application after authorities asked for more evidence, according to a former immigration official cited in earlier coverage. The marriage has been the subject of long-standing allegations — which Omar has publicly denied — that Elmi was her brother, and that the marriage was entered into to help him immigrate to the United States. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin gave those allegations their most forceful official airing in September, when he told podcaster Benny Johnson: "We know that she married her brother to bring him to the United States. We know he lives now in London." Mullin added that there "may be more to this, the whole family migration, and possibly some illegal activity for them to come to the United States to begin with."

Those are serious claims, and they are claims that Omar has denied for years. What the unsealed files add is the confirmation that the government is actively investigating them — not as rumour, but as a federal case, with a search warrant, a grand jury, and subpoenas. Reports indicate that authorities have issued more than a dozen subpoenas to family members to provide testimony before a grand jury in Minnesota. Investigators are also reportedly looking into tax filings from the period in which Omar was legally married to Elmi, as well as the immigration documentation of several of her relatives. The probe, in other words, is not narrow. It reaches across her family, across decades, and across the paper trail of a refugee family's life in America.

The judge, the unsealing, and the fight over secrecy

The unsealing itself became a legal fight with its own political edge. The lawsuit that forced the release was brought by more than a dozen news organisations, and the Justice Department fought to keep the records sealed. On Friday, federal prosecutors filed a notice on the docket noting compliance with a judge's order to submit the search warrant records with redactions for any sensitive investigative information. On Monday, Pirro filed a last-minute motion seeking to extend the sealing deadline. Judge Boasberg ordered the release anyway — and the Justice Department's own lawyers then argued that the judge had contradicted his own earlier decision in the Trump case by unsealing the records in Omar's case. That argument, reported by Fox News, captures the strange politics of the moment: the same department that is investigating a Democratic congresswoman is also, in a separate matter, defending the secrecy of records related to the president.

Boasberg's decision to unseal only part of the file reflects the balance courts strike in cases like this. On one side is the public's right to know what the government is doing when it searches the home of an elected official. On the other is the government's interest in protecting an ongoing investigation — the witnesses who have not yet testified, the evidence that has not yet been gathered, the targets who might destroy documents or flee if they knew what the government had. The judge's compromise — release the warrant and the surrounding filings, keep the affidavit and most of the docket sealed — is a familiar one. It is also one that leaves the public with a picture that is, by design, incomplete. The most important document in the case, the 67-page affidavit, is the one document nobody outside the government and the court has read.

That incompleteness has consequences. Without the affidavit, it is impossible to know what evidence the government presented to the judge to justify the search — what documents, what testimony, what records led a federal judge to conclude that there was probable cause to search a congresswoman's home. Without the affidavit, it is impossible to know whether the investigation is built on a solid foundation of documents or on a thin set of allegations that have been circulating in public for years. And without the affidavit, the public debate about the case is, inevitably, a debate about shadows: about what the redactions might hide, about what the government might know, and about whether the probe is a legitimate investigation or a political weapon.

What Omar has said — and what she has not

Omar has denied all wrongdoing. She has said little about the substance of the investigation since the search, beyond describing what was taken from her home and insisting that she has done nothing wrong. Her public posture has been one of defiance mixed with restraint: she has not, in her public statements, addressed the specific allegations about her birth date, her father's naturalisation, or her marriage to Elmi in the detail that the unsealed files now invite. That restraint is, in part, a matter of legal prudence. A person under federal investigation is rarely well served by litigating the case in public. But it also leaves a vacuum — and in that vacuum, the government's language, however redacted, speaks loudest.

The political context matters, too. Omar won re-election to a fifth term in the House just weeks after the search, in the midterm elections of early November 2026 — elections in which Democrats won 258 seats in the House, their largest majority since 1992. Her victory, in the immediate aftermath of a federal search of her home, was a striking result. It suggested that, at least in her district, voters were not prepared to abandon her on the basis of an investigation that had produced no charges. It also suggested that the case, whatever its legal merits, is now entangled with the politics of a new Democratic majority in the House — a majority that will, in January, take control of the committees that oversee the Justice Department itself.

That entanglement is the reason the government's insistence that the case has nothing to do with Omar's professional life matters so much. If the investigation were about her work in Congress — about votes, about committee business, about the exercise of her office — it would raise constitutional questions about the separation of powers and the Speech or Debate Clause, which protects members of Congress from being questioned about their legislative acts. By insisting that the case concerns only her private past, the government has tried to keep those questions out of the picture. But the insistence cuts both ways. If the case is about her private past, then it is about events that happened years — in some cases, decades — before she entered Congress, and the question becomes why the government is pursuing them now, with the full force of a federal search warrant, against a sitting member of the House.

The long shadow of the allegations

The allegations at the centre of the probe are not new. Questions about Omar's marriage to Elmi, and about the circumstances of her family's immigration, have circulated in public for years — in conservative media, in opposition research, and in the commentary of her political opponents. What is new is that the government has now, in a federal court filing, put its name to the proposition that she committed crimes "over decades." That is a significant escalation, and it is one that carries real legal risk for the government if the case does not result in charges. A pre-indictment investigation that produces no indictment, after a search of a congresswoman's home and the seizure of her passport, her phones and her computers, would be a serious matter — not only for Omar, but for the Justice Department that authorised it.

That risk is one reason the case is being watched so closely by lawyers on both sides of the aisle. A search warrant requires probable cause — a showing, to a judge, that there is a fair probability that evidence of a crime will be found in the place to be searched. The judge who approved the warrant on October 9, 2026, was satisfied that the government had met that standard. But probable cause is not proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and it is not an indictment. Many investigations that begin with a search warrant end without charges. The government has not said whether it expects to bring charges, or when, or what those charges might be. It has said only that it is "in the throes of a pre-indictment investigation" — a phrase that suggests activity, but not imminence.

The other reason the case is being watched is the precedent it sets. Searches of the homes of sitting members of Congress are rare. They are rare because the Constitution gives members of Congress special protections, because the political consequences are enormous, and because the Justice Department has traditionally been cautious about actions that could be seen as interfering with the legislative branch. The search of Omar's home, and now the partial unsealing of the records behind it, is a test of how that caution holds up in a moment of intense political polarisation — and of whether the public will accept the government's assurance that the case is about paperwork, not politics.

What happens next

Several things will determine what happens next. The first is the grand jury in Minnesota, where family members have been subpoenaed to testify. Grand jury proceedings are secret, and it is impossible to know what the witnesses are being asked, or what they are saying. But the existence of the subpoenas — more than a dozen, according to reports — suggests that the investigation is active, and that prosecutors are gathering testimony from the people closest to the events in question. The second is the affidavit. If Judge Boasberg eventually orders it unsealed, in whole or in part, the public will finally be able to judge the strength of the government's case. If it remains sealed, the debate will continue in the dark.

The third is the question of charges. The government has not filed any, and it has not said when — or whether — it will. That silence is, in itself, a fact worth noting. A federal investigation that has searched a congresswoman's home, seized her passport and her phones, obtained her DNA, and subpoenaed her family, and that still, six weeks later, has produced no charge, is an investigation whose outcome is genuinely uncertain. It may be that the prosecutors are building a careful, methodical case, and that charges will come in time. It may be that the evidence, once gathered, does not support the allegations. And it may be that the investigation will end, quietly, without an indictment — leaving Omar vindicated, and the Justice Department with difficult questions to answer.

The fourth is the political calendar. The new Congress takes its seats in January, with Democrats holding 258 seats in the House. The committees that oversee the Justice Department will be chaired by Democrats, and the investigation of a Democratic congresswoman by a Justice Department led by a Republican administration will be a subject of intense scrutiny. Omar's colleagues, who have so far been measured in their public comments, will face a choice: to defend a member of their caucus against what they may see as a political prosecution, or to wait for the legal process to run its course. That choice, like the investigation itself, will be shaped by what the unsealed files do — and do not — show.

For now, the public record consists of this: 169 pages, half of them blacked out. A 67-page affidavit, still sealed. Seventeen items taken from a home in Washington. A timeframe of 1980 to the present. A congresswoman who denies everything and has just been re-elected. And a government that says it believes she committed crimes over decades — but that has not, yet, charged her with any of them. The unsealing was supposed to bring clarity. What it has brought, instead, is a sharper picture of the questions that remain. The answers, if they come, will come from the grand jury, from the court, and from the prosecutors who must decide, in the end, whether the case they have built is strong enough to bring before a jury. Until then, the probe into Ilhan Omar remains what it has been since October 12: a federal investigation, conducted largely in secret, into a sitting member of Congress — and a test of how the American system handles the moment when the law turns its attention to one of its own lawmakers.

Why this case matters beyond one congresswoman

The investigation of Ilhan Omar matters for reasons that go beyond the fate of one politician. It matters because it tests the boundary between legitimate law enforcement and political targeting — a boundary that has become one of the most contested questions in American public life. Every administration insists that its Justice Department is independent, and every opposition insists that it is not. The test of that independence is not what officials say. It is what they do, and whether what they do can withstand scrutiny. A case built on documents, on testimony, on a paper trail that can be examined in court, is a case that can be judged on its merits. A case built on allegations that have circulated for years in partisan media, and that has produced a search but no charge, is a case that invites doubt — and doubt, in a moment like this one, is itself a political fact.

It matters, too, because of who Omar is. She is one of the first two Muslim women elected to Congress, one of the most prominent members of the progressive wing of the Democratic Party, and a figure who has been the target of intense, often hostile attention since she first took office. Her supporters see the investigation as the latest chapter in a long campaign against her — a campaign that has included death threats, racist attacks, and repeated attempts to delegitimise her presence in American politics. Her critics see the investigation as the long-overdue reckoning of a politician who, they believe, has evaded scrutiny for too long. Both sides are, in a sense, arguing about the same thing: whether the law is being applied to Omar because of what she did, or because of who she is. The unsealed files do not answer that question. They confirm that the investigation is real, that it is broad, and that it is serious. They do not confirm that it is justified.

And it matters because of what it says about the state of American institutions. A federal judge ordered the release of these records because a coalition of news organisations asked him to — and because the law, in the end, requires the government to justify its secrecy. That is how the system is supposed to work. But the system also produced a release in which half the pages are blacked out, in which the most important document remains sealed, and in which the public is asked to trust the government's word that the case has nothing to do with politics. Trust, in this moment, is in short supply. The unsealing was a step toward transparency. Whether it was a sufficient step — and whether the full story will ever be told — remains to be seen. The 169 pages are a beginning, not an end. The next chapter will be written in the grand jury room, in the courtroom, and in the judgment of a public that is watching, closely, to see whether the law, applied to a member of Congress, is the same law that applies to everyone else.
