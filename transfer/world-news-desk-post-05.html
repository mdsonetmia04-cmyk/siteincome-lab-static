TITLE: COP31 Climate Talks Collapse Without a Deal — Guterres 'Deeply Disappointed' as Fossil Fuel Fight Derails Summit LABELS: World, Science SEARCH DESCRIPTION: COP31 ended without a climate deal in Antalya after the EU rejected a draft that did not mention fossil fuels. Guterres called it deeply disappointing; focus shifts to COP32. ---BODY---

UN Secretary-General António Guterres in 2025. Photo: Official White House Photo by Daniel Torok / Wikimedia Commons

United Nations climate talks ended in collapse on Sunday, November 22, 2026, after two weeks of negotiations and two days of overtime failed to produce an agreement, making COP31 the first UN climate summit since Copenhagen in 2009 to close without a deal.

The failure, in Antalya, Türkiye, came down to a single phrase: fossil fuels. A draft final text presented by the summit's presidents — Türkiye and Australia — contained no mention of fossil fuels, the main driver of global warming, and no commitment to move away from them. The European Union and a coalition of other countries rejected it outright, while oil-producing states and their allies supported it. With 194 delegations unable to bridge the divide, COP31 President Murat Kurum, Türkiye's environment minister, brought the gavel down on a summit that had become the longest in the history of the climate talks.

"I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted," Kurum told the closing plenary session.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was "deeply disappointed" by the outcome. "I share the very profound frustration of all those who expected governments to rise to the moment the climate crisis demands," he said, adding that there remains "no ambiguity in the science" and that fossil fuels remain the primary driver of global warming. "The take-home message from COP31 is clear: the world must do much more to confront the climate crisis."

How the talks collapsed

The summit, the 31st United Nations Climate Change Conference, opened with modest expectations. After COP30 in Belém, Brazil, last year — which marked the 10th anniversary of the Paris Agreement — no major decisions were on the official agenda in Antalya. But Türkiye and Australia, the two co-chairs of the summit, had announced their intention to push for a framework decision addressing the major challenges: the global energy crisis, attacks on science and the failure to contain global warming to 1.5°C, the most ambitious target of the Paris Agreement.

That framework decision, which became known as the "Antalya Pact", became the focus of intense attention and controversy. Reaching agreement among 194 parties requires progress on at least three fronts simultaneously: reducing greenhouse gas emissions, adapting to global warming and securing financial resources. In Antalya, as one summary of the negotiations put it, the debate was reduced to a negotiation over four elements: one phrase, "fossil fuels"; one technology, carbon capture; one target, 35 percent electrification; and one figure, the amount of adaptation funding.

The draft text considered too weak by its critics had acknowledged the planet's projected temperature overshoot — the expectation that global warming will exceed 1.5°C — and called for faster development of carbon-removal technologies to eventually reverse it, something oil-producing countries had been keen to include. But it said nothing about the root cause of the crisis, made no mention of countries' legal obligations to prevent and repair climate harm, as clarified by the International Court of Justice, and dropped a $120 billion target for funds to help poorer countries adapt to climate change.

As the talks stretched two days into overtime — making COP31 the longest on record — the venue began to empty. Exhibition stands were dismantled and delegations thinned out. In the final plenary session, with the conference centre already partly deserted, the European Union, Panama, Switzerland, the United Kingdom and the group of least developed countries announced that they were rejecting the text. The text, by contrast, received support from Russia, China and the Arab countries.

"An obvious issue is the transition away from fossil fuels, where some parties insisted that must be mentioned. Other parties insisted that if it was mentioned, they would veto," one delegate summary noted. That veto threat was the summit's central fault line, and neither side would cross it.

The European Union's rejection

The European Union's position was stated bluntly. The bloc said it would not endorse a deal that "does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change, and we will not accept negotiating backward in time, and we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuel". A spokesman added that the draft deal "does not begin to address the climate emergency that we face".

Ireland's environment minister, Darragh O'Brien, speaking for the EU's concerns, said it was "unconscionable that we would have a climate act that makes no reference to fossil fuel, no reference to transitioning away from fossil fuels". The EU's stance reflected a judgement that a weak deal was worse than no deal — that signing a text which omitted the central cause of the crisis would legitimise backsliding and make future progress harder.

The opposing camp — led by Saudi Arabia and other large oil-producing countries — argued that fossil fuels will play a role in the economy for the foreseeable future, and lobbied to keep any mention of them out of the text, according to observers at the summit. Their position was not simply obstructionist: for economies built on oil and gas exports, a formal commitment to phase out fossil fuels is an existential threat, and they have resisted such language at every COP since the Paris Agreement was signed.

Between those two positions sat the countries most vulnerable to climate change — small island states, least developed countries and African nations — which wanted both strong language on fossil fuels and serious money for adaptation. The draft's removal of the $120 billion adaptation finance target was, for them, as damaging as the omission of fossil fuels. They were being asked to accept a text that offered neither the language nor the money they needed, and they joined the rejection.

Guterres and the frustration of the UN

For António Guterres, the collapse was a personal as well as an institutional blow. The Secretary-General has made climate action the defining cause of his tenure, and he has repeatedly warned that the climate crisis is outrunning the world's response. At COP31 he said the crisis was outrunning governments, and after the collapse he criticised powerful fossil fuel interests for obstructing climate action, accusing them of distorting scientific evidence, delaying policies and prioritising profits.

Guterres urged campaigners to keep pressing for action and accountability, and stressed that the failure to agree a political declaration in Antalya does not erase the obligations agreed by previous UN conferences. That point matters legally as well as politically: the Paris Agreement, the commitments made at COP26 in Glasgow to phase down coal and the COP28 agreement in Dubai to transition away from fossil fuels all remain in force. What COP31 failed to do was to advance them.

The Secretary-General's frustration was shared by the summit's own leadership. Chris Bowen, Australia's COP31 president of negotiations, said the outcome was the result of disagreements over fossil fuels and climate finance. Kurum, the COP31 president, acknowledged the impasse plainly. Neither man attempted to disguise the scale of the failure.

UN climate chief Simon Stiell, however, pointed to what had been salvaged. According to Stiell, negotiators still agreed on a just-transition package, advanced electrification initiatives and kept existing commitments to shift away from fossil fuels intact, even as divisions over how fast to move derailed the summit's headline deal. Those technical achievements, he suggested, meant the summit was not a total loss — though they were small consolation against the failure of the political declaration.

Why this COP mattered

The collapse of COP31 matters for several reasons, and the first is the simplest: the climate is not waiting. The talks took place against a backdrop of worsening consequences — heat waves, wildfires, floods and powerful storms — that governments around the world are struggling to address. Every year in which the international response stalls is a year in which the damage grows and the cost of adaptation rises.

The second reason is the precedent. The last time the annual UN climate talks failed to end in an agreement was Copenhagen in 2009, a failure so damaging that it took years for the process to recover. The Paris Agreement, signed in 2015, was in part a response to Copenhagen's collapse — a more flexible framework designed to avoid the all-or-nothing dynamic that had doomed the earlier summit. COP31's failure raises the question of whether that framework is now itself under strain, and whether the consensus-based model of climate negotiation can still deliver results when the divisions are as deep as they were in Antalya.

The third reason is the signal it sends to the fossil fuel industry and to financial markets. A summit that cannot even mention fossil fuels in its final text is a summit that has, in effect, accepted the industry's terms. That outcome will be read by investors, by energy companies and by governments as evidence that the political will to constrain fossil fuels is weakening — even as the scientific case for doing so grows stronger.

And the fourth reason is the effect on the countries most exposed to climate change. For small island states facing rising seas, for African countries facing drought and for Asian nations facing floods, the failure of COP31 is not an abstract diplomatic setback. It means less money for adaptation, less certainty about the future and less reason to believe that the international community will act in time. Their frustration, voiced in the closing plenary, was among the most pointed of the summit.

The road to COP32

The focus now shifts to COP32, which will be held in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, next year. The choice of venue is itself significant: the first COP to be held in Ethiopia, and a summit that will place African climate concerns at its centre. Whether it can succeed where Antalya failed will depend on whether the divisions that derailed COP31 can be bridged in the intervening year.

Several things will shape that effort. The first is the question of finance. The $120 billion adaptation target that was dropped from the Antalya draft will return, and the pressure to agree serious funding for the countries most affected by climate change will be intense. Without money, the vulnerable countries have little reason to sign up to a deal, and the summit will stall again.

The second is the question of language. The fight over the phrase "fossil fuels" is, in the end, a fight over whether the international community is willing to name the cause of the crisis it is trying to solve. The EU and its allies have made clear they will not sign a text that omits it. The oil-producing states have made clear they will veto a text that includes it. Breaking that deadlock will require either a compromise formulation that both sides can accept, or a change in the political arithmetic that makes one side's position untenable.

The third is the broader political context. The talks in Antalya took place while wars and economic pressures competed for governments' attention, and the struggle to muster ambitious climate action reflected that competition. Whether the coming year brings a more favourable environment — or a worse one — will shape COP32's prospects as much as anything the negotiators themselves do.

And the fourth is the role of the UN itself. Guterres has been blunt about his frustration, and his warning that there is "no ambiguity in the science" is a direct challenge to the fossil fuel interests he accuses of distorting evidence. Whether his voice — and the voices of the campaigners he urged to keep pressing — can shift the politics of the next summit remains to be seen.

A setback, not an ending

The 1.5°C target and the overshoot problem

At the heart of the Antalya dispute was a number: 1.5°C. The Paris Agreement, signed in 2015, committed the world to holding global warming well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and to pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C. That lower figure was not arbitrary. Scientists concluded that the difference between 1.5°C and 2°C of warming is the difference between serious damage and catastrophic damage for many of the world's most vulnerable people — between coral reefs that survive and coral reefs that die, between island nations that endure and island nations that disappear beneath rising seas.

The draft text in Antalya acknowledged what scientists have been saying for several years: that the planet is projected to overshoot 1.5°C. Overshoot means that global temperatures will rise above the target, possibly for decades, before — if ever — coming back down. The draft called for faster development of carbon-removal technologies to eventually reverse the overshoot, something oil-producing countries had been keen to include, because carbon removal offers a way to keep producing fossil fuels while claiming that the resulting emissions can be captured later.

The problem with that logic, according to the draft's critics, is that carbon-removal technology does not yet exist at the scale required, and may never do so. The world's largest carbon-capture facilities currently remove a tiny fraction of global emissions, and the costs remain prohibitive. Betting the 1.5°C target on technologies that have not been proven at scale is, in the view of the EU and the vulnerable countries, a gamble the world cannot afford.

The overshoot question also exposes a deeper tension in the climate negotiations. The countries that have contributed most to global warming — the wealthy industrialised nations — are not the countries that suffer most from it. The countries that suffer most — small island states, least developed countries, African nations — have contributed least. The failure to agree on adaptation finance in Antalya was, for them, a reminder that the world's promises of help have repeatedly fallen short, and that the gap between what is pledged and what is delivered remains wide.

The history of COP failures

The collapse of COP31 inevitably drew comparisons with the last time the climate talks ended without an agreement: Copenhagen in 2009. COP15 was supposed to produce a binding global treaty to succeed the Kyoto Protocol. Instead, it ended in chaos, with leaders emerging from all-night negotiations with a weak political accord that was not even formally adopted. The failure was so damaging that it took years for the UN climate process to recover its credibility.

What followed Copenhagen was a slow rebuilding. The Paris Agreement of 2015 was designed to avoid Copenhagen's mistakes: instead of a single binding treaty negotiated top-down, it created a flexible framework in which each country sets its own targets and updates them over time. The approach worked, after a fashion. COP26 in Glasgow in 2021 produced the first-ever mention of coal in a COP decision — a commitment to "phase down" unabated coal power. COP28 in Dubai in 2023 went further, with countries agreeing for the first time to "transition away from fossil fuels" in energy systems.

Each of those steps was hard-won, and each was weaker than many participants wanted. But they established a direction of travel: away from fossil fuels, toward cleaner energy, with the pace of the transition contested at every summit. COP31's failure breaks that pattern. For the first time since Paris, a summit could not even mention fossil fuels in its final text — a retreat from the language agreed in Dubai, and a signal that the consensus behind the transition is fraying.

COP30 in Belém, Brazil, last year had marked the 10th anniversary of the Paris Agreement, and its symbolic weight was considerable: a climate summit in the Amazon, hosted by a country that has positioned itself as a champion of forest protection. Expectations for COP31 were initially modest by comparison, with no major decisions on the official agenda. That modesty makes the failure more, not less, significant. A summit with low expectations still could not reach agreement — suggesting that the problem is not the ambition of the agenda, but the depth of the divisions among the parties.

The question now is whether Antalya becomes another Copenhagen — a failure that damages the process for years — or a wake-up call that concentrates minds before COP32. The answer will depend on what governments do in the intervening year, and on whether the political conditions that made agreement impossible in Türkiye have changed by the time the talks reconvene in Addis Ababa.

What happens in the months before COP32 will matter as much as the summit itself. Countries are due to submit updated national climate plans, and the strength of those plans will show whether governments are serious about closing the gap that Antalya exposed. The European Union, having refused a weak deal, will face pressure to demonstrate that its stance produces a stronger one — and the oil-producing states, having blocked the text, will face pressure to show what they are willing to accept instead. The presidency of COP32, in Addis Ababa, will carry the hopes of the African continent, where the consequences of climate failure are already visible in drought, flooding and food insecurity. The world will be watching whether the lesson of Antalya is learned, or repeated.

The collapse of COP31 is the biggest setback for international climate diplomacy since Copenhagen in 2009. It leaves the world without a new political agreement on climate change, without the adaptation funding that vulnerable countries were promised and without even a formal acknowledgement in the summit's text of the fossil fuels driving the crisis.

But it is a setback, not an ending. The Paris Agreement remains in force. The commitments made at previous summits remain binding. The technical work — on just transition, on electrification, on the existing pledges to move away from fossil fuels — continues. And the science, as Guterres said, leaves no ambiguity about what is happening or what needs to be done.

What COP31 showed, above all, is that the gap between what the science demands and what politics can deliver has not closed — and in Antalya, it widened. The question for COP32 in Addis Ababa is whether that gap can be narrowed, or whether the world will watch another summit end the way this one did: with a gavel, a statement of regret and a crisis that does not wait for consensus.
