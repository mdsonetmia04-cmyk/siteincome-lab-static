TITLE: Tidal Is Deleting 29 Million Songs — Every Fully AI-Generated Track, Gone by January LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Tidal will delete 29 million fully AI-generated songs from December 9. 43% of recent uploads were AI-made — the first major streamer to ban them. ---BODY---

Tidal, the music streaming service owned by Jack Dorsey's Block, is about to delete 29 million songs from its catalogue — not because they are unpopular, or because they are old, or because the artists who made them asked for it, but because no human being made them at all. The company announced on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, that from December 9 it will begin removing every track on its platform that was generated entirely by artificial intelligence, and that the purge will be complete by the beginning of next year. The deletion, which will wipe out roughly 29 million recordings, is the most aggressive action any major streaming service has taken against AI-generated music, and it marks a decisive break with the approach taken by its rivals, who have so far settled for labelling AI music, or demonetising it, rather than removing it altogether. Tidal's message, delivered by its platform lead Robert Andersen in a post on X, was blunt: "On December 9, we'll begin removing all wholly AI-generated tracks from our catalog, and you'll see them completely gone by the beginning of the year. This will result in the deletion of 29 million songs, and the estimated rejection of 80-85,000 songs per day."

The scale of the problem Tidal is trying to solve is the story in itself. According to figures shared by Andersen, 43 per cent of the tracks submitted to Tidal over the past 90 days were identified as AI-generated — nearly half of everything uploaded to the platform, produced not by musicians but by machines. The company expects to reject between 80,000 and 85,000 such tracks every single day once the new policy takes effect, a rate that suggests the flood of synthetic music is not slowing down but accelerating. And the vast majority of the AI music already on the platform is, by Tidal's own account, music that nobody listens to: 96 per cent of the fully AI-generated tracks in its catalogue have never been streamed even once. The purge is, in that sense, a clean-up operation as much as a statement of principle — the removal of millions of recordings that exist only to game the system, to collect fractions of a cent in streaming royalties, and to crowd out the work of the human musicians whose livelihoods depend on being found. But it is also a statement of principle, and a gamble: Tidal is betting that listeners, and artists, would rather have a smaller catalogue made by people than a larger one filled with machines.

The flood: 43 per cent of everything uploaded is AI-made

The numbers Tidal released on Tuesday are the first time a major streaming service has put a precise figure on the scale of the AI music flood, and they are startling. In the last 90 days, 43 per cent of the tracks submitted to Tidal — nearly one in every two songs uploaded to the platform — were identified as having been generated entirely by artificial intelligence. That is not a niche, and it is not a curiosity. It is a structural transformation of what a music streaming service is, carried out in a matter of months, by tools that can generate a finished song — lyrics, melody, vocals, production — in seconds, at a cost close to zero, and at a scale that no human workforce could match. The tools have names that are now familiar to anyone who follows the music industry: Suno, Udio, and a growing number of competitors, each of them capable of producing music that is, to a casual listener, difficult to distinguish from the work of a human artist, and each of them used by a growing number of people to upload tracks by the thousands, in the hope that a fraction of them will be streamed, and that the streams will generate royalties.

The economics of the flood are simple, and they explain why it has grown so fast. Streaming services pay royalties per stream, and the payments, while tiny for any individual play, add up when a track is streamed thousands of times — or when thousands of tracks are each streamed a handful of times. A person with an AI music generator can produce hundreds of songs in a day, upload them all, and collect the royalties on whatever streams they attract, without ever picking up an instrument, writing a lyric, or hiring a singer. The practice has a name in the industry — "AI slop" — and it has become a business model, pursued not by musicians but by operators who treat music as a volume game, and who have no interest in whether anyone actually enjoys what they upload. The result is a catalogue that grows by millions of tracks a year, most of them never listened to, all of them competing for the same pool of royalty money that human artists depend on. Tidal's figures make the scale of that competition concrete: 43 per cent of uploads, 29 million tracks to be deleted, 80,000 to 85,000 rejections a day. The flood is not coming. It is already here.

What makes Tidal's numbers particularly striking is that they come from a platform that is, by the standards of the streaming industry, a small one. Tidal has far fewer subscribers than Spotify or Apple Music, and its catalogue is far smaller — and yet 43 per cent of its uploads are AI-generated. If the same proportion holds across the industry, and there is no reason to think Tidal is uniquely affected, then the number of AI-generated tracks being uploaded to the world's streaming services every day runs into the hundreds of thousands, and the total number already in circulation runs into the tens of millions. The industry has known, in general terms, that AI music was growing. What it did not know, until Tuesday, was how fast, and how completely it had come to dominate the flow of new music. Tidal's announcement has, in effect, published the industry's open secret, and the secret is worse than most people thought.

December 9: what gets deleted, and what survives

The policy Tidal announced is narrower, and more precise, than the headline suggests — and the precision matters, because it is the difference between a ban on AI music and a ban on a particular kind of it. From December 9, 2026, Tidal will no longer accept tracks that were generated entirely by artificial intelligence, and it will begin removing the wholly AI-generated tracks that are already in its catalogue, a process it expects to complete by the beginning of next year. The ban applies only to tracks that were generated wholly by AI tools. Songs that were produced with AI assistance — a human artist using AI to help with a beat, a vocal effect, a mix, or an arrangement — will still be accepted, and will still be available on the platform. The distinction is the one the music industry has been struggling to draw for the last two years, and it is a difficult one: at what point does a song stop being a human work that used a tool, and start being a machine's work that used a human? Tidal's answer is that the line is drawn at wholesale generation — if the AI made the whole thing, it goes; if a person made it with AI's help, it stays.

To enforce that line, Tidal will rely on detection technology provided by Ircam Amplify, a company affiliated with IRCAM, the French music research institute founded by the composer Pierre Boulez. Ircam Amplify's technology is designed to identify recordings that were generated by AI, by analysing the audio for the signatures that machine-generated music leaves behind — patterns in the sound that are difficult for a human listener to detect but that are, to a trained system, as distinctive as a fingerprint. The reliance on detection technology is itself a statement about the state of the industry: the same AI that generates the music is now needed to detect it, and the contest between generation and detection has become an arms race, with each side improving in response to the other. Tidal's bet is that the detectors are, for now, good enough — and that the tracks they flag are, overwhelmingly, the synthetic filler that no one listens to anyway. The 96 per cent figure — the proportion of fully AI-generated tracks that have never been streamed once — is the company's evidence for that bet, and it is a powerful one: if almost no one is listening to these tracks, then deleting them costs the platform almost nothing in listener satisfaction, and costs the artists who made them almost nothing in lost royalties.

There is a second reason the December 9 date matters, and it is the deadline it sets for the rest of the industry. Tidal is the first major streaming service to ban AI music outright, but it is unlikely to be the last to act, and the announcement has put pressure on its rivals to explain what they are doing about the same problem. Spotify, the largest streaming service in the world, has so far taken a different approach, labelling AI-generated music and, in some cases, removing tracks that impersonate real artists, but not banning synthetic music as a category. Apple Music has also stuck to labels, identifying AI-generated tracks rather than removing them. Both companies have argued that listeners should be able to choose what they listen to, and that the platform's role is to inform, not to censor. Tidal's answer is that the choice is illusory when nearly half of everything uploaded is synthetic, and that the platform's role is to protect the artists who make the music that listeners actually want to hear. The two positions are not easily reconciled, and the debate between them will define the streaming industry's response to AI for the next several years.

Why now: the artists' revolt and the royalty drain

Tidal's decision did not come out of nowhere. The company had already taken steps against AI music before Tuesday's announcement, labelling fully AI-generated tracks and stripping them of monetisation — removing the financial incentive to upload them — in an effort to protect real artists without banning the synthetic music outright. That approach, Andersen said, was not enough. The flood continued, the proportion of AI uploads kept rising, and the labelling and demonetisation did not stop the operators who were uploading synthetic tracks by the thousands. The decision to move from labelling to removal is, in that sense, an admission that the softer measures failed, and that something more drastic was needed. "Something more drastic needs to be done," was the message of the announcement, and the drastic thing is the deletion of 29 million songs — a figure that is, by itself, larger than the entire catalogue of some streaming services.

The pressure to act has come, above all, from artists. Musicians have been arguing for two years that AI-generated music is not a victimless phenomenon — that every stream of a synthetic track is a stream that could have gone to a human artist, that every dollar of royalty paid to an AI operator is a dollar that does not reach a songwriter, a performer, or a producer, and that the flood of synthetic music makes it harder for real artists to be discovered, because the platforms' recommendation systems are drowned in content that no one made and no one wants. The argument has been made most forcefully by independent artists, for whom streaming royalties, however small, are a meaningful part of their income, and for whom discovery — being found by a listener who has never heard of them — is the difference between a career and a hobby. Tidal, which has always positioned itself as the artist-friendly streaming service, with higher royalty rates and a focus on sound quality, has been more receptive to that argument than its rivals, and Tuesday's announcement is the most concrete expression of it yet: a streaming service that has decided its catalogue should be made by people, and that it is willing to make that catalogue smaller in order to keep it human.

The royalty argument is also, it should be said, an economic argument about the platform itself. Streaming royalties are paid from a fixed pool — a share of the platform's subscription and advertising revenue — and that pool is divided among the tracks that are streamed. When millions of synthetic tracks are uploaded and a fraction of them are streamed, even a handful of times each, they draw money from the pool that would otherwise go to human artists. The amounts involved for any individual synthetic track are tiny, but the aggregate is not: 29 million tracks, even at a fraction of a cent per stream, represent a meaningful diversion of money away from the people who make music for a living. Tidal's decision to delete those tracks is, among other things, a decision to redirect that money — to ensure that the royalties its subscribers generate go to the artists they actually listen to, rather than to the operators who have learned to game the system. It is a business decision as much as a moral one, and it is one that the company's artist-friendly branding makes easier to defend.

The human cost: who makes the music that gets deleted

There is a question that Tidal's announcement raises and does not fully answer, and it is the question of who, exactly, is making the 29 million tracks that are about to be deleted. The answer, for the most part, is not musicians. It is operators — individuals and, increasingly, organised groups — who use AI music generators to produce tracks at industrial scale, uploading them under invented artist names, with AI-generated cover art, and with titles designed to catch the algorithms that recommend music to listeners. Some of them are hobbyists, experimenting with a new technology. Some of them are entrepreneurs, who have worked out that the economics of streaming reward volume over quality, and who have built small businesses around the mass production of synthetic songs. And some of them are, by the industry's own account, bad actors — people who use AI to impersonate real artists, to create fake versions of popular songs, or to flood the platform with tracks designed to capture royalties that belong to someone else. The 29 million tracks are not, for the most part, the work of people who think of themselves as musicians. They are the output of a system that rewards the production of music as a commodity, and they are the reason the system is now being challenged.

That distinction matters, because it is the difference between a policy that harms artists and a policy that protects them. If the 29 million tracks were the work of human musicians who had used AI as a tool, then deleting them would be an act of censorship, and Tidal's announcement would be an attack on creativity. But Tidal's position is that they are not — that they are, overwhelmingly, the product of wholesale generation, with no human creative input beyond the prompt that started the process, and that their removal harms no artist, because no artist made them. The 96 per cent figure is the evidence for that claim: if 96 per cent of these tracks have never been streamed once, then they are not music that anyone is listening to, and their removal will not be noticed by any listener. The remaining 4 per cent — the synthetic tracks that have been streamed, sometimes many times — are the harder case, and they are the ones that will test the policy: tracks that listeners have found and enjoyed, even if no human made them, and whose removal will be felt, however slightly, by the people who liked them. Tidal's answer is that the enjoyment is beside the point — that a platform built for human music should not host music that no human made, whatever its listeners think of it.

There is also a question about the people who will be affected by the daily rejections — the 80,000 to 85,000 tracks a day that Tidal expects to turn away once the policy is in force. Some of those will be the work of operators, who will simply move to another platform, or find another way to upload. Some of them will be the work of genuine musicians who used AI in a way that the detection system flags as wholesale generation, and who will find their work rejected by a system that cannot tell the difference between a tool and a replacement. That is the risk of any detection-based policy, and it is a risk that Tidal has acknowledged implicitly by drawing the line at wholesale generation rather than at any use of AI. The company will have to manage the appeals, the false positives, and the complaints from artists who feel they have been wrongly caught — and it will have to do so while rejecting tens of thousands of tracks a day. The scale of the operation is, in itself, a measure of the problem: a streaming service that has to reject 80,000 songs a day is a streaming service that is being flooded, and the flood will not stop because one platform has decided to dam it.

Spotify and Apple: labels instead of bans

Tidal's announcement has thrown the different approaches of the streaming industry into sharp relief, and the contrast with its two largest rivals is the clearest illustration of the choice the industry faces. Spotify, the world's largest streaming service, has taken the position that AI-generated music should be labelled, not banned — that listeners should be told when a track was made by AI, and then left to decide for themselves whether to listen to it. The company has removed AI-generated tracks that impersonate real artists, and it has taken action against operators who game the royalty system, but it has not banned synthetic music as a category, and it has not proposed to. Apple Music has taken a similar approach, identifying AI-generated tracks and giving listeners the information to make their own choices. Both companies argue that the platform's role is to inform, not to decide, and that a ban on AI music would be a form of censorship — a judgement about what counts as music that a streaming service has no business making.

Tidal's answer to that argument is, in effect, that the choice Spotify and Apple offer is not a real choice. When 43 per cent of everything uploaded is AI-generated, the argument goes, the catalogue is no longer a neutral marketplace in which listeners can choose between human and synthetic music. It is a marketplace that has been flooded, in which human music is harder to find, harder to recommend, and harder to sustain, because the systems that surface music to listeners — the playlists, the recommendations, the search results — are drowned in synthetic content. A label does not solve that problem, because a label does not reduce the volume, and it does not redirect the royalties. Only removal does. That is the logic of Tidal's decision, and it is a logic that will be tested by what happens next: if listeners embrace the cleaner catalogue, and if artists flock to the platform that protects them, then the rivals' labelling approach will look timid, and the pressure on them to follow Tidal will grow. If listeners do not notice, or do not care, and if the operators simply move their synthetic tracks to Spotify and Apple Music, then Tidal's ban will look like a gesture — a principled one, but a gesture all the same.

The industry's response, so far, has been to watch and wait. Spotify and Apple have not commented on Tidal's announcement, and there is no sign that either is planning to follow it. That silence is itself informative: the two largest platforms have the most to lose from a ban, because they have the largest catalogues, the most to clean up, and the most to explain to the operators and the listeners who would be affected. They also have a different business model from Tidal's — one built on scale, on being the place where all music lives, rather than on being the place where human music is protected. Whether that model can survive the AI flood is the question Tidal has forced the industry to ask, and it is a question that will not be answered by one announcement, however bold. It will be answered by the numbers: by the proportion of uploads that are synthetic a year from now, by the royalties that flow to human artists, and by the listeners who decide, with their subscriptions, which kind of platform they want to pay for.

What it means for musicians — and for listeners

For musicians, Tidal's announcement is the most encouraging news they have had since the AI flood began. Independent artists, in particular, have been vocal about the damage synthetic music does to their livelihoods, and they have welcomed a platform that is willing to act rather than merely to label. The reaction on social media, where the announcement spread quickly, was overwhelmingly positive among musicians and music fans, with one commentator writing that the decision had convinced them to leave Spotify after eighteen years — a sentiment that, if it spreads, could be worth more to Tidal than any amount of advertising. The company is, after all, a small player in the streaming market, and it has always struggled to compete with Spotify and Apple on scale. Tuesday's announcement gives it something its rivals do not have: a clear, defensible position on the most contentious issue in the music industry, and a story to tell to the artists and listeners who care about it.

For listeners, the effect of the purge will be, in the short term, almost invisible. The 29 million tracks being deleted are, by Tidal's own account, tracks that almost no one listens to, and their removal will not change what any individual subscriber hears when they press play. The longer-term effect is harder to predict, and it depends on whether the ban works — whether it makes Tidal's catalogue cleaner, its recommendations better, and its artists better paid, or whether the synthetic music simply migrates to other platforms and the problem persists in a different form. There is also a question about what listeners actually want: whether they care, in practice, about whether the music they listen to was made by a human or by a machine, or whether they care only about whether it sounds good. Tidal is betting that they care, or that they will come to care, and that a platform that guarantees its music was made by people will be worth paying for. It is a bet on the idea that authenticity matters, and that the value of music is not only in the sound but in the person who made it. Whether that bet pays off will be the test of the next year.
