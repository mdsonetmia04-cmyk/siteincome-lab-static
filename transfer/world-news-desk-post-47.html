TITLE: Macy's 100th Thanksgiving Parade Brings Back a Balloon Lost Since 1931 — 100 Inflatables, 39 Balloons and 11,000 Volunteers Take Over New York LABELS: Entertainment SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The 100th Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade steps off Thursday with 100 inflatables, 39 balloons and a Blue Hippo lost since 1931 — now flying for Fiona. ---BODY---

The Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York. The 100th parade steps off on Thursday, November 26, 2026, with 100 inflatable elements, 39 balloons and more than 11,000 volunteers. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Look up on Thursday morning, and you will see a ghost. A gigantic blue hippopotamus with a goofy smile, 30 feet long, floating above the streets of Manhattan for the first time in 95 years. The Blue Hippo was one of the stars of the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade in 1931 — and then it vanished. In those days, the balloons were released into the sky after the parade, and the hippo was last seen drifting past the Empire State Building, never to be found. Now, for the parade's 100th anniversary, Macy's has lovingly reconstructed the hippo by hand from old photographs and video, age spots and all, and it will take its rightful place in the show on Thursday, November 26, 2026, leading a parade of 100 inflatable elements, 39 balloons, 28 floats, nine marching bands, 1,000 clowns and more than 11,000 volunteers down a 2.5-mile route from the Upper West Side to Herald Square. It is the biggest parade Macy's has ever staged — and, in the most touching detail of all, the Blue Hippo will fly in memory of another hippopotamus entirely.

The parade steps off at 8:30 a.m. Eastern Time on Thursday, November 26, and runs until around noon, broadcast live on NBC and streamed on Peacock. The route begins at 77th Street and Central Park West, winds down through Manhattan, and ends at the Macy's flagship store at Herald Square, at 34th Street and Seventh Avenue. It will go on rain or shine — though the giant balloons, as always, depend on the wind. If the gusts are too strong on Thursday morning, the balloons may have to be grounded, and the parade's organisers will be watching the forecast as closely as any of the 3.5 million people expected to line the route. But if the weather holds, the 100th parade will be a spectacle unlike any before it: a century of American popular culture, floating 30 feet above the pavement.

The numbers tell the story of the scale. More than 11,000 participants and volunteers. One hundred inflatable elements — the most in the parade's history. Thirty-nine balloons, including 10 all-new ones. Five balloonicles — inflatables attached to drivable vehicles. Twenty-eight floats, including eight brand-new ones. Nine specialty units, 31 clown groups, nine marching bands, and performance groups and musical acts spanning pop, country, rock, Latin and R&B. A 101-foot-long Doodlebug caterpillar snaking along the streets. And, at the end of it all, Santa Claus, as always, bringing up the rear and officially opening the Christmas season. It is a parade that took a year to build, a small army to stage, and a century to earn.

The hippo that came home — and the hippo it honours

The Blue Hippo is the star of this year's parade, and its story is the kind that could only happen at Macy's. The original balloon flew exactly once, in 1931. In the early years of the parade, the balloons were not deflated and stored after the show; they were released into the sky, and anyone who found one and returned it to Macy's received a reward. The Blue Hippo was set loose in 1931 and never came back. It was last seen drifting past the Empire State Building, and then it was gone — a mystery that lingered for 95 years. When the parade's organisers began planning the centennial, they decided to bring it home. The problem was that no pattern, no blueprint and no surviving piece of the original balloon existed. The team had to reconstruct it by hand from old newspaper photographs and video, studying the shape of the smile, the curve of the back, the proportions of the body, and building a new balloon that matched the old one as closely as humanly possible. They even added age spots to the 2026 version — a deliberate touch, as one of the builders put it: "It's had some time out there, and we're really excited to bring it back."

But the Blue Hippo's return carries a second, sadder story. In August, Macy's announced that the balloon would be back for the centennial, months before anyone knew what would happen next. On November 19, Fiona, the beloved hippopotamus at the Cincinnati Zoo, died at the age of nine, 11 days after swallowing a knit hat that had been thrown into her habitat. Fiona was a celebrity in her own right — a premature baby hippo who survived against the odds in 2017 and became one of the most famous animals in America, with fans around the world. Her death, a week before the parade, prompted an outpouring of grief — and a petition, signed by almost 500 people, asking the parade to dedicate the Blue Hippo balloon to her. Macy's agreed. The volunteers handling the Blue Hippo on Thursday will wear purple patches with Fiona's face inside a heart and the words "Celebrating Fiona", and Macy's has made a donation to the Cincinnati Zoo in her memory, with the zoo's blessing. The balloon that was lost for 95 years will fly, in the end, for a hippo that was loved by millions and lost far too soon.

The coincidence of the timing — Macy's unveiling the balloon and its hippo-themed Herald Square windows on the very day Fiona died — was not lost on the parade's fans, some of whom said the timing felt almost fated. It was not planned, and nobody at Macy's could have foreseen it. But it has given the centennial parade an emotional centre that no amount of planning could have manufactured: a giant blue balloon, rebuilt from photographs, flying over New York in memory of a real hippopotamus who never saw a parade. It is the kind of story that makes a parade more than a spectacle, and it is the reason the Blue Hippo, rather than any of the newer or flashier balloons, is the one everyone will be watching.

Ten new balloons, one per decade

The Blue Hippo is not the only new balloon in the sky. This year's parade features 39 balloons, including the debut of 10 all-new ones, and the parade's executive producer, Will Coss, the vice president of Macy's Studios, wanted each decade of the parade's history represented. Joining the Blue Hippo will be Bluey, the beloved Australian cartoon dog, making her parade debut wearing a Christmas cracker crown; Sorcerer's Apprentice Mickey, from Disney; Hello Kitty, holding an apple in honour of the Big Apple, her balloon standing 107 apples tall; Joe Cool — Snoopy's alter ego — with Woodstock, from Peanuts; Earl the Squirrel, the holiday mascot of Universal Orlando Resort; Buckaroo Bob the Minion, from Illumination's Despicable Me, replacing Kevin the Minion, who was retired after the 2025 parade; Billie Brightpaw, a cheerful and curious bear created by Macy's Studios; Macy's Blueprint Stars; and Underdog, from DreamWorks Animation. There will also be a new balloonicle, Fuzzyeti's Fur Wheel Drive, presented by Levi's — an inflatable attached to a drivable vehicle.

The returning favourites are just as beloved. Buzz Lightyear, Mario, Pac-Man, Pikachu, SpongeBob SquarePants and Gary, Spider-Man, Derpy Tiger from KPop Demon Hunters and the Pillsbury Doughboy will all be back in the sky. And in a detail that will delight parade historians, Felix the Cat — the first character balloon in parade history, introduced in 1927 — will make his return after nearly 100 years. The 100th parade will also surface balloons designed by artists Tim Burton, Keith Haring, Jeff Koons and Yayoi Kusama, from the Macy's Blue Sky Gallery, a reminder that the parade has always been, among other things, a floating art exhibition. The 101-foot-long Doodlebug caterpillar, at 31 metres, is the longest single element in the parade, and the 1,000 clowns — in 31 clown groups — are the human heartbeat of the show, the performers who keep the crowds laughing between the balloons.

The new balloons got their first time in the air on Saturday, November 7, at Balloonfest, the parade's annual test flight above the MetLife Stadium car parks in East Rutherford, New Jersey. Balloonfest is where the handlers learn to fly the new balloons, where the engineers check the rigging, and where the parade's organisers discover whether a balloon that looked perfect in the studio will behave in the wind. This year, the test flights went well — and the photographs from Balloonfest, of giant new characters rising over the stadium, were the first glimpse the public got of the centennial line-up. The balloons are built at the Macy's Parade Studio in Moonachie, New Jersey, where carpenters, sculptors, painters, electricians and balloon technicians build the parade year-round, and where the eight new floats were unveiled on November 17.

Eight new floats and a Broadway medley

The floats are the parade's other great tradition, and this year there are 28 of them, including eight new ones. The new floats were shown off on Tuesday, November 17, at the Macy's Parade Studio, and they include Tony Sarg's Amazing Marionettes, a tribute to the puppeteer who helped create the early parade — on the float, Sarg is searching for the lost Blue Hippopotamus from 1931, a nod to the balloon's story that ties the whole centennial together. There is Macy's Parade All-Stars, featuring past-parade characters including Popeye, Olive Oyl and Swee'Pea, Bullwinkle, Sonic the Hedgehog, Scooby-Doo, Curious George and the Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. There is Dashing All the Way, from DoorDash, with a cafe and a market on one side and a family gathering on the other. And there is Hogwarts Holiday, from HBO, inspired by the set of the upcoming Harry Potter series, along with the Mr Peanut Power Plant and four more new floats that Macy's has kept under wraps until parade day.

The musical line-up is as star-studded as the balloons. Megan Moroney, Teddy Swims, Queen Latifah, Keke Palmer, JoJo and Jimmy Fallon & The Roots will all perform, along with a tribute to Dolly Parton led by Megan Hilty, and special appearances by Melissa Joan Hart, Jack McBrayer, Janet McTeer and Holly Robinson Peete, all leading the way for Santa Claus. But the most ambitious musical moment of the parade is the one that replaces a long-standing tradition. Rather than the traditional individual performances from several productions currently on Broadway, this year's parade will feature a special one-time-only medley uniting the casts of nine musicals, in a performance honouring Broadway's past and present. It is a fitting choice for a centennial: nine shows, one stage, a hundred years of theatre in a single number. The medley will be performed in Herald Square, in front of the Macy's flagship store, where the television cameras are waiting and where the parade reaches its climax.

The parade's history is, in miniature, the history of American entertainment. It began in 1924, when Macy's employees — many of them immigrants — marched from Harlem to Herald Square dressed as clowns, cowboys and knights, with live animals borrowed from the Central Park Zoo. The balloons came in 1927, replacing the live animals, and Felix the Cat was the first character balloon. The parade was cancelled from 1942 to 1944, during the Second World War, when the rubber from the balloons was donated to the war effort — which is why this year, 102 years after the first parade, is the 100th parade. The balloons have been grounded by wind, the parade has been soaked by rain, and the crowds have grown from 10,000 in 1924 to more than 3.5 million today, with tens of millions more watching on television. Through it all, the parade has remained what it was in 1924: a store's gift to its city, and a city's gift to itself.

How to watch — and what to watch for

For the millions who will watch from home, the details are simple. The 100th Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade airs live on Thursday, November 26, from 8:30 a.m. to noon Eastern Time in all time zones, on NBC, and it will be available to stream on Peacock and Fubo. The broadcast runs for a full three and a half hours, and it will be hosted from Herald Square, where the performances happen and where Santa Claus makes his entrance. For those in New York, the best viewing is along Central Park West and Sixth Avenue, where the route is widest and the balloons are at their most spectacular — though the crowds arrive early, and the best spots are taken hours before the 8:30 start.

There are a few things to watch for, beyond the obvious. First, the Blue Hippo, and the purple Fiona patches on its handlers — a small, human detail that will be visible only to those watching closely. Second, the Tony Sarg float, and the puppet master's search for the lost hippopotamus — a story within a story, and a reminder that the parade's history is part of the show. Third, the Broadway medley, which is the most ambitious single performance the parade has ever staged, and which will be judged not only by the audience in Herald Square but by the theatre world watching at home. Fourth, Felix the Cat, back after nearly a century, a balloon that first flew when Calvin Coolidge was president and that will fly again on Thursday. And fifth, the weather: if the wind is strong, the balloons will fly low, or not at all, and the parade will be a different show than the one that was planned. The organisers will make that call on Thursday morning, and the whole country will know within minutes.

A century of firsts — and a few famous mishaps

A hundred parades produce a hundred stories, and some of the best are the ones that went wrong. In 1927, the first year of the balloons, Felix the Cat was filled with so much helium that he nearly floated away before the parade even began. In the early years, when the balloons were released after the show, they drifted for miles — one was found in a field in New Jersey, another tangled in a tree in Connecticut, and the Blue Hippo, of course, was never found at all. In 1997, high winds drove the Cat in the Hat balloon into a lamppost, injuring several spectators and changing the parade's safety rules for ever: since then, the balloons fly lower in strong wind, and the handlers — each balloon needs dozens of them, holding ropes in teams — train for months. In 2013, a gust pushed the Sonic the Hedgehog balloon into a tree, puncturing it, and the balloon had to be walked the rest of the route with a visible wound. The parade has been rained on, snowed on and wind-battered, and it has never once been cancelled for weather — only for war.

The parade has also been a mirror of its times. In the 1930s, the balloons were cartoon characters and nursery animals. In the 1950s, they were television stars. In the 1980s, they were video-game heroes. In the 2000s, they were movie franchises. This year's line-up — Bluey, a Minion, a KPop Demon Hunters character, a Harry Potter float — is a snapshot of what children (and their parents) are watching in 2026, just as the 1931 line-up was a snapshot of 1931. Will Coss, the parade's executive producer, put it well when he said that the parade every year is a snapshot, a time capsule of that specific year. The 100th parade is a time capsule with a hundred years inside it: a hippo from 1931, a cat from 1927, a mouse from the 1930s, a hedgehog from the 1990s, and a blue cartoon dog from the 2020s, all floating down the same street on the same morning.

The people who make the parade are part of the story too. The balloon handlers are volunteers, many of whom return year after year, and some of whom have walked the route for decades. The clown groups rehearse for months. The marching bands — nine of them this year, from high schools and universities across the country — are chosen by audition, and for many of the students, marching in the Macy's parade is the biggest performance of their lives. The 11,000 volunteers include the people who inflate the balloons the night before, in the streets around the Museum of Natural History, in a public event that has become a tradition of its own: families come to watch the balloons swell on the pavement, lying flat and enormous, like beached whales, before they rise the next morning. The parade is a television show, but it is also a neighbourhood event, built by hand, in a studio in New Jersey, by people who have spent their whole careers making giant balloons fly.

What the parade means, a century on

There is also the question of what the parade means, a century on. It began as a marketing event for a department store, and it remains, in part, exactly that. But it has become something larger: the unofficial start of the American Christmas season, the backdrop to millions of family Thanksgivings, the show that plays on the television while the turkey is in the oven and the relatives are arriving. It is watched by people who have never been to New York and never will be, and it is loved by people who have no interest in parades at all. Its endurance is a small mystery — why does a procession of balloons down a Manhattan avenue still matter, in an age of streaming and smartphones and a thousand other ways to spend a Thursday morning? The answer, perhaps, is that it is one of the few things in American life that has not changed very much in a hundred years, and that still belongs to everyone at once. A giant blue hippo, lost since 1931, flying over New York in memory of a hippopotamus from Cincinnati, is not the kind of thing that happens every day. On Thursday, for three and a half hours, it will happen — and for a little while, the whole country will be looking up.
