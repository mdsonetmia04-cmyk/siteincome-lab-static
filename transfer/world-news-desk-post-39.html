Nidal Hasan, the former Army psychiatrist who killed 13 people in the 2009 shooting at Fort Hood in Texas, has asked a federal judge to halt his execution by firing squad, which is scheduled for next week. Attorneys acting for Hasan filed a motion on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, in the United States District Court in Kansas, asking the court to pause the execution while he prepares a legal challenge to his conviction and death sentence. The execution, set for December 3 at Fort Hood, would be the first carried out by the United States military since 1961 — and the first by firing squad since the Second World War. In the filing, which Hasan signed himself, he says he intends to demonstrate that the military court's judgment and his resulting sentence of death "were obtained and are maintained in violation of his federal constitutional and statutory rights." A hearing on the request has been set for Wednesday, and the filing has the potential to add months or even years of delay to the Trump administration's plans while the legal battle plays out.

The case is one of the most consequential death penalty cases in modern American military history, and it has been divisive from the moment the Pentagon announced it. President Donald Trump ordered the execution on October 2, 2026, and the announcement that Hasan would be put to death by firing squad — a method the American military has not used in more than eighty years — drew immediate attention around the world. Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth then declared that the execution would be livestreamed, a proposal that provoked such an outcry that Trump himself later pulled the plug on the plan. Now, with eight days to go before the scheduled date, Hasan's lawyers have moved to stop it altogether, arguing that he has not yet had a proper opportunity to challenge a conviction that was handed down by a military court in 2013 and has been working its way through the military appeals system ever since. The question before the court in Kansas is a narrow one — whether the execution should be paused while Hasan prepares his challenge — but the answer will determine whether the United States military carries out its first execution in sixty-five years next week, or whether the case returns to the slow, grinding machinery of the courts, where it has already spent more than a decade.

What happened on Tuesday: the stay motion

The filing landed in the United States District Court in Kansas on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, and it was, by the standards of federal court filings, a short and pointed document. Hasan, who is imprisoned at the military prison at Fort Leavenworth in Kansas, asked the court to delay his execution by firing squad so that he can prepare a petition contesting his sentence. The filing was made in the civilian trial-level federal court, rather than in the military court system that convicted him, because Hasan is being held at Fort Leavenworth and because the challenge he intends to bring is a habeas corpus petition — the legal principle that allows a person who believes they are being unlawfully detained or imprisoned to petition a federal court for their release, or in a death penalty case, for the sentence to be set aside. Under American law, a condemned person can seek that kind of legal review once they have been told the date and method of their execution, and Hasan's lawyers moved within weeks of the October announcement that set the date.

The motion says that Hasan plans to raise at least two constitutional violations, according to the filing, and that he reserves the right to identify, prepare, and present additional issues once a stay of execution is granted and standby counsel has sufficient opportunity to investigate and research potential claims in order to assist him. That language is important, because it signals that the filing is not the full challenge but the opening move: the request for time to build one. His attorneys wrote that Hasan "intends to demonstrate that the military court's judgment and his resulting sentence of death were obtained and are maintained in violation of his federal constitutional and statutory rights" — a broad claim that, if it succeeds, would unwind a conviction that has already survived years of military appeals. The filing also says that Hasan intends to keep representing himself in the habeas proceedings, as he did at his court-martial, although he is being assisted by lawyers from the American Civil Liberties Union Capital Punishment Project, who filed the motion on his behalf.

The case has been assigned to Judge Tony Mattivi in the United States District Court in Kansas. Mattivi was nominated by Trump to serve as a federal judge and was confirmed by the Senate on June 9, 2026 — a detail that has not gone unnoticed, given that the execution order he is being asked to halt was issued by the same president who put him on the bench. A hearing on the stay request has been set for Wednesday, November 25. The speed of the timetable reflects the urgency on both sides: the execution is scheduled for December 3, eight days after the hearing, and any decision the judge makes will have to survive the appeals that are certain to follow, whichever way he rules. If he grants the stay, the government will appeal. If he denies it, Hasan's lawyers will appeal. Either way, the case is likely to move quickly to the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals, and possibly to the Supreme Court, in the days that remain before December 3.

One detail in the filing points to the argument Hasan most wants to make. The motion says he wants to present an argument about his motive for the attack that he was barred from making at his first trial — an argument that the military judge ruled out before the court-martial began. At his trial in 2013, Hasan wanted to argue that he had acted to protect fellow Muslims from the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, a defence the judge rejected, ruling that his motive was irrelevant to whether he had committed murder. Hasan represented himself at that trial, and he put on almost no defence at all, calling no witnesses and making only a brief opening statement. The question of whether he should have been allowed to present his motive has followed the case ever since, and it is one of the issues his lawyers are expected to develop in the habeas petition — if the court gives them the time to do it.

December 3: the date, the method, and the history

The execution that Hasan is asking the court to stop is scheduled for December 3, 2026, at Fort Hood in Texas — the same base where he carried out the attack seventeen years ago. The choice of location is deliberate, and it is part of what makes the case so charged: the military intends to put Hasan to death at the place where he killed thirteen of his fellow soldiers. The method is a firing squad, a choice that has not been used by the American military since the Second World War. The last person the United States military executed by firing squad was Private Eddie Slovik, a soldier shot for desertion on January 31, 1945, in France. The last military execution of any kind was in 1961, when the Army hanged Private John A. Bennett at Fort Leavenworth for rape and attempted murder. Hasan would be the first person executed by the United States military in sixty-five years, and the first by firing squad in eighty-one.

The order for the execution came from the top. President Donald Trump ordered it on October 2, 2026, and the announcement was made by Army and Pentagon officials that same month. The decision was divisive from the start, not only because of the method and the long gap since the last military execution, but because of the politics surrounding it. The Fort Hood attack was one of the worst mass shootings on an American military base in history, and the demand for Hasan's execution has been a fixture of American political debate for more than a decade. Trump, who has repeatedly called for the death penalty to be used more often and more swiftly, made the order a public statement of intent — and then, in the weeks that followed, the administration's handling of the details turned the case into a running controversy. Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth declared that the execution would be livestreamed, a proposal that drew condemnation from legal experts, veterans' groups, and members of Congress, who called it a spectacle unworthy of the military and a violation of the dignity the armed forces are supposed to uphold. Trump later called off the livestream plan, but the damage to the administration's presentation of the case was done, and the episode left the impression of an execution being managed as a public event rather than as a solemn act of military justice.

The military has said little about the logistics of the execution itself. An Army official, speaking on condition of anonymity to discuss sensitive plans, said that the military is not planning to release any details about the firing squad or its protocols, including the number of service members involved. That secrecy is standard for executions, but it has fed the uncertainty around the case, and it is part of the reason the court filing has drawn so much attention: for a case that has been conducted largely in the open — a public trial, a public sentence, a public order from the president — the final act is being prepared behind closed doors. What is known is that the execution would take place at Fort Hood, that it would be carried out by firing squad, and that it would be the first time the American military has put a service member to death since 1961. What is not known, and what the court in Kansas may now decide, is whether it will happen on December 3 at all.

November 5, 2009: the attack that killed 13

To understand why this execution matters so much, it is necessary to go back to November 5, 2009, to the Soldier Readiness Processing Center at Fort Hood, a sprawling Army base in central Texas that is one of the largest military installations in the world. Hasan, then a 39-year-old Army major and psychiatrist, walked into the medical facility where soldiers were being processed for deployment and opened fire with a laser-sighted handgun. He killed thirteen people — twelve soldiers and one civilian — and wounded more than thirty others, in an attack that lasted only minutes but left the base, and the country, in shock. Hasan was shot by responding civilian police officers during the attack and was left paralysed from the chest down. He survived, was taken into military custody, and has been in confinement ever since.

The thirteen people Hasan killed were soldiers preparing to deploy to Iraq and Afghanistan, and a civilian employee of the base. Their names were read aloud in court, and their families have waited seventeen years for the sentence handed down in 2013 to be carried out. The attack was, at the time, the worst mass shooting ever carried out on an American military base, and it prompted a national debate about the warning signs that had been missed. Hasan had been the subject of concern among his colleagues and superiors for years: his presentations at Walter Reed Army Medical Center had alarmed fellow officers, his communications with the radical cleric Anwar al-Awlaki had drawn the attention of the FBI, and his growing opposition to the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan was well known to those around him. The failure to act on those warnings became the subject of official reviews, congressional hearings, and a lasting argument about whether the military's reluctance to confront a Muslim officer's extremism had allowed a tragedy to happen. That argument has never been resolved, and it is part of the reason the case still resonates: the attack was not only a crime but a failure of the institution that Hasan served.

The scale of the attack is worth stating plainly, because it is the scale that has driven the demand for the death penalty for seventeen years. Thirteen people were killed. More than thirty were wounded by gunfire, many of them seriously, and some of them permanently disabled. The shooting took place in a building full of unarmed soldiers, many of them sitting in chairs waiting for medical checks, with no reason to expect that the psychiatrist who walked in among them was about to open fire. Hasan fired more than two hundred rounds, according to the evidence presented at his trial, reloading his weapon as soldiers fled and fell around him. The attack ended only when civilian police officers arrived and shot him, and the fact that he survived — paralysed, but alive — has meant that the families of his victims have had to watch him live through years of appeals while the sentence of death handed down in 2013 went uncarried. For those families, the December 3 date was, until Tuesday's filing, the end of a wait that has lasted longer than the lives of some of the soldiers Hasan killed had lasted when they died.

The trial and the death sentence

Hasan was tried by a military court-martial in 2013, four years after the attack, in a proceeding that was as unusual as the crime. He chose to represent himself, against the advice of his military lawyers, and he conducted a defence that was, by any measure, minimal. He called no witnesses. He presented almost no evidence. He made only a brief opening statement, in which he acknowledged that the evidence would show he was the shooter, and he did not contest the facts of the attack. The jury — a panel of thirteen senior officers — convicted him of thirteen counts of premeditated murder and thirty-two counts of attempted premeditated murder, and, after a short sentencing phase, sentenced him to death. The sentence was automatic in one sense and extraordinary in another: under military law, a death sentence requires the unanimous agreement of the jury, and the panel gave it. But the sentence has never been carried out, because the American military justice system builds in years of mandatory review, and because the case has been caught in the long, slow process of appeals that follows any death sentence in the United States.

The trial itself raised questions that have never gone away. Hasan wanted to argue that he had acted to protect Muslims from American wars, and the military judge, Colonel Tara Osborn, ruled that his motive was not a defence to murder and barred him from presenting it. That ruling meant the jury heard almost nothing about why Hasan said he had done what he had done, and it meant that the trial was, in effect, a trial about the facts of the shooting rather than about the reasons behind it. Hasan's supporters — and some legal scholars — have argued ever since that the ruling denied him a fair trial, because it prevented him from presenting the only defence he wanted to make. His critics reply that motive is not a defence to murder under any system of law, military or civilian, and that allowing him to argue it would have turned the trial into a platform for his views. The argument is one that the habeas petition is expected to revive, and it is the reason the filing in Kansas matters beyond the immediate question of whether the execution goes ahead on December 3: it reopens a debate about the fairness of a trial that was conducted thirteen years ago, under rules that Hasan has always said were stacked against him.

The years since the trial have been spent in the military appeals system, which is slower than its civilian counterpart and, for death penalty cases, deliberately so. A death sentence handed down by a court-martial must be reviewed by the Army Court of Criminal Appeals, then by the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces, and then by the president, who must personally approve the execution. That process took more than a decade in Hasan's case, and it was only in 2026, with Trump's order of October 2, that the final step was taken. The length of the delay is itself a feature of the system: the military has not executed anyone since 1961, and the apparatus for doing so — the regulations, the protocols, the personnel — has had to be rebuilt from scratch. That is part of the reason the announcement of the December 3 date was so striking: it was not only the end of a seventeen-year wait for the families, but the revival of a practice the military had abandoned for two generations.

The livestream plan — and why Trump stopped it

Of all the details surrounding the execution, the one that drew the most attention was also the one that was abandoned the fastest. After the October announcement, Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth declared that Hasan's execution by firing squad would be livestreamed — a proposal that had no precedent in American military history and that provoked an immediate and furious response. Legal scholars called it a violation of the dignity of the proceedings. Veterans' organisations said it would turn a solemn act of military justice into a spectacle. Members of Congress from both parties condemned it, and the families of some of the victims said they did not want the killing of the man who murdered their loved ones to be broadcast as entertainment. The proposal also raised practical questions: who would watch, who would control the feed, and what would happen if something went wrong in front of a live audience. Within days, Trump himself pulled the plug on the plan, and the administration moved on without explaining why the idea had been floated in the first place.

The livestream episode matters, because it shaped the way the public understands the execution, and because it is part of the argument Hasan's lawyers are making — that the process around his death sentence has been conducted as a political act rather than a judicial one. The order for the execution came from the president, the announcement of the method came from the Pentagon, and the proposal to livestream it came from the defence secretary, all within days of each other, in a sequence that looked less like the careful, deliberate process that the military justice system is supposed to follow and more like a series of announcements designed for maximum public impact. None of that, on its own, makes the sentence unlawful. But it has given Hasan's lawyers material to work with, and it has given the case a political charge that it did not have when the sentence was handed down in 2013, in a courtroom, by a jury of officers, under rules that had nothing to do with the politics of the moment. The question the court in Kansas must now answer is whether any of that matters to the legality of the execution — and whether the time Hasan needs to make that argument is a reason to stop the firing squad from being assembled on December 3.

There is a second reason the livestream episode matters, and it is simpler: it showed that the administration's handling of the execution was not as careful as the gravity of the act required. An execution is the most serious thing a government can do to a person, and the American military has not done it for sixty-five years. The decision to announce it, to choose a method unused since the Second World War, and then to propose broadcasting it live, all in the space of a few weeks, suggested a process moving faster than the institution's own capacity to manage it. The military has said it will not release details of the firing squad's protocols, and it has said nothing about who will be present, what the procedure will be, or how the aftermath will be handled. That silence is understandable — executions are not conducted in public — but it sits uneasily alongside the very public way the execution was announced, and it leaves the impression of an institution that is confident about the decision and uncertain about the details. Tuesday's filing exploits that gap, arguing, in effect, that a process moving this fast, after waiting seventeen years, is a process that has not given the condemned man the time the law requires to challenge it.

The three survivors who cannot watch

The court action on Tuesday came during uncertainty over some of the logistics of the execution, and one of the most painful details emerged in the same hours as the filing. Military officials said on Tuesday that three survivors of the shooting at Fort Hood will not be allowed to witness the execution. On November 10, the Army said that the three survivors could not attend because none of them was the direct victim of the charges on which Hasan was convicted, citing regulations governing military executions. The decision, which was communicated to the survivors only weeks before the scheduled date, has added a bitter note to a case that was already fraught: the people who were shot by Hasan, who lived through the attack and have carried its consequences for seventeen years, have been told that they do not qualify as victims for the purpose of watching the man who shot them be put to death.

The regulations are, on their face, a matter of definition. Military execution regulations limit attendance to a defined list of people, and the Army's position is that the three survivors do not fall within it because the specific charges of which Hasan was convicted did not name them as the direct victims. But the distinction has struck many as legalistic to the point of cruelty. The three survivors were in the building, they were shot at, and they were wounded — and the argument that they are not victims because of the way the charges were drafted has been difficult for them, and for the public, to accept. The families of those who were killed will be permitted to attend, and the execution will be witnessed by military officials, but the survivors' exclusion has become a symbol of a process that, for all its talk of justice for the victims, has been conducted according to rules that the victims themselves did not write and do not understand. It is one more reason the case has become a lightning rod: every detail, from the livestream to the witness list, has been read as a statement about what the execution is for, and who it is for.

What a habeas petition can and cannot do

The legal vehicle Hasan is using — a habeas corpus petition in federal court — is one of the oldest and most powerful tools in American law, and it is also one of the most limited. A habeas petition allows a prisoner to ask a federal court to review whether their detention, or their sentence, violates the Constitution or federal law. It is not a retrial. It is not an appeal. It is a collateral challenge, brought after the direct appeals have been exhausted, and it faces formidable barriers: the prisoner must show that the original proceedings were constitutionally defective, not merely that the outcome was harsh or that the evidence was disputed. For a death penalty case, the stakes of a habeas petition are absolute — if it succeeds, the sentence is set aside; if it fails, the execution proceeds — and the courts treat it with a seriousness that matches those stakes. That is why the request for a stay matters so much: without a stay, the execution could take place before the court has had time to decide whether the petition has merit.

Hasan's lawyers face a steep climb. The military courts that reviewed his conviction spent years on it, and they upheld the sentence. The argument that he was denied a fair trial because he could not present his motive has been made before, and it has been rejected before. The argument that the sentence was obtained in violation of his constitutional rights will have to be more specific than the broad language of Tuesday's filing, and it will have to survive the government's response, which is certain to argue that Hasan has already had his day in court — several days, in fact, spread over thirteen years of military appeals — and that the time for new challenges has passed. But the filing also has something in its favour, and it is the thing that has made death penalty habeas cases so difficult for courts to resolve quickly: the fact that the execution, once carried out, cannot be undone. Courts are reluctant to allow an execution to proceed while a serious legal challenge is pending, because the cost of being wrong is irreversible, and because the law has always treated the taking of a life by the state as an act that requires the highest degree of certainty. That reluctance is the reason stays of execution are granted as often as they are, and it is the reason the hearing on Wednesday will be watched so closely.

There is also a practical question that the court must weigh, and it is one that cuts in Hasan's favour: the time he has had to prepare. The execution date was set on October 2, and the filing came on November 24 — seven weeks later, and nine days before the scheduled date. His lawyers argue that seven weeks is not enough to prepare a habeas petition in a case with a thirteen-year history, a trial record running to thousands of pages, and constitutional issues that have never been fully litigated in a civilian court. The government will argue that Hasan has known since 2013 that he was under a sentence of death, and that he has had thirteen years to prepare the challenge he is only now bringing. Both arguments have force. But the court's decision will turn, in the end, on a simpler question: whether the risk of executing a man whose legal challenge has not been heard is greater than the cost of delaying an execution that has already been delayed for seventeen years. In death penalty cases, American courts have usually answered that question in favour of delay.

The judge: Tony Mattivi, confirmed five months ago

The judge who will decide the question is, by the standards of the federal bench, a newcomer. Judge Tony Mattivi was nominated by President Trump to serve on the United States District Court in Kansas and was confirmed by the Senate on June 9, 2026 — five months before the case landed on his docket. His confirmation was, like most judicial confirmations in the current era, a partisan affair, and his appointment was part of the administration's broader effort to reshape the federal judiciary. That he is now being asked to decide whether to halt an execution ordered by the president who appointed him is a coincidence of timing that would be remarkable in any case, and it is doubly so in this one, because the execution is one of the administration's most high-profile acts and the judge is one of its most recent appointees.

There is no suggestion that Judge Mattivi will decide the case on the basis of who appointed him, and federal judges routinely rule against the administrations that put them on the bench. But the optics of the assignment have not gone unnoticed, and they are part of the reason the hearing on Wednesday will be watched with unusual care. If Mattivi grants the stay, he will be the Trump-appointed judge who stopped the Trump-ordered execution of the Fort Hood shooter, and the political reaction will be immediate. If he denies it, he will be the Trump-appointed judge who allowed the execution to go ahead, and the reaction will be equally immediate, from the other side. Neither outcome will be read, in public, as a purely legal decision, however carefully the judge writes his opinion. That is the burden of deciding a case like this one, and it is a burden that falls on a judge who has been on the bench for less than six months. The hearing on Wednesday morning — or Wednesday afternoon, as one report put it — will be his first encounter with a case that has already consumed seventeen years, thirteen lives, and the attention of two presidents.

The military death penalty: sixty-five years without an execution

To understand how extraordinary the December 3 date is, it helps to understand how rarely the American military uses the death penalty at all. The military has not executed anyone since 1961, when Private John A. Bennett was hanged at Fort Leavenworth. In the sixty-five years since, military juries have handed down death sentences — Hasan's was one of a small number — but none has been carried out. Presidents have declined to approve them, appeals have dragged on for decades, and the military itself has, in practice, treated the death penalty as a sentence that is imposed but not executed. The result is that there are men on military death row at Fort Leavenworth who have been there for decades, their sentences uncarried, their cases unresolved, in a kind of legal limbo that reflects the military's ambivalence about the ultimate punishment. Hasan's case was supposed to break that pattern. The October order was presented as a decision to end the limbo, to carry out a sentence that had been waiting for thirteen years, and to demonstrate that the military justice system still means what it says.

That ambition is part of what makes the case so significant, and it is also part of what makes it so risky. If the execution goes ahead on December 3, it will be the first in sixty-five years, and it will set a precedent for the other men on military death row, whose cases will suddenly move from the realm of the theoretical to the realm of the imminent. If it is delayed, and then delayed again, the pattern of the last sixty-five years will reassert itself, and the question of whether the military can ever carry out a death sentence will remain unanswered. The administration's supporters argue that the delay is itself an injustice — that a sentence handed down by a jury of officers in 2013 should not still be uncarried in 2026, and that the families of the thirteen people Hasan killed should not have to wait any longer. The administration's critics argue that the haste is the problem — that an execution after sixty-five years, by a method unused for eighty-one, announced in October and scheduled for December, is being rushed for political reasons, and that the courts should slow it down. Both arguments will be in the room on Wednesday, and both will be heard by a judge who has been on the bench for five months.

The history of the firing squad is worth pausing on, because it is the detail that has captured the public imagination. The last American soldier executed by firing squad was Private Eddie Slovik, shot on January 31, 1945, in France, for desertion — the only American soldier executed for desertion since the Civil War. Slovik's execution was itself controversial, and it has been the subject of books, films, and decades of argument about whether the military's use of the firing squad was a deterrent or a disgrace. That Hasan would be executed by the same method, eighty-one years later, is a choice that the administration has not fully explained. The military's regulations allow for execution by lethal injection, which is the method used by the federal government and by most states. The decision to use a firing squad instead — a method associated, in the public mind, with wartime discipline and with the harshest traditions of military justice — was presented as a return to military tradition, but it has been read by many as a statement in itself: a deliberate choice of the most severe, most visible, and most archaic method available. Whether that choice survives the legal challenge is one of the questions the court in Kansas may have to answer.

Why this case divides even death-penalty supporters

The Hasan case is unusual in American death penalty politics, because it divides people who would normally agree with each other. Among supporters of the death penalty, there are those who believe the execution should have happened years ago — that thirteen murders, proven beyond doubt, with a unanimous jury verdict, is the clearest possible case for the ultimate punishment, and that the seventeen-year delay is a failure of the system. And there are those who support the death penalty in principle but believe the Hasan case is the wrong one to revive it with — because the trial was flawed, because the motive argument was never heard, because the process has been politicised, and because the military's first execution in sixty-five years should not be a case with this many unresolved questions. Among opponents of the death penalty, there are those who oppose the execution on principle, and those who oppose it on the specifics — the haste, the method, the livestream proposal, the exclusion of the survivors — even while conceding that the crime itself was among the worst in American military history.

That division is reflected in the legal community, where the case has prompted an unusually frank debate about the state of military justice. Some military lawyers argue that the court-martial system worked as it was supposed to: Hasan was tried, convicted by a unanimous jury, sentenced to death, and his case was reviewed for thirteen years before the president approved the execution. Others argue that the system failed at the first step, when the judge barred Hasan from presenting his motive, and that everything that followed — the appeals, the delay, the political order — has been an attempt to paper over a trial that was not fair. The argument is not about whether Hasan committed the murders. No one seriously disputes that he did. It is about whether the process that condemned him was conducted according to the standards the military sets for itself, and whether a death sentence handed down under those circumstances should be carried out by an institution that has not executed anyone in sixty-five years. The court in Kansas will not decide that question on Wednesday. But the stay motion is the vehicle through which it will eventually be decided, and the hearing is the first step.

There is also a question that is rarely asked in public but that shapes the debate in private: what the execution is for. The families of the victims want it because they want justice for the people they lost, and because they have waited seventeen years for a sentence to be carried out. The administration wants it because it wants to demonstrate that the military justice system works, and that the death penalty, long dormant in the armed forces, is a live instrument again. The military wants it, or says it wants it, because the sentence was handed down by a court-martial and the president has ordered it to be carried out. But there are those who ask what the execution will actually achieve — whether it will deter anyone, whether it will bring closure to the families, whether it will change anything about the military's ability to prevent the next attack, or whether it is, in the end, a symbolic act, carried out for reasons that have more to do with politics than with justice. That question has no legal answer, and it will not be answered in court. But it is the question that will be asked, again and again, in the days between now and December 3, and it is the question that will still be asked after the firing squad has done its work — or after the court has stopped it.

What happens next: Wednesday's hearing

The immediate future of the case will be decided on Wednesday, November 25, when Judge Mattivi hears Hasan's request for a stay of execution. The hearing is expected to be brief, and the arguments are expected to be familiar: Hasan's lawyers will argue that he needs time to prepare a habeas petition, that the petition raises serious constitutional questions, and that the execution should not go ahead while those questions are unanswered. The government's lawyers will argue that Hasan has had thirteen years to prepare his challenge, that the military courts have already reviewed his conviction, and that the stay would be a delay for its own sake, prolonging a case that has already taken seventeen years. The judge will have to decide quickly, because the execution is eight days away, and because either decision will be appealed immediately. If he grants the stay, the government will ask the Tenth Circuit to lift it. If he denies it, Hasan's lawyers will ask the Tenth Circuit to impose one. The case could reach the Supreme Court before December 3, and the justices could be asked to decide, on an emergency basis, whether the first military execution in sixty-five years goes ahead on schedule.

Beyond the hearing, the calendar is unforgiving. The execution is scheduled for December 3, and the days between now and then will be filled with filings, hearings, and emergency appeals, in a compressed timetable that is itself a measure of how unusual the case has become. In most death penalty cases, the final weeks before an execution are a blur of last-minute motions and late-night court orders, and Hasan's case is likely to follow that pattern. The difference is that this case has been waiting for seventeen years, and the final week will be the first time the courts have had to decide, under real time pressure, whether the sentence should be carried out. The families of the victims will be watching, and so will the survivors, and so will the military, and so will a public that has followed the case since the morning of November 5, 2009. What happens on Wednesday will not end the case. But it will determine whether the next eight days are a countdown to an execution or the beginning of another long delay — and whether the United States military carries out its first execution since 1961 next week, or whether the wait goes on.

Why this story matters beyond Fort Hood

It is easy, reading about a stay motion in a Kansas courtroom, to file this story under "legal procedure" and move on. The execution is scheduled, the lawyers have filed, the judge will decide, and the machinery of the courts will do what it does. But that would be a mistake, and the reasons are worth spelling out. First, the case is about the death penalty itself — about whether the United States, after sixty-five years without a military execution, is going to start carrying them out again, and about what that means for the men still on military death row. Second, it is about the fairness of a trial that was conducted thirteen years ago, under rules that prevented the defendant from presenting the argument he most wanted to make, and about whether a death sentence handed down in those circumstances should be carried out. Third, it is about the politics of the death penalty — about an execution ordered by a president, announced by the Pentagon, and proposed for livestream by the defence secretary, in a sequence that has made the case a test of whether the military justice system can still function as a judicial process rather than a political one.

And there is a fourth reason, simpler than the others. The thirteen people Hasan killed were soldiers, and the thirty-odd people he wounded were soldiers, and the attack took place on a military base, in a building where soldiers were preparing to deploy to war. The military is an institution that asks its members to accept the risk of death in service, and it promises them, in return, that the institution will protect them, and that if someone harms them, justice will be done. The Hasan case is a test of that promise. For seventeen years, the families of the victims have waited for the sentence to be carried out, and for seventeen years, the system has told them to wait a little longer. The December 3 date was supposed to be the end of that wait. Tuesday's filing may extend it, perhaps by months, perhaps by years. Whether that is justice delayed or justice denied depends on where you stand. But it is the question the case has always asked, and it is the question the court in Kansas will begin to answer on Wednesday.

The bottom line

Nidal Hasan, the former Army psychiatrist who killed 13 people and wounded more than 30 in the November 5, 2009, attack at Fort Hood in Texas, has asked a federal judge in Kansas to halt his execution by firing squad, which is scheduled for December 3, 2026, at Fort Hood. Attorneys filed the motion on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, in the United States District Court in Kansas, asking the court to pause the execution while Hasan prepares a habeas corpus petition challenging his conviction and death sentence, which was handed down by a military court-martial in 2013. In the filing, which Hasan signed, he says he intends to demonstrate that the military court's judgment and his resulting sentence of death were obtained and are maintained in violation of his federal constitutional and statutory rights, and he plans to raise at least two constitutional violations, including an argument about his motive that he was barred from making at his first trial. A hearing on the stay request has been set for Wednesday, November 25, before Judge Tony Mattivi, who was nominated by President Trump and confirmed by the Senate on June 9, 2026. President Trump ordered the execution on October 2, 2026. It would be the first execution carried out by the United States military since 1961 and the first by firing squad since 1945. Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth's proposal to livestream the execution was later called off by Trump. Military officials said on Tuesday that three survivors of the shooting will not be allowed to witness the execution, because none of them was the direct victim of the charges on which Hasan was convicted. The filing has the potential to add major delays to the administration's plans while the legal battle plays out — and the decision the court makes on Wednesday will determine whether the firing squad is assembled at Fort Hood on December 3, or whether the wait, now seventeen years long, goes on.
