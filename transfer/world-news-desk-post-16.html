TITLE: SpaceX and Tesla Are Talking Merger — Five Directors Appointed to Oversee the Deal of the Decade LABELS: Technology, Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: SpaceX and Tesla have appointed five directors to oversee merger talks, reports say — a deal that would unite Musk's rockets, cars and AI empire. ---BODY---

Elon Musk at a conference in Big Sky, Montana, March 28, 2024. Photo: Wcamp9 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0).

SpaceX and Tesla — the two companies that have defined Elon Musk's career, and that have reshaped the rocket industry and the automobile industry in turn — are in talks about a merger. According to reports published on Monday, the two companies have assigned five directors to oversee merger discussions, a formal step that suggests the talks are serious, structured, and moving beyond the realm of speculation. If the deal happens, it would be one of the largest and most consequential corporate mergers in history: a combination of the world's most valuable private space company and the world's most valuable car company, under a single corporate roof, led by the same man.

The reports are, for now, just that — reports. Neither company has confirmed the talks publicly, and the details of any deal — its structure, its valuation, its timeline — have not been disclosed. But the appointment of directors to oversee the discussions is a concrete step, and it is the kind of step that companies take when they are serious about a transaction. Here is what we know, what a merger would mean, and why the two companies — and the man who runs them both — might want it to happen.

The report: five directors, one conversation

According to the reports, which surfaced on Monday, SpaceX and Tesla have each appointed directors to a group that will oversee merger discussions — five directors in total, tasked with examining the possibility of combining the two companies. The appointment of a dedicated group, with directors from both sides, is the kind of formal step that precedes serious negotiations. It is not a casual conversation. It is a structured process, with named people, a defined mandate, and the authority to explore a deal.

What the directors will examine, in the first instance, is whether a merger makes sense — and if so, on what terms. That examination will cover the valuation of both companies, the structure of a combined entity, the regulatory issues that a merger would raise, and the governance of a company that would be, by any measure, one of the largest and most complex in the world. It will also cover the question that matters most to the markets: what the combined company would be worth, and whether the whole would be worth more than the sum of its parts.

The reports do not name the directors, and they do not disclose the timeline. They do not say whether the talks are in an early stage or an advanced one, or whether a deal is likely or merely possible. What they do say is that the conversation is happening, that it is formal, and that it involves the most senior people at both companies. In the world of corporate mergers, that is a significant fact in itself. Companies do not appoint directors to oversee merger discussions unless they are seriously considering a merger.

It is worth noting what the reports do not claim. They do not claim that a deal has been agreed, or that one is imminent. They do not claim that the boards of either company have approved a merger, or that the shareholders have been consulted. A merger of this scale would require the approval of both boards, the approval of shareholders, and the clearance of regulators in the United States and around the world. The appointment of five directors is the beginning of a process, not its end — and the process, if it continues, will take months, and possibly longer.

Two companies, one man

To understand why a merger is being discussed, it helps to understand what the two companies are — and how deeply they are already connected. SpaceX, founded by Musk in 2002, is the world's most successful private space company. It builds the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets, which have made reusable spaceflight routine. It operates Starlink, the satellite internet constellation that now serves millions of customers around the world. It is developing Starship, the largest and most powerful rocket ever built, which is designed to carry humans to the Moon and to Mars. And it is, by most estimates, the most valuable private company in the world — a company whose valuation has been reported in the hundreds of billions of dollars.

Tesla, which Musk joined in 2004 and has led as chief executive since 2008, is the world's most valuable automobile company. It builds electric vehicles — the Model 3, the Model Y, the Model S, the Model X, and the Cybertruck — and it has forced the entire global auto industry to electrify. It builds batteries, solar panels, and energy storage systems. It is developing Optimus, a humanoid robot, and it is pursuing full self-driving technology that, if it works, would transform transportation. And it is a public company, listed on the Nasdaq, with a market capitalization that has, at times, exceeded a trillion dollars.

The two companies are separate. They have separate boards, separate shareholders, separate employees, and separate finances. But they share a chief executive, and they share a vision — Musk's vision of a future in which humanity is multiplanetary, in which energy is sustainable, and in which artificial intelligence transforms every industry. They also share technology, talent, and ambition, in ways that are not always visible from the outside. Engineers move between the two companies. Suppliers serve both. And the strategic thinking that guides one is, inevitably, the strategic thinking that guides the other, because the same man is doing the thinking.

A merger would formalize what is already, in practice, a close relationship. It would create a single company that spans space, energy, transportation, robotics, and artificial intelligence — a company that would be, in Musk's own framing, a bet on the future of civilization itself. And it would give that company the scale, the capital, and the structure to pursue ambitions that neither company could pursue alone.

Why now: the case for a merger

The case for a merger rests on several arguments, and each of them has grown stronger in recent years. The first is capital. SpaceX's ambitions — Starship, Starlink, a Mars program — require enormous amounts of money, and the company, as a private entity, raises that money from a limited pool of investors. Tesla, as a public company with a trillion-dollar market capitalization, has access to the public markets, to a far larger pool of capital, and to a stock that can be used as currency for acquisitions and for investment. A merged company could fund SpaceX's ambitions with Tesla's access to capital, and it could do so at a scale that SpaceX, on its own, cannot match.

The second argument is technology. The two companies are, in Musk's vision, working on different parts of the same problem: the problem of building a sustainable, multiplanetary civilization. Tesla's batteries power electric vehicles; SpaceX's rockets carry satellites that provide internet to the world; Tesla's AI drives cars; SpaceX's AI — through xAI, Musk's artificial intelligence company, which is closely tied to both — powers Grok, the chatbot that is now at the center of a legal and political storm. A merged company could integrate these technologies more deeply, sharing research, sharing talent, and sharing the data that makes AI work.

The third argument is Starlink. The satellite internet constellation, which SpaceX operates, is one of the most valuable assets in the world — a network of thousands of satellites that provides internet access to places that terrestrial networks cannot reach. Starlink's value is, in part, a function of its scale, and its scale is a function of SpaceX's launch capability. But Starlink's future is also tied to Tesla's: to the connected cars that need constant internet, to the energy systems that need remote monitoring, to the robots that will need to communicate. A merged company could integrate Starlink into Tesla's products in ways that a separate company cannot — and it could use Tesla's manufacturing scale to build the terminals, the antennas, and the devices that Starlink needs.

The fourth argument is the simplest, and perhaps the most important: Musk himself. He is the chief executive of both companies, and he is stretched across them — and across xAI, Neuralink, and The Boring Company as well. A merger would simplify his role, consolidate his attention, and give him a single company to lead, rather than two. It would also consolidate his control: as the largest shareholder in both companies, a merger would make him the dominant shareholder in a combined entity that would be among the largest companies in the world.

The precedent: Tesla and SolarCity

The proposed merger has a precedent, and it is one that Musk himself created. In 2016, Tesla acquired SolarCity, the solar energy company that Musk had co-founded and chaired, in a deal worth about $2.6 billion. The acquisition was controversial at the time: SolarCity was losing money, it was heavily indebted, and critics argued that Tesla was bailing out a company in which Musk had a personal financial interest. Shareholders sued, alleging that Musk had used his control of Tesla to benefit himself, and the case went to trial in Delaware. Musk won — the court found that the acquisition, while flawed in its process, was fair in its price — but the controversy has never fully faded.

The SolarCity deal is the closest analogy to a SpaceX-Tesla merger, and it is a cautionary one. It shows that Musk is willing to combine his companies when he believes the strategic logic is compelling, and that he is willing to face the legal and shareholder challenges that follow. It also shows that the challenges are real: the SolarCity acquisition generated years of litigation, and it remains a case study in the governance risks of a chief executive who controls multiple companies and moves assets between them.

A SpaceX-Tesla merger would be SolarCity on a vastly larger scale. The companies are worth hundreds of billions of dollars each. The shareholders number in the millions. The regulatory scrutiny would be intense, and the litigation — if the deal is perceived as favoring one set of shareholders over another, or as benefiting Musk at the expense of others — would be inevitable. The five directors appointed to oversee the discussions will be aware of this history, and they will be aware that the deal they are examining will be judged against it.

The obstacles: regulation, valuation, and governance

A merger of SpaceX and Tesla would face formidable obstacles, and the first of them is regulation. SpaceX is a defense contractor — it launches satellites for the United States military and for the intelligence community, and it holds security clearances that are among the most sensitive in the country. Tesla is a global automobile manufacturer, with factories in the United States, China, and Germany, and with operations in dozens of countries. A merger would combine a defense contractor with a consumer products company, and it would do so under a chief executive who is also a senior adviser to the President of the United States. The regulatory review — by the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, by the Federal Trade Commission, by the Department of Defense, and by regulators in Europe and China — would be among the most complex ever conducted.

The second obstacle is valuation. SpaceX is a private company, and its valuation — reported in the hundreds of billions of dollars — is based on private funding rounds, not on a public market. Tesla is a public company, and its valuation is set every day by the stock market. Agreeing on the relative value of the two companies — on how many shares of the combined entity the shareholders of each should receive — would be one of the most difficult negotiations in corporate history, and it would be a negotiation in which Musk, as the largest shareholder in both, has a direct financial interest on both sides.

The third obstacle is governance. A merged company would be one of the largest and most complex in the world, spanning industries — space, automotive, energy, robotics, artificial intelligence — that have little in common except for the man who runs them. Governing such a company would require a board, a management team, and a structure that could oversee businesses as different as launching rockets and building cars. It would require a chief executive who could do both, and Musk — for all his talents — is one person, with finite time and attention.

And then there is the question that hangs over everything Musk does: the legal and political storm surrounding his companies. SpaceX is, through its subsidiary SpaceX Super Intelligence, a defendant in lawsuits filed by the states of Virginia and Oregon over Grok, the AI chatbot, and the sexualized images it has generated. Tesla faces its own legal challenges, from autopilot lawsuits to labor disputes. A merged company would inherit all of these problems, and it would inherit them in a single entity — concentrating the risk, and the scrutiny, in one place.

What it would mean for the world

If the merger happens, it would create a company unlike any that has existed before. It would be a company that builds rockets and cars, that operates a satellite internet constellation and an energy business, that develops humanoid robots and artificial intelligence, and that does so under a single leader with a vision that spans the Earth and Mars. It would be, in Musk's framing, a company that is building the future — and it would be, by market capitalization, one of the largest companies in the world.

For the space industry, a merged SpaceX-Tesla would mean a space company with access to public capital markets, and with the manufacturing scale of the world's largest electric vehicle maker. For the auto industry, it would mean a car company with a space division, a satellite network, and an AI capability that no other automaker possesses. For the energy industry, it would mean a company that spans generation, storage, and consumption — from solar panels to batteries to electric vehicles to the satellites that monitor them. And for the technology industry, it would mean a competitor that operates across every frontier at once.

For Musk himself, a merger would be the consolidation of an empire — the formal unification of the companies that have defined his career, and that have made him the richest person in the world. It would give him a single vehicle for his ambitions, and a single platform from which to pursue them. And it would make him, if the merger is structured as expected, the controlling shareholder of a company whose value would be measured in the trillions.

Whether that is a good thing — for the shareholders, for the customers, for the industries involved, and for the public — is a question that the five directors, the boards, the shareholders, and the regulators will have to answer. It is a question that will take months to resolve, and that will generate debate, litigation, and scrutiny at every step. But the fact that the question is being asked at all — that SpaceX and Tesla have appointed directors to examine a merger — is itself a measure of how far Musk's companies have come, and of how seriously the world now takes the idea that they might become one.

The xAI factor: Grok, lawsuits, and the third company

Any discussion of a SpaceX-Tesla merger must account for a third company: xAI, Musk's artificial intelligence venture, which is closely tied to both. xAI builds Grok, the AI chatbot that has become one of the most controversial products in Musk's empire — and one of the most legally exposed. In recent days, the states of Virginia and Oregon filed lawsuits against Musk's companies, including SpaceX and its subsidiary SpaceX Super Intelligence, over Grok's generation of sexualized images, including images that appeared to depict children. The lawsuits, the first by states against the chatbot, allege that Grok's safeguards were stripped, that its "spicy mode" was marketed to answer questions other AI systems refuse, and that millions of sexualized images were posted on X.

The xAI connection matters for a merger in two ways. First, Grok has been integrated into Tesla's vehicles, where it serves as a voice assistant — a link between the AI company and the car company that is already a reality, not a plan. Second, xAI's computing infrastructure — the massive data centers it has built, including the Colossus supercomputer in Memphis — is among the largest in the world, and it is an asset that a merged SpaceX-Tesla could draw on for everything from self-driving to Starship design to the analysis of satellite data.

But xAI is also a liability. The lawsuits in Virginia and Oregon are the first of what may be many, and they raise questions about the governance and the culture of Musk's companies that go beyond any single product. A merged SpaceX-Tesla would not include xAI — the reports concern only the two companies — but it would be closely associated with it, through Musk, through Grok's integration into Tesla vehicles, and through the shared infrastructure that links them. The five directors examining the merger will have to weigh that association, and the risks it carries, alongside the strategic logic of the deal.

The xAI story is, in the end, a reminder that Musk's empire is not two companies, or three. It is a network — of rockets and cars, of satellites and AI, of robots and tunnels — all connected by a single founder, a single vision, and a single set of risks. A merger of SpaceX and Tesla would make that network more formal, and more visible. But it would not make it any simpler.

The bottom line

SpaceX and Tesla have appointed five directors to oversee merger discussions, according to reports published on Monday. The talks are in an early stage, and no deal has been agreed. But the appointment of a formal group to examine a merger is a significant step, and it suggests that the two companies — and the man who runs them both — are seriously considering a combination that would create one of the largest and most consequential companies in history.

A merger would unite the world's most valuable private space company with the world's most valuable car company, under a single roof, with access to public capital, shared technology, and a vision that spans the Earth and Mars. It would also face formidable obstacles: regulatory review, valuation disputes, governance challenges, and the legal storm surrounding Musk's companies. The five directors have their work cut out for them. And the world will be watching what they decide.
