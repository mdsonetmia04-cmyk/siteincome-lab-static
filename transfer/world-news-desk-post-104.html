TITLE: Trump Just Invited Putin to the G20 in Florida — Then Told Him to Stop Attacking Europe LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Trump says Putin is welcome at the December 14-15 G20 summit in Florida — and told him to pull back military aggression across Europe. ---BODY---

Donald Trump, President of the United States. Speaking at his Florida golf resort on Thanksgiving Day, he said Vladimir Putin would be welcome at the G20 summit in Florida next month — and revealed he has told the Russian leader to pull back his military aggression across Europe. Photo: Official White House portrait, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Donald Trump spent his Thanksgiving Day doing two things that, in any other presidency, would seem impossible to do in the same afternoon. He invited Vladimir Putin to come to America. And he told Putin, in public, to stop attacking Europe. Speaking to reporters on Thursday, November 26, at his golf resort in Florida, the president said the Russian leader would be welcome at the G20 summit in Florida on December 14 and 15 — and then, in the same breath, revealed that he has been pressing Putin to pull back his military aggression in the hope of a quick deal to end the war in Ukraine.

"If he wanted to come, he certainly can," Trump said of Putin and the Florida summit. "If it were up to me, I'd like to have him." He added, with characteristic bluntness, that some people would not like to see Putin at the meeting, and that he doubted Putin himself would want to attend. The Kremlin, for its part, said on Thursday that no decision had been made yet on whether Putin would participate in the summit. Putin himself, after meeting Trump last week in China, said he would consider attending after discussing it with the American president — but that he would need to take the situation in Russia into account when deciding whether to travel to the United States.

The invitation is extraordinary on its own. Putin has not set foot in the United States since 2015, and he has been the subject of an arrest warrant from the International Criminal Court since 2023. The United States is not a party to the court, and the warrant would not oblige American authorities to detain him, but the optics of a Russian president under an ICC warrant attending a summit on American soil would be staggering. Trump is betting that the optics are worth it — that bringing Putin into the room is the fastest way to end a war that has now dragged on for nearly five years.

The message to Putin: pull back

Earlier on Thursday, Trump posted on Truth Social about the substance of his conversations with Putin, and the message was strikingly direct. "During my conversations in China with President Vladimir Putin of Russia, I spent a lot of time talking about the fact that I would like to see President Putin pull back all Military aggression against territories throughout Europe, in the hopes that a Deal can be made between Russia and Ukraine, and made quickly," Trump wrote. "I really believe this is a strong possibility."

The post matters because of what it reveals about the private diplomacy between the two men. Trump and Putin met last week in China, on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen, and the conversation was evidently longer and more detailed than either side has publicly acknowledged. Trump spent "a lot of time," by his own account, pressing Putin on the central question of the war: whether Russia is willing to stop its military operations across Europe as the price of a settlement. The phrase "throughout Europe" is deliberately broad — it covers not only Ukraine but the pattern of Russian pressure on the continent, from the Baltic states to the Black Sea, that has defined the last decade.

Trump's belief that a deal is a "strong possibility" is the most optimistic statement he has made about the war in months. It is also, characteristically, a statement that rests entirely on his personal relationship with Putin. The president's theory of the war has always been that it is a problem of personalities — that the fighting continues because the leaders involved do not get along, and that a deal becomes possible when they do. He said as much on Thursday, arguing that maintaining a cordial relationship with the Russian leader was important to the negotiations, and that openly expressing hostility toward Putin could complicate efforts to reach a settlement. He drew an explicit comparison with the strained relationship between Putin and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, implying that his own approach — engagement rather than confrontation — is what makes a deal possible.

"The bottom line is he would like to make a deal," Trump said of Putin. It is a claim he has made before, and one that has been tested repeatedly by events on the ground. Each time Trump has declared that Putin wants peace, Russia has continued its military operations. Each time the White House has announced progress, the fighting has gone on. The test of this latest claim will be the same as every previous one: what happens on the battlefield in the weeks after the words are spoken.

Thanksgiving at Mar-a-Lago: Rutte, the troops and the G8

The G20 invitation did not come out of nowhere. It followed a meeting at Mar-a-Lago between Trump and NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, and the joint statement issued after that meeting shows that the conversation was about far more than the summit. The two leaders discussed the Russia-Ukraine war and the conflict in Iran, including the need for Russia to avoid actions that could widen the conflict and the urgency of securing a peaceful settlement. The Iran reference is significant: the United States and Israel are in the middle of a war with Iran that has already spread across the Gulf, and Washington is anxious that Russia — Iran's most important great-power partner — does not escalate it further.

Trump also spent part of Thanksgiving on a call with American military personnel, alongside First Lady Melania Trump, from his Mar-a-Lago residence. He told the troops that his priority was to stop the ongoing loss of lives — a line that connects the Thanksgiving message to the G20 invitation and to the Truth Social post. The president's argument, repeated across all three settings, is consistent: the wars must end because people are dying, and the way to end them is to talk to the people waging them.

He also used the day to revisit one of his longest-standing grievances: Russia's expulsion from the Group of Eight. Trump recalled that Russia had once been a member of the G8, and said former President Barack Obama had wanted Moscow removed from the grouping. "I believe if they didn't do that, I really believe strongly that if they didn't do that, we would not have that war going on right now," Trump said. The argument — that excluding Russia from the club of leading economies set the country on the path to war — is one Trump has made for years, and it explains a great deal about his approach to Putin. In Trump's telling, Russia is not an adversary to be isolated but a great power to be accommodated, and the G20 invitation is the practical expression of that belief.

Russia was expelled from the G8 in 2014, after its annexation of Crimea, and the group has been the G7 ever since. The decision was taken by the other members collectively, not by Obama alone, and it was a response to a violation of international law that the United States and its allies regarded as fundamental. Trump's retelling of that history — as a mistake by a single president — is characteristic of the way he personalises geopolitics. But the policy consequence is real: a president who believes Russia should never have been expelled is a president who will always be looking for a way to bring it back in.

What the invitation would actually mean

If Putin does come to Florida, the summit on December 14 and 15 would become the most consequential diplomatic event of the year — and one of the most controversial. The G20 brings together the leaders of the world's largest economies, and its summits are normally choreographed months in advance, with agendas negotiated line by line. A Putin appearance would dominate everything else on the agenda. It would also force every other leader in the room to make a decision: whether to shake his hand, whether to sit in the same meetings, whether to be photographed alongside a man under an ICC warrant for war crimes in Ukraine.

The reaction from America's allies would be sharp. European leaders, who have spent the last four years building a sanctions regime against Russia and arming Ukraine, would see a Putin visit to Florida as a betrayal of the coalition they have constructed. Zelenskyy, who has watched Trump grow closer to Putin even as Russian missiles continue to fall on Ukrainian cities, would see it as further evidence that Washington's priorities have shifted. And the American public, which has been told for years that Putin is an adversary, would have to reconcile that message with the image of the Russian president being welcomed onto American soil by an American president.

Trump is aware of all of this. His acknowledgment that "some people would not like to see Putin" at the summit is an understatement so large it is almost a joke. He knows the invitation will infuriate Europe. He knows it will alarm Kyiv. He knows it will dominate the news for weeks. He is doing it anyway, because his calculation is that the political cost is worth paying if it produces a deal — and because he believes, genuinely, that his personal relationship with Putin is the asset that makes a deal possible.

There is also the practical question of whether Putin would actually come. The Kremlin's statement — no decision yet — is the language of a government keeping its options open. Putin said after the China meeting that he would consider attending, but that he would need to take the situation in Russia into account. That is a careful formulation. Travelling to the United States would expose Putin to risks he has spent years avoiding: the ICC warrant, the security complications, the political optics of leaving Moscow while a war is being fought. It would also put him in a room where he could be pressed, in person, by leaders who have spent four years opposing him. The G20 is not a bilateral meeting with Trump. It is a summit of twenty governments, most of which have condemned the invasion of Ukraine.

Trump's own doubt — that Putin would not want to attend — may be the most realistic assessment offered by anyone on Thursday. The invitation is real, and the welcome is genuine. But the man being invited has every reason to stay home.

The other Thanksgiving message

The G20 invitation was not the only Trump message on Thanksgiving Day. The president also posted a 461-word Thanksgiving message on Truth Social that attacked liberals, immigrants and a federal economic data agency, making a series of unsubstantiated claims about economic data, immigration and crime. The message, which demanded an investigation into revised GDP figures, was of a piece with the president's recent attacks on the Bureau of Economic Analysis — the agency whose revised figures showed third-quarter growth of 4.3 percent, a number Trump has called "fake" and demanded be investigated.

The contrast between the two messages captures something essential about this presidency. In one, Trump is the peacemaker, inviting his counterpart to Florida and pressing for a deal to end a war. In the other, he is the pugilist, attacking his domestic opponents and the institutions that produce the numbers he does not like. Both messages were posted on the same day, from the same account, by the same man. They are not contradictions. They are two sides of the same approach to power: personal, direct, and indifferent to the conventions that used to govern both diplomacy and domestic politics.

The Thanksgiving message also shows the limits of Trump's optimism about Putin. A president who is willing to accuse his own government's statisticians of fabricating economic data is a president whose trust is selective. He trusts Putin's word that he wants a deal. He does not trust the Bureau of Economic Analysis's word that the economy grew by 4.3 percent. The pattern is not random: Trump trusts the people he has a personal relationship with, and distrusts the institutions he does not control. That is why the G20 invitation matters so much — and why it is so risky. The entire strategy rests on a personal judgment about a man who has misled American presidents before.

The deal that may or may not exist

Strip away the theatre, and the question is simple: is there a deal to be made? Trump believes there is, and he believes the path to it runs through his relationship with Putin. The evidence for that belief is thin. Russia has not withdrawn from any Ukrainian territory it occupies. It has not stopped its missile attacks on Ukrainian cities. It has not signalled any willingness to accept a settlement that Ukraine could accept. The prisoner swap that Trump's envoy Jared Kushner has been negotiating — the largest Russia-Ukraine exchange of the war, with 1,200 to 1,300 prisoners on each side — is real progress, but a prisoner swap is not a peace deal. It is a humanitarian gesture that both sides can make without giving up anything they care about.

The G20 invitation is, in that sense, a test. If Putin comes to Florida, Trump will have the opportunity he has been seeking: a face-to-face meeting, on American soil, with the cameras watching, in which he can press for the deal he believes is possible. If Putin does not come, the invitation will stand as another example of Trump's willingness to offer what his predecessors would not — and of Putin's unwillingness to take it. Either way, the summit on December 14 and 15 will be the moment when the president's theory of the war is tested in public.

For Ukraine, the stakes are existential. A deal made between Trump and Putin, without Kyiv at the table, would be a settlement imposed on the country that has done the fighting. Zelenskyy has said repeatedly that no agreement about Ukraine can be made without Ukraine. Trump's approach — negotiating with Putin first, and presenting the result to Kyiv afterwards — is the opposite of that principle. The G20 invitation, if it leads to a meeting, would be the most dramatic expression of that approach yet.

For Europe, the stakes are almost as high. A G20 summit at which Putin is welcomed back into the club would signal that the isolation strategy of the last four years is over. The sanctions, the diplomatic freeze, the exclusion from international forums — all of it would be called into question by a single photograph of Putin in Florida. European leaders know this, and they will be lobbying Washington intensely in the weeks before December 14. Whether they can change Trump's mind is an open question. The president has heard their arguments before, and he has not been persuaded.

The invitation has been issued. The ball is now in Moscow's court. In the next two and a half weeks, the world will find out whether Vladimir Putin is willing to walk into a room full of his adversaries, on the soil of the country that leads them, at the invitation of a president who believes he can make a deal. It is the kind of gamble that defines a presidency — and the kind that, if it fails, defines it too.

The road to Florida: Alaska, Budapest and Shenzhen

The G20 invitation is the latest chapter in a diplomatic story that has been running for more than a year, and it helps to remember how many times it has already changed direction. In August 2025, Trump and Putin met in Anchorage, Alaska, for a summit that was billed as the moment the war would end. It did not. The two men spoke for hours, appeared together before the cameras, and left without an agreement — and the war went on. The Alaska meeting showed both the promise and the limits of Trump's approach: he could get Putin into a room, but getting him into a room was not the same as getting him to stop fighting.

Two months later, in October 2025, a second summit was planned for Budapest. It was cancelled before it happened, after disagreements over the terms of a settlement and over the American peace framework that had been circulating — a plan, associated with Trump's envoy Steve Witkoff and his son-in-law Jared Kushner, that would have required Ukraine to make territorial concessions in exchange for security guarantees. The cancellation was a reminder that the gap between the two sides is not a matter of misunderstanding. It is a matter of substance: Russia wants territory and a veto over Ukraine's future; Ukraine wants its land back and the freedom to choose its own alliances. No amount of personal chemistry has yet bridged that gap.

The Shenzhen meeting last week, on the sidelines of the APEC summit in China, was the third attempt in four months. It produced the conversation Trump described on Thursday — the long discussion about pulling back military aggression across Europe — and it produced the G20 invitation. Whether it produced anything else is the question that will decide whether the Florida summit matters. The pattern of the last year suggests caution: each meeting has generated optimism, and each burst of optimism has been followed by more fighting.

There is also the question of what, exactly, Trump is offering Putin in exchange for a pullback. The prisoner swap that Kushner has been negotiating — the largest of the war, with 1,200 to 1,300 prisoners on each side — is a humanitarian measure, not a concession. A G20 invitation, on the other hand, is a concession of a different kind: it offers Putin the international legitimacy he has been denied since 2022, the chance to sit at the table with the world's leading economies as an equal. If Trump is offering that legitimacy in exchange for a ceasefire, it is a significant bargain. If he is offering it for nothing, it is a significant gift.

The warrant hanging over the invitation

There is one more complication that no amount of presidential warmth can wish away. In March 2023, the International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Vladimir Putin, alleging responsibility for the unlawful deportation of Ukrainian children to Russia. The warrant obliges the court's 125 member states to arrest him if he enters their territory. The United States is not a member of the court, and it has no legal obligation to execute the warrant. But the warrant shapes everything about a Putin visit to America: the security arrangements, the diplomatic choreography, and the reaction of every ICC member state whose leader would be sitting in the same room.

Putin has avoided travelling to ICC member states since the warrant was issued, and his foreign travel has been limited to countries that have given him explicit assurances. The United States would not arrest him — the Trump administration has made its position on the court clear, and has in fact sanctioned it. But the warrant means that Putin's presence at the G20 would be, for many of the leaders in the room, a direct challenge to an institution they support. It is one more reason for the Russian leader to think carefully before accepting, and one more reason for Trump's allies to think carefully about the invitation itself.

What to watch

The first signal will come from the Kremlin: any statement about Putin's participation, or any movement in the preparations for the Florida summit, will be read as a sign of intent. The second will come from Kyiv and the European capitals, where the reaction to the invitation will shape the pressure on Washington. The third will come on the battlefield, where the pace of Russian operations will show whether the China conversation produced any change in behaviour. And the fourth will come on December 14, when the leaders gather in Florida — and the world sees whether the seat reserved for Russia is filled or empty.
