TITLE: BepiColombo Finally Reaches Mercury After 8 Years — but a Glitch Left Its Orbit in Doubt LABELS: Science SEARCH DESCRIPTION: After eight years in space, Europe's BepiColombo spacecraft reached Mercury orbit — but an early braking burn left its final orbit uncertain. ---BODY---

Mercury in enhanced color, imaged by NASA's MESSENGER spacecraft. Photo: NASA / Johns Hopkins University Applied Physics Laboratory / Carnegie Institution of Washington (public domain).

After eight years in space, after a journey of billions of kilometers and nine gravity assists, the BepiColombo spacecraft has finally reached Mercury. The European-Japanese mission — one of the most ambitious planetary expeditions ever attempted — arrived at the Solar System's innermost planet this week, on November 21, 2026, and began the maneuver that was supposed to settle it into orbit around the smallest and least explored of the rocky planets. But the arrival did not go entirely as planned. A braking burn — the engine firing that was meant to slow the spacecraft and capture it into Mercury's gravity — ended early, and a software glitch left the spacecraft in an uncertain state, with engineers on Earth working to determine exactly where it is, and what comes next.

It is a dramatic, and deeply human, moment in the history of space exploration: a mission that took eight years to reach its destination, arriving only to find that the final step — the one that matters most — did not go as planned. But it is also a reminder of why missions like BepiColombo exist, and what they are trying to learn. Mercury is the least understood of the rocky planets, a world of extremes that holds the key to how the Solar System formed. And BepiColombo, whatever its current state, is the most sophisticated spacecraft ever sent to study it. Here is what happened, what the mission is, and what the rest of the week brought from the frontiers of space.

The journey: eight years, nine gravity assists, one destination

BepiColombo was launched in October 2018, aboard an Ariane 5 rocket from French Guiana, as a joint mission of the European Space Agency and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency. It is, in fact, two spacecraft flying together: the Mercury Planetary Orbiter, built by ESA, which will study the planet's surface and interior; and the Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, known as Mio, built by JAXA, which will study the planet's magnetic field and its interaction with the solar wind. Together, the two orbiters carry 16 scientific instruments, and together they represent the most comprehensive study of Mercury ever attempted.

The journey to Mercury was not a straight line. It could not be. Mercury orbits so close to the Sun that a spacecraft traveling directly toward it would be pulled in by the Sun's gravity and accelerated to speeds that no engine could counter. Instead, BepiColombo took the long way — a looping, eight-year trajectory that used the gravity of the planets themselves to slow it down. The spacecraft flew past Earth once, past Venus twice, and past Mercury six times, each flyby stealing a little of the spacecraft's energy and bending its path a little closer to its destination. It was a journey of extraordinary precision, planned years in advance, and executed with a patience that is rare in any human endeavor.

The British-built spacecraft — the mission's propulsion and structure were led by the United Kingdom, with contributions from across Europe and Japan — traveled billions of kilometers over those eight years, and it did so while enduring conditions that no spacecraft had endured before. Near the Sun, the spacecraft's solar arrays were heated to temperatures that would melt lead. Its thermal shielding, designed to protect it from the Sun's glare, was tested to its limits. And its ion engines, which provided the gentle, continuous thrust that shaped its trajectory, fired for thousands of hours — longer than any ion propulsion system had ever operated in deep space.

On November 21, 2026, the journey ended — or, at least, it was supposed to. The spacecraft arrived at Mercury and began its braking burn, the final maneuver that would slow it enough for Mercury's gravity to capture it into orbit. It was the moment the entire mission had been building toward. And it was the moment that went wrong.

The glitch: a braking burn that ended early

What happened during the braking burn is still being established, and the details are, as of this writing, incomplete. What is known is that the burn ended early — that the engine firing stopped before it was supposed to — and that a software glitch was involved, leaving the spacecraft in an uncertain state. The spacecraft is in communication with Earth, and engineers are working to determine its exact trajectory, its velocity, and the orbit — if any — that it is now in.

The situation is serious, but it is not unprecedented. Spacecraft have recovered from worse. The history of planetary exploration is full of missions that arrived at their destinations in unexpected states and were saved by the ingenuity of the engineers on the ground — by revised maneuvers, by new software, by patience and by improvisation. BepiColombo was designed with margins, and its mission plan includes contingency options for exactly this kind of event. Whether those options will be enough depends on the details of the spacecraft's current state, and those details are still being determined.

What is at stake is considerable. BepiColombo is the first mission to Mercury since NASA's MESSENGER, which orbited the planet from 2011 to 2015 and which transformed our understanding of it. MESSENGER showed that Mercury has water ice in its permanently shadowed polar craters, that its surface is rich in volatile elements that should not have survived so close to the Sun, and that its magnetic field — weak, but real — is generated by a liquid outer core. BepiColombo was designed to go further: to map the planet's surface in unprecedented detail, to study its interior structure, to understand its magnetic field, and to answer the question that MESSENGER left open — how a planet so small, so close to the Sun, came to be the way it is.

The two orbiters carry instruments that MESSENGER did not, and they will observe Mercury from two positions at once — one close to the planet, one farther out — giving scientists a view of the planet and its environment that no single spacecraft could provide. If the mission can be saved, it will transform our understanding of Mercury, and of the formation of the Solar System itself. If it cannot, it will be one of the most painful losses in the history of planetary exploration — a mission that traveled eight years and billions of kilometers, only to falter at the final step.

Mercury: the planet we know least

To understand why BepiColombo matters, it helps to understand how little we know about Mercury. It is the smallest planet in the Solar System, with a diameter of about 4,880 kilometers — barely larger than the Moon. It is the closest planet to the Sun, orbiting at an average distance of 58 million kilometers, and it races around the Sun in just 88 days, at nearly 47 kilometers per second — the fastest of any planet. Its surface is a landscape of craters, scarred by billions of years of impacts, and its temperature swings are the most extreme in the Solar System: from more than 400 degrees Celsius on the sunlit side to less than minus 180 degrees in the shadowed craters at the poles.

For most of the history of astronomy, Mercury was almost impossible to study. It is so close to the Sun that it is rarely visible from Earth, and when it is visible, it is only for a short time at dawn or dusk, low on the horizon, through the thickest part of the atmosphere. Telescopes could see little more than a blurry disk. The first spacecraft to visit Mercury was NASA's Mariner 10, which flew past the planet three times in 1974 and 1975 and photographed about half of its surface. For more than thirty years, that was all we had — half a planet, seen in passing, from a spacecraft that was never designed to stay.

MESSENGER changed that. Launched in 2004, it flew past Mercury three times before entering orbit in 2011, and it spent four years circling the planet, mapping its entire surface, measuring its gravity and its magnetic field, and discovering the water ice in its polar craters. It showed that Mercury is not the dead, airless rock that astronomers had assumed, but a dynamic world with a complex history — a world that had volcanic activity, that had a magnetic field, and that held surprises that no one had predicted. And then, in 2015, it ran out of fuel and crashed into the surface, ending its mission in the way that all Mercury orbiters must eventually end.

BepiColombo was designed to be MESSENGER's successor and its superior — to answer the questions that MESSENGER raised and could not answer, and to study the planet with instruments that MESSENGER did not carry. The mission's two orbiters, working together, would have given scientists the most complete picture of Mercury ever obtained. Whether they will still get that chance depends on what happens in the coming days.

The rest of the week in space

BepiColombo was not the only space story of the week. On Mars, NASA's Curiosity rover — which has been exploring the Red Planet since 2012 — found chip-like crystal formations at a site called Nazca, a discovery that has intrigued planetary scientists and that adds to the growing picture of Mars as a world that was once far wetter, and far more habitable, than it is today. The formations, which resemble chips or flakes of mineral, are the kind of feature that forms in the presence of water, and their discovery at Nazca is another piece of evidence that Mars, billions of years ago, had the conditions that life requires.

And far beyond Mars, beyond the planets entirely, Voyager 1 — the most distant human-made object in history — has now traveled more than one light-day from Earth. Launched in 1977, Voyager 1 has been traveling outward for 49 years, and it is now in interstellar space, the region beyond the Sun's influence, sending back data from a place that no human-made object has ever been. A light-day is the distance that light travels in 24 hours — about 26 billion kilometers — and Voyager 1 has now passed it, a milestone that is both a technical achievement and a poetic one: a spacecraft built in the 1970s, carrying a golden record of human civilization, now more than a day's light-travel from the world that built it.

These are different stories, from different missions, at different distances. But they share a theme. They are stories about the human desire to know — to send a spacecraft eight years to Mercury, to drive a rover across the surface of Mars, to listen to a probe that has traveled beyond the Sun's reach. They are stories about patience, about engineering, and about the willingness to attempt things that may fail. And they are stories that remind us, in a week when the news on Earth has been dominated by politics and conflict, that there is a larger story unfolding — the story of a species reaching outward, into the dark, and learning what is there.

What happens next for BepiColombo

In the coming days, the engineers at ESA's mission control in Darmstadt, Germany, and at JAXA in Japan, will work to determine BepiColombo's exact state. They will analyze the telemetry — the data the spacecraft sends back about its position, its velocity, its systems — and they will model the trajectory that the early end of the braking burn has left it on. They will determine whether the spacecraft is in orbit around Mercury, in a different orbit than planned, or on a trajectory that will carry it past the planet and back into space.

If the spacecraft is in an orbit — even a wrong one — the mission may be salvageable. Orbits can be adjusted. Engines can be fired again. The mission plan, which was designed with margins for exactly this kind of event, may allow the spacecraft to be maneuvered into its intended orbit over weeks or months. If the spacecraft is not in orbit, the situation is more difficult, but not necessarily hopeless: a spacecraft that misses its capture can sometimes be brought back, with careful planning and with the fuel that remains.

What is certain is that the coming days will be tense for the mission team, and for the planetary science community that has waited eight years for BepiColombo to arrive. The spacecraft represents the work of thousands of people, across two space agencies and more than a dozen countries, over more than two decades. Its loss would be a blow to European and Japanese space science, and to the study of Mercury, that would be felt for years.

But the history of space exploration suggests that it is too early to give up. Spacecraft have been saved from worse. Missions have been rescued from failures that seemed final. And the engineers who built BepiColombo, and who have guided it for eight years across billions of kilometers, are among the best in the world at the work they do. The spacecraft is in communication. The team is working. And the planet is still there, waiting.

How BepiColombo will study Mercury

If the mission can be saved, what BepiColombo will do at Mercury is extraordinary. The two orbiters are designed to work as a team, observing the planet from different positions at the same time — a capability that no previous Mercury mission has had. The Mercury Planetary Orbiter, built by ESA, will fly in a low, close orbit, mapping the planet's surface in high resolution, measuring its topography with a laser altimeter, and studying its composition with spectrometers that can identify the minerals in its rocks. The Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, known as Mio and built by JAXA, will fly in a wider, more elliptical orbit, studying the planet's magnetic field and the way it interacts with the solar wind — the stream of charged particles that flows outward from the Sun and that, at Mercury's distance, is far more intense than it is at Earth.

Together, the two orbiters carry 16 scientific instruments, and together they are designed to answer the questions that have puzzled planetary scientists since MESSENGER. Why is Mercury so dense? The planet's core — a ball of iron and nickel — accounts for about 85 percent of its radius, a proportion far higher than any other planet in the Solar System. Theories abound: that Mercury was once larger, and that a giant impact stripped away its outer layers; that the Sun's heat vaporized the lighter elements from its surface; that it formed from metal-rich material in the inner Solar System. BepiColombo's measurements of the planet's gravity, its composition, and its interior structure are designed to distinguish between these theories — and to tell us how the innermost planet came to be.

Another question is the magnetic field. Mercury has a global magnetic field — weak, about a hundred times weaker than Earth's, but real — and its existence is a puzzle. A planet as small as Mercury should have cooled long ago, and its iron core should have solidified, shutting down the dynamo that generates a magnetic field. Yet the field persists, and MESSENGER showed that it is offset from the planet's center and tilted relative to its rotation axis, in ways that no simple model can explain. BepiColombo's magnetometers, on both orbiters, will measure the field in unprecedented detail, and they may finally reveal how it is generated — and what it tells us about the planet's interior.

And then there is the water. MESSENGER's most surprising discovery was that Mercury — the closest planet to the Sun, with surface temperatures above 400 degrees Celsius — has water ice in the permanently shadowed craters at its poles, where the Sun never shines and the temperature never rises above minus 170 degrees. The ice is a reminder that the Solar System is full of surprises, and that the most hostile places can harbor the most unexpected things. BepiColombo will map the ice in detail, measure its extent, and study the craters that contain it — and in doing so, it will add to our understanding of how water, the essential ingredient for life, is distributed through the Solar System.

The cost of getting to Mercury

BepiColombo is one of the most expensive planetary missions ever flown, at a reported cost of around 1.65 billion euros, and the reasons for that cost are written into the physics of getting to Mercury. The planet is, in some ways, the hardest destination in the Solar System to reach — harder, in terms of the energy required, than the outer planets. A spacecraft traveling to Mercury must fight the Sun's gravity every step of the way, shedding speed as it falls inward, and it must do so while enduring temperatures that would destroy an unprotected spacecraft.

The solution that BepiColombo's engineers chose was patience: an eight-year trajectory, nine gravity assists, and ion engines that fired for thousands of hours, providing a gentle, continuous thrust that shaped the spacecraft's path. It was a solution that traded time for energy, and it worked — until the final maneuver. The cost of that patience is measured not only in euros, but in years: the mission was approved in the early 2000s, launched in 2018, and arrived in 2026. The scientists who proposed it have spent their careers waiting for it. The engineers who built it have spent theirs guiding it. And the data it returns, if it returns it, will shape planetary science for a generation.

That is why the current uncertainty is so painful, and why the effort to save the mission will be so determined. BepiColombo is not just a spacecraft. It is the culmination of decades of work, by thousands of people, across two space agencies and more than a dozen countries. It is the most ambitious attempt ever made to understand the Solar System's innermost planet. And it is, for the moment, in doubt — a spacecraft that traveled eight years and billions of kilometers, waiting to learn whether its journey will end in orbit, or in the void.

The bottom line

After eight years in space, the BepiColombo spacecraft reached Mercury on November 21, 2026 — but a braking burn ended early, and a software glitch left the spacecraft in an uncertain state. Engineers at ESA and JAXA are working to determine its exact trajectory and whether the mission can be saved. BepiColombo, a joint European-Japanese mission carrying two orbiters and 16 instruments, is the most sophisticated spacecraft ever sent to Mercury, and its loss would be a major blow to planetary science.

Elsewhere in space this week, NASA's Curiosity rover found chip-like crystal formations at a site called Nazca on Mars, and Voyager 1 — launched in 1977 — has now traveled more than one light-day from Earth, into interstellar space. It is a week that reminds us why we explore: because the questions are worth asking, and because the answers, when they come, are worth the wait — even when the wait is eight years, and the arrival does not go as planned.
