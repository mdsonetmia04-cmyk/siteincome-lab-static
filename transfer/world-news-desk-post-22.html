TITLE: California Congressman Jimmy Gomez Is Resigning on December 1 After Ethics Panel Finds "Substantial Evidence" of Sexual Misconduct LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Rep. Jimmy Gomez will resign on Dec. 1 after the House Ethics Committee found substantial evidence of sexual misconduct toward a staffer. ---BODY---

Representative Jimmy Gomez of California. Photo: US House Office of Photography / Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

Democratic Representative Jimmy Gomez of California announced on Monday, November 23, that he will resign from Congress effective December 1, 2026, within an hour of the House Ethics Committee releasing a report that found "substantial evidence" he engaged in sexual misconduct toward a House staffer. The bipartisan committee, which includes five Democrats and five Republicans, concluded that Gomez violated House rules by "making unwelcome sexual advances and engaging in unwelcome sexual conduct towards a House staffer and disregarding the power imbalance inherent in his relationship with House staffers." The panel said his conduct "could constitute criminal misdemeanor sexual abuse" under the laws of Washington, DC. It was a devastating end to a congressional career that began in 2017 — and it made Gomez the third member of Congress to resign this year in the face of sexual misconduct allegations.

The report, released on Monday as part of a tranche of investigative findings, was the culmination of a months-long investigation that began in August, after a press report that Gomez had been seen kissing a staffer from another congressional office at a party. The committee said it had first learned in April 2026 that Gomez was seen at a party kissing a House of Representatives staffer. In response to the committee's inquiry, Gomez disclosed interactions with two separate women, describing the encounters as consensual. The committee, however, found the staffer at the centre of the case credible — and concluded that Gomez had failed in his obligation to behave in a manner that reflects creditably on the House, and that his conduct was exacerbated by his failure to appreciate the inherent power imbalance between members of Congress and their staff.

Gomez, who turns 52 this week and has been married for more than a decade, was already set to leave Congress in January. Earlier this month, he lost his re-election bid to fellow Democrat Angela Gonzales-Torres by nearly 25 points, a crushing defeat in a seat he had held for almost a decade. His resignation on December 1 simply brings forward by a month a departure that the voters had already ordered. But the manner of it — a damning ethics report, a finding of sexual misconduct, and a resignation announced within the hour — ensures that Gomez will be remembered not for his decade in Congress, but for the report that ended it.

What the Ethics Committee found

The House Ethics Committee's report is unsparing. It found that Gomez made unwelcome sexual advances toward a House staffer, that he engaged in unwelcome sexual conduct with her, and that he did so while disregarding the power imbalance inherent in his relationship with House staffers — the imbalance between a member of Congress, who holds power over hiring, firing and careers, and a young staffer who depends on that world for her livelihood. The committee said Gomez's conduct caused the staffer "significant and ongoing distress" and contributed to her decision to leave House employment altogether. And it said his conduct "could implicate D.C. laws related to criminal sexual assault, including misdemeanor sexual abuse," a finding that carries implications far beyond the House's own disciplinary process.

The woman at the centre of the report, identified only as Witness 1, was 20 years younger than Gomez. She told investigators that Gomez made sexual contact with her without her consent at a bar in Washington, DC, in February 2024, and that he was later forceful with her and tried to kiss her in his congressional office in July 2024. According to CNN, which reviewed the report, the woman told the panel that Gomez put his hand down her skirt during one encounter. She said she had more than one encounter with Gomez and did not consider any of them consensual. The committee found her credible. Gomez, for his part, later admitted to making sexual contact with her but insisted the encounter was consensual — a claim the committee rejected.

The report also found that the case was not an isolated incident. Gomez disclosed to the committee that he had interactions with two women. With one of them, he described one occasion of consensual kissing. With Witness 1, the committee found a pattern of unwelcome conduct, spread across months and across settings — a bar, and then his own office. The committee concluded that Gomez had violated the House's rules, that he had failed to appreciate the power imbalance between a member and a staffer, and that his conduct reflected "a broader environment of sexual misconduct and toxic working conditions in some Capitol Hill offices which the Committee is determined to address." That last sentence was one of the most striking in the report: it was not just an indictment of one man, but of a culture.

Gomez's response: responsibility, and defiance

Within an hour of the report being made public, Gomez issued a statement announcing his resignation — and a defence. He said he recognised the seriousness of the Ethics Committee's report and took "full responsibility for the personal mistakes I committed outside of my marriage." But he also said he had "strong disagreements with the Committee's report and its conclusions." The report, he said, "does not include key portions of my testimony that are essential to a full and fair understanding of what occurred," and he raised "serious concerns about the process," saying he had answered the committee's questions "only to see my statements selectively quoted, disparaged, and disregarded throughout the final report, to which I was given no opportunity to respond."

"With this in mind, I see no other alternative than to resign, effective December 1, to allow for my staff to close out important constituent casework and needs," Gomez wrote. It was a statement that tried to do two things at once: to accept responsibility for the personal conduct that had brought him to this point, and to reject the committee's characterisation of that conduct as non-consensual misconduct. The committee, in its report, had anticipated that defence — and rejected it, finding the staffer credible and concluding that Gomez's conduct violated House rules regardless of how he characterised it.

The staffer's lawyers, Lisa Banks and Sarah Nesbitt, issued their own statement, saying their client had testified to the committee truthfully and at personal risk, and that the panel's report "confirms that Representative Gomez sexually harassed and assaulted our client while she was a staffer on Capitol Hill." The contrast between the two statements — his, emphasising process and disagreement; theirs, emphasising truth and risk — captures the central tension of the case. One side sees a flawed process that produced an unfair conclusion. The other sees a powerful man who used his position to prey on a young staffer, and a committee that finally believed her.

How the investigation unfolded

The road to Monday's report began in April 2026, when the Ethics Committee learned that Gomez had been seen kissing a House staffer at a party. It was, at that point, a rumour — a report of a congressman behaving inappropriately at a social event. But the committee took it seriously enough to make an inquiry, and in response, Gomez disclosed that he had had interactions with two women. In August, the committee formally announced its investigation, saying it was reviewing allegations that Gomez "may have engaged in sexual misconduct in violation of the Code of Official Conduct or any other applicable standard of conduct, including engaging in inappropriate sexual contact with a House staffer." Gomez denied the allegations at the time.

What followed was a months-long investigation in which the committee interviewed witnesses, reviewed evidence and weighed Gomez's account against the staffer's. The committee's conclusion, released on Monday, was that the staffer was credible, that Gomez's conduct was unwelcome, and that it violated the rules of the House. The committee did not recommend any particular sanction for Gomez, who is serving his fifth term — a decision that reflects the fact that he was already leaving Congress, and that he announced his resignation within the hour. There was, in the end, nothing left to sanction. The voters had already removed him. The report simply explained why his departure would be remembered as a disgrace rather than a retirement.

The timing of the report's release was also significant. It came as part of a tranche of investigative findings released on Monday, which also included reports involving Representative Troy Nehls, a Republican from Texas, and Representative Mike Collins, a Republican from Georgia, and Collins's former chief of staff, Brandon Phillips. Those reports examined allegations of campaign-finance violations and failures in congressional oversight — serious matters, but different in kind from the sexual misconduct found in the Gomez case. The decision to release them together was, in part, a demonstration that the Ethics Committee, evenly divided between the parties, investigates members of both parties. But it was the Gomez report that dominated the day, and it was the Gomez report that ended a career.

The election that ended it first

It is worth remembering that the Ethics Committee did not end Jimmy Gomez's career. The voters did — weeks before the report was released. Earlier in November, Gomez lost his re-election bid to Angela Gonzales-Torres, a fellow Democrat, by nearly 25 points, a margin that in California's top-two primary system, where the top two vote-getters advance to the general election regardless of party, represented a comprehensive rejection. Gomez was not set to return to Congress in January. His resignation on December 1 brings his departure forward by a single month. The report, in that sense, did not change the outcome. It changed the meaning of it.

Gomez had served in Congress since 2017, when he won a special election to succeed Xavier Becerra, who had been appointed California's attorney general after Kamala Harris was elected to the Senate. He represented California's 34th congressional district, covering parts of Los Angeles, and he had built a career as a reliable progressive Democrat, a member of the Congressional Hispanic Caucus and a vocal advocate for immigrant rights — a cause that was personal to him, as the son of immigrants himself. He was, by most accounts, a diligent and well-liked member. None of that survived Monday. A decade of service was, in a single afternoon, reduced to a single word in the headlines: misconduct.

The scale of his election defeat is itself a question. Did the voters of the 34th district know, or sense, what the Ethics Committee would later confirm? The investigation was public knowledge — it had been announced in August — and the allegations had been reported in the press. It is possible that voters, weighing a congressman under an ethics cloud against a fresh challenger, chose the challenger. It is also possible that the defeat had other causes: a restless electorate, a strong challenger, a district that wanted change. What is certain is that the defeat and the report, taken together, leave no room for a comeback. Gomez is leaving Congress on December 1, and he is leaving in disgrace.

The third resignation of the year

Gomez is the third member of Congress to resign this year in the face of sexual misconduct allegations, according to CNN — a statistic that says something troubling about the institution, and about the moment. The House of Representatives has, for decades, struggled with how to deal with the sexual misconduct of its own members. The power imbalance between a member of Congress and a young staffer is extreme: the member controls hiring, firing, references and careers, and the staffer, often in her twenties and at the start of her professional life, has little recourse. The Ethics Committee exists, in part, to police that imbalance. But its processes are slow, its proceedings are often secret, and its sanctions have historically been mild. The fact that three members have resigned this year suggests that something is changing — that the committee is acting more aggressively, that staffers are more willing to come forward, and that the political cost of staying has become too high.

The Gomez report itself gestures at that change. Its observation that his conduct "reflects a broader environment of sexual misconduct and toxic working conditions in some Capitol Hill offices which the Committee is determined to address" is unusually blunt for an ethics report, and it suggests that the committee sees the Gomez case not as an aberration but as a symptom. The woman at the centre of the report, according to the committee, "repeatedly referenced a toxic and predatory culture" on Capitol Hill — a phrase that, if true, indicts far more than one congressman. It indicts a workplace in which young staffers are expected to tolerate behaviour that would be unacceptable anywhere else, and in which the powerful are protected by the very institution that is supposed to hold them accountable.

Whether the committee follows through on that determination remains to be seen. Ethics investigations are slow, and the political incentives to look away are strong. But the Gomez case, and the two other resignations this year, suggest that the cost of looking away is rising. Staffers are speaking. Lawyers are listening. And committees, even bipartisan ones, are finding the evidence.

What happens to the seat

Gomez's resignation on December 1 leaves California's 34th congressional district without a representative for the final weeks of the current Congress — a gap that matters little in practice, since the new Congress, including his successor Angela Gonzales-Torres, takes office in January. The seat does not change hands in any meaningful sense: Gonzales-Torres won it in November, and she will hold it from January. The resignation is, in the end, a matter of timing and of symbolism. Gomez will spend his final weeks in office closing out constituent casework, as he put it, rather than legislating. His staff will wind down the office. And on December 1, his name will be removed from the roll of the House.

There is a larger context that makes the timing of this resignation particularly striking. Earlier in November, the Democrats won a large majority in the House of Representatives, taking 258 seats — their largest majority since 1992, according to media reports — and a North Carolina victory gave them their biggest House majority since the early 1990s. Gomez's resignation does not affect that majority: he was already leaving, and his successor is also a Democrat. But it is a reminder that the new majority, which takes office in January, will arrive with its own scandals, its own ethics cases and its own questions about how it polices its members. The Democrats won the House on a promise of change. The Gomez case will be one of the first tests of whether that change extends to their own ranks.

It is also a reminder of the human cost of these cases. The staffer at the centre of the report suffered, in the committee's words, "significant and ongoing distress" and left House employment altogether — a career, derailed before it had properly begun, by a man who held power over her. Her lawyers say she testified at personal risk. She did so, and the committee believed her. That, in the end, is the most important sentence in the report: the committee found her credible. For years, staffers who came forward were ignored, disbelieved or punished. This time, the institution believed the staffer, and the congressman resigned within the hour.

The culture the report describes

The most important part of the Ethics Committee's report may not be its findings about Jimmy Gomez. It may be its description of the culture in which he operated. The committee said Gomez's conduct "reflects a broader environment of sexual misconduct and toxic working conditions in some Capitol Hill offices." Witness 1, according to the report, "repeatedly referenced a toxic and predatory culture" on Capitol Hill. These are not the words of a committee describing an isolated incident. They are the words of a committee describing a workplace problem — a problem that has persisted for decades, through multiple scandals, multiple reforms and multiple promises that things would change.

Capitol Hill is, in many ways, a uniquely vulnerable workplace. The staffers are young, often in their twenties, often far from home, working long hours for low pay in the hope of building a career in public service. The members are powerful, often wealthy, often accustomed to deference, and protected by an institution that has historically been reluctant to discipline its own. The imbalance is not incidental. It is structural. And it creates precisely the conditions in which misconduct flourishes: a powerful person, a vulnerable subordinate, and an institution that looks away.

The reforms that followed earlier scandals — mandatory training, new reporting procedures, changes to the way complaints are handled — were supposed to fix this. The Gomez case suggests they have not, or not enough. The committee's determination to address the "broader environment" is welcome, but it is also a promise that has been made before. Whether it is kept will depend on what the committee does next: whether it investigates other offices, whether it publishes its findings, and whether it is willing to hold powerful members accountable even when doing so is politically inconvenient. The Gomez case is a test of that willingness, and the committee passed it. The next test will come soon enough.

What comes next

Jimmy Gomez will leave Congress on December 1, 2026, and his departure will be noted, briefly, and then forgotten by most of Washington. Angela Gonzales-Torres will take the seat in January. The Ethics Committee will move on to its next case. But the report will remain — a public record of what the committee found, and of what it said about the culture of Capitol Hill. And the staffer at its centre will live with the consequences for far longer than Gomez will. She left House employment. She testified at personal risk. She was believed. That is, in the end, the measure of whether the system worked: not that a congressman resigned, but that a young woman who said she was harmed was heard, and believed, by the institution that employed them both.

There are questions that remain unanswered. Will prosecutors in Washington, DC, act on the committee's finding that Gomez's conduct could constitute criminal misdemeanor sexual abuse? The committee's report is not a criminal referral, and no charges have been announced. Will the Ethics Committee follow through on its promise to address the broader culture of misconduct on Capitol Hill? Its record on that question is mixed. And will the House, under its new Democratic majority, do more to protect the staffers who work in its offices? That will be decided in January, when the new Congress takes office, and when the promises made on the campaign trail meet the realities of governing.

For now, the facts are these: a congressman was found by a bipartisan committee to have engaged in sexual misconduct toward a staffer 20 years his junior. He admitted to the contact, insisted it was consensual, and was not believed. He resigned within an hour of the report's release, effective December 1. He was the third member to resign this year over sexual misconduct. And the committee that investigated him said his conduct reflected a broader environment of misconduct and toxic working conditions that it is determined to address. That determination, if it is real, will be the most important legacy of the Gomez case. If it is not, then the case will be remembered as it began: as a party, a kiss, a staffer who said no, and a congressman who would not listen.
