WWE star Bronson Reed is dead at 38. The company announced on Friday, 27 November 2026, that Jermaine Haley — the Australian wrestler known to millions of fans as Bronson Reed — had died earlier that day due to "medical complications". He was 38 years old. He leaves behind his wife, Paige, who is pregnant, and their daughter, Deja.

"WWE is devastated to announce that Jermaine Haley, also known as Bronson Reed, passed away earlier today at the age of 38 due to medical complications," the company said in a statement posted on X and Instagram, just before a taped episode of SmackDown went on air. "WWE remembers the legacy of Jermaine Haley, also known as Bronson Reed. Our thoughts and deepest condolences are with Jermaine's entire family, including his pregnant wife, Paige, their daughter, as well as his friends, co-workers and fans."

The news came just three weeks after Reed was seriously injured at WWE's Crown Jewel event in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, on 7 November, when he was thrown off a stage and through tables. He suffered broken ribs and a lacerated kidney, according to reports at the time, and had to stay in Saudi Arabia for treatment before returning home to continue his recovery. WWE has not said whether his death is connected to that injury. It has given no cause beyond "medical complications", and it has not said where he died. That is all the company has confirmed — and the silence around the details has left the wrestling world, and his fans, searching for answers.

Who was Bronson Reed?

Jermaine Haley was born on 25 August 1988 in Adelaide, South Australia, into a family of Samoan descent. He grew up to become one of the most physically imposing wrestlers of his generation: a super-heavyweight who moved with a speed and agility that belied his size, and whose finishing move — the Tsunami, a devastating splash from the top rope — became one of the most feared weapons in WWE.

He was best known for his two stints in WWE, from 2019 to 2021 and then from 2022 to 2026. Before that, he had wrestled on the independent scene and in Japan under the name Jonah Rock, and as Jonah — names that older fans still remember, and that tell the story of a wrestler who built his reputation the hard way, in small halls and foreign rings, before the biggest company in the business came calling.

In his second WWE run, Reed became a central figure in one of the company's most prominent stables, The Vision, alongside Bron Breakker and Austin Theory. The group was positioned as the future of the company: young, powerful, and ruthless. Reed's role in it was the enforcer — the man who could end a match, and a feud, with a single Tsunami. He was, by every account, at the peak of his career: a featured performer on WWE's biggest shows, a regular on premium live events, and a wrestler whose best years were supposed to be ahead of him.

His final weeks in the ring tell their own story. His last match was on 2 November, a six-man tag team match at the Barclays Center in Brooklyn. Five days later, he was in Riyadh for Crown Jewel, where he was not booked to wrestle but got involved in a World Tag Team Championship match featuring his Vision stablemates against Je'Von Evans and Penta. It was during that run-in that he was injured — thrown off the stage by Oba Femi and sent crashing through tables below. The images of the fall were shown around the world. Nobody watching them could have imagined that, twenty days later, he would be dead.

The injury at Crown Jewel

The Crown Jewel event on 7 November in Riyadh was one of WWE's biggest shows of the year, staged in Saudi Arabia as part of the company's long-running partnership with the kingdom. Reed was not scheduled to wrestle that night. His involvement came as a run-in — a surprise appearance during the World Tag Team Championship match, designed to help his Vision stablemates Bron Breakker and Austin Theory against Je'Von Evans and Penta.

What happened next was reported in detail by the wrestling press in the days that followed. According to Bryan Alvarez of Wrestling Observer Live, Reed suffered broken ribs and a lacerated kidney when Oba Femi threw him off the stage and through tables. Fightful Select reported the same injuries: rib and kidney damage, sustained in the fall. The injury was serious enough that Reed had to stay in Saudi Arabia for treatment after the show, rather than flying home with the rest of the roster.

He did eventually return home to continue his recovery, according to Alvarez, and the expectation in the wrestling world was that he would be out of action for weeks, perhaps months — but that he would come back. A lacerated kidney is a serious injury, and broken ribs are painful and slow to heal, but both are injuries that wrestlers have recovered from before. There was no public suggestion, in the three weeks between Crown Jewel and his death, that his life was in danger.

That is what makes the announcement of 27 November so shocking. WWE has not said whether the death is connected to the Crown Jewel injury, and it would be wrong to assume a connection that the company has not confirmed. But the timing — twenty days between the injury and the death, in a 38-year-old athlete at the peak of his physical powers — is the question that everyone in wrestling is now asking, and that WWE has so far not answered.

What WWE has said — and what it has not

The statement WWE released on Friday is short, and its brevity is deliberate. "WWE is devastated to announce that Jermaine Haley, also known as Bronson Reed, passed away earlier today at the age of 38 due to medical complications." That is the whole of it, as far as the facts go. No cause. No location. No mention of Crown Jewel. No mention of the injury. The company has not said whether the two are connected, and the wrestling press has been careful to report the same: that WWE has not said whether the death is connected to the injury, and that the connection, if there is one, is unconfirmed.

That caution is appropriate, and it is worth explaining why. A lacerated kidney is a serious injury, but it is not usually a fatal one, and broken ribs, while painful, are among the most common injuries in professional wrestling. For a death to follow three weeks later, in a man of 38 who had returned home and was recovering, raises medical questions that only a proper examination can answer — and those answers, if they come, will come from doctors and from the family, not from a wrestling company's press release.

What is known is the timeline, and the timeline is what makes the story so hard to absorb. On 2 November, Reed wrestled his final match at the Barclays Center in Brooklyn, in front of a sold-out crowd. On 7 November, he was thrown off a stage in Riyadh and through tables, suffering broken ribs and a lacerated kidney. He stayed in Saudi Arabia for treatment. He returned home. And on 27 November, twenty days after the injury, he was dead. Every fact in that sequence is confirmed. What connects them — if anything connects them — is not.

The wrestling world has learned, over decades of premature deaths, to wait for the facts. The industry has lost too many performers too young, and the speculation that follows a death can cause as much pain as the death itself. WWE's statement, for all its brevity, does the right thing: it names the man, it gives his age, it offers condolences to his family, and it does not pretend to know more than it does. The rest — the cause, the circumstances, the question of whether Crown Jewel played a part — will have to wait for the family and the medical authorities to speak.

The tributes begin

The news broke less than an hour before WWE was due to begin a live event at the Akins Ford Arena in Athens, Georgia, on Friday evening. The timing meant that the wrestlers on that show learned of their colleague's death almost as the audience did — and the tributes began almost immediately. Kevin Owens, one of WWE's most respected veterans, gave an emotional speech at the house show about Reed, telling the crowd that Reed loved doing what he did. "He loved doing this," Owens said, in a moment that was captured and shared across social media within minutes.

It was the first of what will be many tributes. WWE will hold its Survivor Series premium live event on Saturday, 28 November — the first televised event since Reed's death — and tributes are expected to continue there, in front of a global audience. The company has a long tradition of honouring its fallen performers on air, and Survivor Series, one of the biggest nights of the wrestling year, will be the stage on which the industry says its formal goodbye.

The reaction online has been overwhelming. Fans have shared clips of Reed's greatest moments: the Tsunami splashes from the top rope, the wars with the company's biggest stars, the moments when a man of his size did things that men of his size are not supposed to be able to do. Fellow wrestlers have posted their condolences. The wrestling press has run obituaries. And in Adelaide, the city where he was born, and in the Samoan community he came from, the news has been received with a grief that goes beyond wrestling — the loss of a son of the city, and a son of his people, at 38.

A family left behind

Behind the headlines and the tributes, there is a family. Reed is survived by his wife, Paige, who is pregnant, and by their daughter, Deja. The photograph that has circulated most widely since the announcement shows Reed with his family — a reminder that the man the fans knew as Bronson Reed, the enforcer of The Vision, the human wrecking ball who flew off the top rope, was at home a husband and a father, with a child on the way.

WWE's statement named them specifically: "including his pregnant wife, Paige, their daughter, as well as his friends, co-workers and fans." It is a small detail, but it matters, because it is the part of the story that will last longest. The wrestling world will move on — Survivor Series will happen on Saturday, the shows will go on, new stars will take Reed's place. But for Paige and Deja, and for the child who will be born without ever meeting its father, the loss is permanent, and it is not a storyline.

The company has not announced any fund or support for the family, and it would be wrong to speculate about what it may do. What is certain is that the wrestling community, which has a long history of rallying around the families of fallen performers, will not leave them alone. The tributes that began in Athens on Friday night are only the beginning of that.

Why this death hits so hard

Professional wrestling is a business built on the illusion of invulnerability. The performers are presented as larger than life — superhuman athletes who can be thrown off stages, driven through tables, and hit with steel chairs, and get up and do it again the next night. Bronson Reed embodied that illusion more than most: a man of enormous size who flew through the air like a cruiserweight, whose finishing move was to launch himself from the top rope onto a prone opponent, and who seemed, on screen, indestructible.

His death at 38 shatters that illusion in the most painful way possible. It is a reminder that the men and women who perform these feats are not indestructible, that the falls are real, that the tables are real, and that the injuries — broken ribs, a lacerated kidney — are real injuries with real consequences. The audience sees the spectacle. The performer lives with the damage. And sometimes, the damage is more than the body can bear.

It is also a reminder of how young 38 is. Reed was born in 1988, the same year as many of the fans who watched him. He had been wrestling for more than a decade, and he had finally reached the top of his profession — a featured performer in WWE, a member of its most prominent stable, a man whose best years were supposed to be ahead of him. He had a wife, a daughter, and a child on the way. He had everything to live for, and twenty days after a fall in Riyadh, he was gone.

The questions will continue. Was the death connected to the Crown Jewel injury? What exactly were the "medical complications" that WWE cited? Where did he die, and who was with him? Those questions deserve answers, and they will be answered in time — by the family, by the doctors, and by the authorities, not by speculation. For now, what is certain is this: Jermaine Haley, known to the world as Bronson Reed, died on 27 November 2026, at the age of 38, three weeks after the worst injury of his career. The wrestling world has lost one of its most spectacular performers. A family has lost a husband and a father. And a child will be born who will only ever know its father through photographs and videotape.

From Adelaide to the top of WWE

Reed's journey to the top of professional wrestling was a long one, and it is worth telling in full, because it explains why his death has hit the industry so hard. He was born in Adelaide, in South Australia, into a family of Samoan descent — a heritage he carried proudly throughout his career, and one that placed him in a long line of Samoan wrestlers who have shaped the business, from the Wild Samoans of the 1980s to the stars of today. He did not arrive in WWE as a finished product. He built his name the hard way, wrestling on the independent scene and in Japan under the names Jonah Rock and Jonah, earning a reputation as a big man who could move, and a worker who could be trusted with the biggest spots on the card.

That reputation is what brought him to WWE, first in 2019, and then again in 2022 after a year away. The second stint was the one that made him a star. He was not a nostalgia act, and he was not a novelty. He was a featured performer in the prime of his career, trusted with a central role in The Vision, the stable that WWE positioned as the future of the company, alongside Bron Breakker and Austin Theory. His finishing move, the Tsunami — a splash from the top rope, delivered by a man of his size — was one of the most spectacular sights in the sport, and one of the most dangerous. Every time he climbed to the top rope, the crowd rose with him. Every time he came down, the building shook.

As recently as 10 October 2026, he was making his way to the ring at Money in the Bank in New Orleans, Louisiana, in front of a huge crowd, doing the job he loved. Seven weeks later, he is gone. That is the speed with which a career, and a life, can end in this business — and it is why the tributes pouring in from his colleagues carry a note of shock as well as grief. They saw him in October, healthy and at the top of his game. They saw him injured in November, and they expected him back. They did not expect to be mourning him before the month was out.

What happens next

Survivor Series takes place on Saturday, 28 November, and it will be the first televised WWE event since Reed's death. Expect tributes — a video package, a moment of silence, perhaps a mention from the commentators — and expect them to be heartfelt, because the people paying them knew him. That is how his colleagues will remember him — not for the injury, and not for the unanswered questions, but for a man who loved the business he gave his life to, in the most literal sense of the words.

Beyond Survivor Series, the questions will remain. WWE will face them, as it has faced them before: about the safety of its performers, about the risks of the stunts it stages, about the duty of care it owes to the men and women who put their bodies on the line for its entertainment. Those are legitimate questions, and they deserve serious answers. But they are questions for another day. Today, the wrestling world is grieving — for a 38-year-old father, husband and performer who went to work in Riyadh three weeks ago and never came home the same man.
