Thanksgiving Day 2026 will not be remembered in America for turkey and football. It will be remembered for the dark. A powerful coastal storm — a classic nor'easter — slammed into the East Coast on Thursday, November 26, knocking out power to nearly 1 million homes and businesses, flooding streets from North Carolina to New York, and pushing ocean tides to heights never recorded before. By 7:30 p.m. Eastern Time, more than 961,000 utility customers had no electricity, according to PowerOutage.us, and the number was still climbing as the storm ground on into the night.

The storm struck on the busiest travel day of the American year, when tens of millions of people were on the move to reach family tables. It turned highways into rivers, closed major bridges, cancelled hundreds of flights, and forced the postponement of the 100th Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade — the first time in the parade's century-long history that weather has pushed it off Thanksgiving Day itself. In New Jersey, where the flooding was worst, officials declared a state of emergency before the first drop of rain had even fallen. By nightfall, some shore towns were reporting the worst flooding in their history.

What made this storm so destructive was not one single ingredient but the combination. Heavy rain fell on ground already soaked by days of wet weather. Winds gusted to 70 miles per hour, strong enough to topple trees and snap power lines. And the storm arrived on top of king tides — the highest tides of the month — so the sea was already running high when the onshore winds began piling water against the coast. The National Weather Service warned of moderate to major coastal flooding through Thursday and Friday tide cycles, and the water delivered exactly that.

Nearly 1 Million in the Dark — State by State

The outage numbers told the story of a storm that hit almost everywhere at once. As of 7:30 p.m. ET on Thursday, PowerOutage.us counted more than 961,000 customers without power across the United States. Pennsylvania was the hardest hit, with more than 235,000 customers in the dark. New Jersey followed with over 180,000 outages, Maryland with more than 156,000, and Virginia with 132,000. Missouri, far from the coast, still had nearly 106,000 outages from the same sprawling system, while New York state counted more than 56,000 and Delaware over 21,000.

Earlier in the day, the national total had already passed 1 million, according to aggregated utility data reported during the morning, before some customers were restored and others lost power in turn. By 6 p.m., a separate count put the figure at more than 743,000 customers across seven East Coast states alone — more than 226,000 in Pennsylvania and over 170,000 in Maryland. Dominion Energy reported nearly 123,000 customers without power in Virginia and North Carolina, including more than 20,000 in Fairfax County, near Washington. Pepco, which serves Maryland and Washington, reported more than 22,000 customers out, while Baltimore Gas and Electric reported over 102,000.

Utilities warned that the outages could stretch into the weekend. Rain-soaked ground is the enemy of power lines in a windstorm: wet soil loosens the grip of tree roots, and a 70 mph gust can then bring a whole tree down across a line. Crews cannot safely work in the worst of the wind, so repairs wait until the storm passes — and with hundreds of thousands of customers out across a dozen states, the queue for restoration is long. For families who had planned a Thanksgiving dinner, the timing could hardly have been worse: ovens went cold, refrigerators warmed, and in many homes the holiday meal was cooked by candlelight or not at all.

The power failures also knocked on to water supplies. In Maryland, intermittent power disruptions at filtration plants prompted authorities in Montgomery County and Prince George's County to ask nearly 2 million water customers to conserve water, easing pressure on treatment and pumping facilities that were running on backup or intermittent power. It was a reminder that in a modern storm, the failure of one system — electricity — cascades quickly into others.

Record Tides: 10.3 Feet of Water Where It Had Never Been

If the power cuts were the storm's broadest blow, the tides were its most extraordinary. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's preliminary data showed water levels breaking records that had stood for decades. At Ship John Shoal in New Jersey, the water rose 10.3 feet — a record. At Cape May, the tide reached 9.4 feet, also a record. In Wachapreague, Virginia, a six-foot rise in water levels broke a record that had stood since 1998 by more than 2.1 feet.

These were not abstract numbers on a gauge. They were streets under water, cars floating, and homes taking on the sea. The National Weather Service said strong onshore winds, combined with king tides, were causing coastal flooding in low-lying communities along portions of the East Coast, with significant inundation reported in Virginia, the Delaware Bay, parts of the Chesapeake Bay and the southern New Jersey coast. Coastal flood warnings and advisories covered shoreline communities from North Carolina to New York, and forecasters warned that peak high tides on Thursday evening could inundate low-lying roads, parking areas and basements in waterfront neighbourhoods.

King tides are the highest tides of the year, occurring when the moon is closest to Earth and the sun, moon and Earth align. On their own they cause nuisance flooding in low-lying coastal towns. Add a nor'easter's onshore winds, which physically push seawater towards the shore and pile it up against the coast, and the result is water levels far beyond anything the tide tables predict. That is what happened on Thanksgiving Day: the astronomical tide was already high, and the storm surge rode on top of it.

New Jersey Underwater: "The Worst Flooding in Our History"

Nowhere was hit harder than New Jersey. The state was under a state of emergency, along with parts of New York, and the Shore bore the full force of the storm. Avalon, a borough on the Jersey Shore, was seeing some of the worst flooding in its history, according to Borough Business Administrator Scott J. Wahl. Floodwaters washed debris over roads, and a few drivers were rescued after driving around barricades into floodwaters. No injuries were reported in Avalon, Wahl said, but the scale of the water was unlike anything the town had seen.

In Ocean City, New Jersey, resident Lance Miller, 54, said floodwaters in his neighbourhood were about a foot deep, and that driving to the nearest supermarket would probably "destroy your car". In Margate City, near Atlantic City, the fire department posted video on Facebook of water pouring through the city's streets, even before the heavy rains had started — the sea was arriving on the tide before the rain added its own weight. Heavy rains flooded coastal areas up and down the Shore, while utilities warned that rain-soaked ground and strong winds could increase the risk of falling trees and further outages.

New York was not spared. Governor Kathy Hochul issued a state of emergency for New York City, Long Island and Westchester County. For much of Thursday afternoon, heavy rain fell on New York City, and local officials responded to street flooding on some major roads. New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani warned that the rain could lead to flash flooding. The city's emergency management had been bracing for wind gusts strong enough to threaten the Macy's parade balloons — which is why the parade was postponed in the first place.

The Parade That Never Marched — A First in 100 Years

The storm's most symbolic casualty was the 100th Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade. On Wednesday, November 25, organisers announced that the parade would be postponed to Saturday, November 28 — the first postponement in the event's century-long history. The parade had marched through rain, snow and cold for 100 years, but the combination of 60 to 70 mph gusts and driving rain made it unsafe to fly the giant balloons down the streets of Manhattan. Workers were photographed deflating a balloon after the postponement was announced, a sad sight for an event that had been planned for a year.

The decision left an enormous question hanging over the weekend: what happens to the parade's expansive roster of performers, marching bands, dancers and Broadway casts, many of whom had travelled from across the country and had other commitments on Saturday? The parade's organisers faced the task of restaging a spectacle built for Thanksgiving Day on a different day entirely, with many performers already booked to be elsewhere. For New York, the postponement was a piece of history nobody wanted — the centenary parade, the biggest in the event's history, undone by weather on the very day it was meant to celebrate.

The storm had already been threatening the parade for days. Forecasters had warned that the nor'easter would bring prolonged heavy rain, damaging wind gusts and coastal flooding, and that the worst impacts would concentrate from the mid-Atlantic into New England. New Jersey and New York had declared states of emergency on Wednesday in anticipation of what forecasters described as multiple rounds of moderate to major coastal flooding, particularly during Thursday's and Friday's tide cycles. The parade decision was the most visible consequence, but it was only one of thousands.

Travel Chaos: 440 Flights Cancelled, Bridges Closed

Thanksgiving is the busiest travel period in the United States, and the storm hit it head-on. FlightAware reported a total of 440 US-related cancellations on Thursday and more than 2,200 delayed flights, as the storm snarled air travel along the East Coast and into the Midwest. The Transportation Security Administration had said it expected to screen nearly 17.6 million passengers nationwide between Sunday, November 24, and Saturday, November 30, with more than 3 million travellers forecast on Sunday, November 29, alone. For hundreds of thousands of them, the journey home — or to the dinner table — became an ordeal of delays, cancellations and rebooked flights.

The storm also disrupted travel on the ground. In Virginia and the wider Chesapeake region, high winds forced the temporary closure of the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel on Thursday morning after wind speeds exceeded safety thresholds. The Chesapeake Bay Bridge in Maryland was closed for several hours on Thursday afternoon. The Commodore Barry Bridge, connecting New Jersey and Pennsylvania, was expected to remain closed at least until Saturday after maintenance equipment was damaged by the wind. On the roads, intense rainfall led to ponding on highways and flash flooding in poor-drainage locations, and in the Midwest the same sprawling system brought winter storm warnings, heavy wet snow and hazardous driving across parts of Missouri and neighbouring states.

Rail and road journeys along key Midwest and East Coast corridors were disrupted as well. The storm system stretched from the central United States to the Atlantic seaboard, combining winter storm conditions in parts of the Midwest with the strong coastal storm along the shoreline. National Weather Service offices reported winter storm warnings across parts of Missouri, where snow and mixed precipitation made road travel hazardous and reduced visibility on interstate highways. It was, in every sense, a storm that covered the map.

What Is a Nor'easter — and Why This One Was So Bad

A nor'easter is a storm that forms along the East Coast of North America and takes its name from the direction of its strongest winds — from the northeast. Warm, moist air from the Atlantic meets cold air spilling down from Canada, and the clash spins up a powerful low-pressure system that tracks up the coastline, dragging wind, rain and snow with it. The classic nor'easter brings heavy precipitation, gale-force winds and coastal flooding, and the worst of them — the famous blizzards and the 1991 "Perfect Storm" — are among the most destructive weather events in American history.

This storm had all the ingredients. A strong coastal low tracked up the Eastern Seaboard on Thanksgiving Day, concentrating its worst impacts from the mid-Atlantic into New England. Forecast discussions issued early on Thursday highlighted the risk of heavy rain, damaging wind gusts and coastal flooding that would continue into Friday in some areas, especially around high tides. Wind gusts of 50 to 70 mph were reported or forecast across parts of Virginia, Maryland, Delaware, New Jersey, New York and coastal New England, according to the National Weather Service. And because the storm arrived on king tides, the sea was already at its highest when the winds began pushing it ashore.

Climate scientists have long warned that coastal flooding is becoming more frequent and more severe as sea levels rise. Higher baseline sea levels mean that a storm surge of a given size now reaches further inland than it would have a generation ago, and king tides that once caused only minor nuisance flooding now close roads and flood homes. The Thanksgiving storm of 2026 was a textbook illustration: the surge rode on top of an already-elevated sea, and records fell in three states in a single day.

The West Coast Was Flooding Too

Remarkably, the East Coast was not the only American coastline in trouble on Thanksgiving. On the other side of the continent, king tides and strong waves were battering California, where part of the Pacifica Municipal Pier — a 1,140-foot structure built in 1973 — had already collapsed earlier in the week under the pounding surf. Waves of 20 to 30 feet were reported along the California coast, and Charleston in Oregon recorded water 1.12 metres above mean high water, while San Francisco saw November tides more than 8 feet high.

The coincidence was not a coincidence at all: the same king tides that lifted the Atlantic also lifted the Pacific. The moon does not respect coasts. What it meant was that on a single holiday, both ends of the United States were fighting the sea at once — a reminder that coastal America, east and west, faces the same rising water, and that the infrastructure built in a calmer era is now being tested by tides it was never designed for.

What Happens Next

Conditions were expected to improve gradually across many inland locations on Friday, November 27, as the storm system moved away from the coast. But forecasters warned that pockets of lingering flooding, debris on roads and slick surfaces would continue to affect travel, particularly during the morning hours in parts of the mid-Atlantic and Northeast. In coastal communities, the Friday high tides could bring another round of flooding before the water finally receded.

For the hundreds of thousands still without power, the wait for restoration would stretch into the weekend. Utilities said crews would work around the clock once winds dropped to safe levels, but with damage spread across a dozen states, full restoration would take days in the worst-hit areas. Families were advised to stay away from downed power lines, to never use generators indoors, and to check on elderly neighbours who might be without heat as temperatures dropped behind the storm.

The storm also left a question for the weekend: the postponed Macy's parade, rescheduled for Saturday, would depend on the weather clearing in time — and on the performers, bands and balloon crews being able to reassemble at 24 hours' notice. And for the millions who travelled for Thanksgiving, the journey home on Sunday — forecast to be the busiest travel day of the period, with more than 3 million passengers expected through airport security — would be the storm's final test. If the skies cleared, the holiday would end as it began for so many: in motion, tired, and grateful to be home. If not, the Thanksgiving storm of 2026 would have one more chapter to write.

The People Who Kept Going

Behind the outage numbers were the people who worked through the holiday to put things right. Utility crews from neighbouring states were already on the road before the storm peaked, driving through the rain to reinforce local teams. Fire departments in shore towns spent the night pulling drivers from flooded cars. In Maryland, water utility workers kept treatment plants running on backup power so that taps did not run dry for two million people. And in homes across the East Coast, neighbours shared generators, extension cords and hot food with the family next door whose stove had gone cold. Thanksgiving is, after all, a holiday about gratitude — and in 2026, a great many Americans found themselves grateful for a neighbour with a working oven, a flashlight, and a spare phone charger.

Why This Storm Matters

The Thanksgiving nor'easter of 2026 was not the deadliest storm in American history, and it was not the costliest. No deaths were reported in the first day of coverage. But it was one of the most disruptive: it struck on the single day of the year when the most Americans are travelling, cooking and gathering, and it hit them in the dark. Nearly 1 million people lost power. Record tides rewrote the record books in three states. A century-old parade was postponed for the first time. Bridges closed, flights were cancelled, and two million people were told to save water because the pumps had no power.

It was also a preview. As sea levels rise and storms draw more moisture from warmer oceans, the combination that made this storm so destructive — heavy rain, high winds and high tides arriving together — will become more common, not less. The towns of the Jersey Shore rebuilt after Hurricane Sandy in 2012; they are rebuilding their defences again now, and they will be asked to rebuild them again. The question the Thanksgiving storm leaves behind is not whether the sea will come again, but whether the coast will be ready when it does.

For now, the clean-up begins: the trees are cleared, the lines are restrung, the streets are pumped dry, and the balloons — deflated on Wednesday — wait for Saturday. Thanksgiving 2026 will be remembered as the year the lights went out. The hope, for the million Americans who spent it in the dark, is that next year the only thing that knocks out the power is somebody tripping over the extension cord.
