The hackers got inside the agency that is supposed to catch the hackers' money. The US Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network — FinCEN — disclosed on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, that a cybersecurity incident had resulted in unauthorized access to a limited subset of its systems, and that it had taken some of those systems offline as a proactive measure. The agency that collects the suspicious-activity reports banks file when they suspect money laundering, terrorist financing or fraud had itself become the victim of an intrusion.

The disclosure, made through an alert posted on FinCEN's Bank Secrecy Act E-Filing System website, was brief and carefully worded. "FinCEN is investigating a cybersecurity incident that resulted in unauthorized access to a limited subset of its systems," the alert said. "As a proactive measure, FinCEN has taken some systems offline. The BSA E-Filing website remains operational; however, some services may be temporarily unavailable." The agency added that it was extending deadlines for financial institutions affected by the disruption, and that it was working diligently to return all systems to normal operations as soon as possible.

What FinCEN did not say was almost as significant as what it did. It did not say which systems the intruders reached. It did not say who was responsible, or whether any information was viewed, copied or stolen. It did not say how the intruders got in, or how long they had been inside before they were detected. And it did not say when the systems that had been taken offline would be restored. A FinCEN official, speaking to Reuters on Wednesday, confirmed that the Treasury was working with the Federal Bureau of Investigation, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) and third-party forensic examiners to determine the scope and impact of the incident — the standard formula used when an investigation is in its early stages and the answers are not yet known.

One thing the agency was able to say, and said repeatedly, was that there was no evidence of any breach of the Bank Secrecy Act database or the beneficial ownership database — the two repositories that hold the most sensitive information FinCEN collects. That assurance, repeated by the FinCEN official to Reuters and to The Register, is the single most important fact in the story so far. If it holds, the incident is an operational disruption rather than a catastrophic theft of financial intelligence. If it does not hold — if forensic examiners find that the intruders did reach those databases — the consequences would be far more serious.

What FinCEN does — and why it matters

To understand why this breach matters, it helps to understand what FinCEN is. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network is a bureau of the US Treasury Department, and it sits at the centre of America's system for detecting and preventing financial crime. Under the Bank Secrecy Act of 1970, banks and other financial institutions are required to keep records of certain transactions and to file reports with the government when they detect activity that may indicate money laundering, terrorist financing, tax evasion, fraud or other illicit conduct.

Those reports — millions of them every year — flow into FinCEN. The agency collects them, analyses them, and shares the resulting financial intelligence with law enforcement agencies across the United States and with partner governments around the world. When the FBI investigates a drug cartel, when the Justice Department prosecutes a sanctions evader, when the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control freezes the assets of a terrorist network, the trail very often begins with a report filed through FinCEN's systems.

FinCEN also administers the beneficial ownership reporting regime, under which millions of American companies are required to disclose who really owns and controls them. That database, built up over the past two years, is one of the most sensitive collections of corporate information held by the US government. It is designed to make it harder for criminals, oligarchs and sanctions evaders to hide behind shell companies. The idea that hackers might have reached it — even the possibility — is what makes this incident different from an ordinary government website outage.

The agency's systems are also the plumbing of the American financial system. Banks file currency transaction reports, suspicious activity reports and other documents through the BSA E-Filing System, a secure online portal. When those systems go down, or become unreliable, the flow of financial intelligence is interrupted. Banks cannot file on time. Law enforcement cannot receive the reports it depends on. The system does not stop — the E-Filing website remained operational, and FinCEN emphasised that — but it slows, and it becomes less reliable, at precisely the moment when the people who move illicit money through the financial system are most likely to exploit any gap.

The deadline extension — and what it reveals

The clearest evidence of the disruption's practical impact came not from the alert itself but from the order that accompanied it. On November 24, FinCEN issued an order, identified as FIN-2026-R002, granting temporary, limited exceptive relief to filers who could not meet their reporting obligations because of the service disruption. The order extended the deadlines for certain filings that were otherwise due between November 18, 2026 and December 29, 2026, requiring those submissions to be filed no later than December 30, 2026. FinCEN said it could modify or extend the order at its discretion.

The relief applied to a specific set of filers: financial institutions that use the Secure Data Transfer Mode to file Bank Secrecy Act reports, companies required to file beneficial ownership information reports, and individuals who needed to update their FinCEN Identifier — the unique number the agency issues to people who file beneficial ownership reports. In other words, the disruption affected the channels through which some of the most important financial crime reports are submitted, and the agency's response was to give the filers more time.

The dates in the order are revealing. The relief window begins on November 18 — six days before the public disclosure on November 24. That suggests that the filing problems began before the incident was disclosed, and possibly before it was detected. The Register, which reported the story on November 25, noted that the clues in the deadline extension notice suggested the disruption affected FinCEN's reporting services for banks and other financial institutions required to submit reports detailing certain financial transactions and suspicious activity. The agency has not confirmed that the intrusion caused the filing problems that preceded the announcement, but the dates show how the problem can compound without proving its cause.

There were other signs of disruption. Attempts by journalists to visit the FinCEN Identifier website brought up the message "This site can't be reached." The Financial Institutions Hotline — the telephone number banks can use to expedite the delivery of suspicious activity information to law enforcement — was not affected, FinCEN said, and the agency urged any financial institution that needed to get information to law enforcement quickly to use the hotline on 1-866-556-3974. The fact that the agency had to direct filers to a telephone number is a measure of how far the disruption reached.

A wave of attacks on American agencies

The FinCEN incident did not happen in isolation. It came in the middle of a wave of cyberattacks on US government agencies that has rattled Washington over the past month, and it followed a series of breaches that have already been reported in detail.

Hours before the FinCEN disclosure, reports emerged of a breach of systems at the Central Intelligence Agency that also affected the Office of the Director of National Intelligence and the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, which collects and analyses location and imagery intelligence to support national security. That breach, reported in detail in this newspaper's earlier coverage, involved unauthorised access to unclassified systems and prompted a major investigation. The FBI's National Crime Information Center — the database that police officers across America query millions of times a day — was also hacked, in an incident that exposed some 19.3 million records and raised questions about the security of the country's most important law enforcement database.

The Financial Express, reporting on the pattern, listed the agencies affected in recent weeks: FinCEN, the CIA, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, the FBI and the US Department of Agriculture. The list is striking not only for its length but for its breadth. These are not obscure agencies with outdated systems. They are the core institutions of American national security and financial regulation, and they have been hit in rapid succession by intruders whose identity and motives remain, in most cases, unknown.

The pattern has fuelled speculation about whether the attacks are connected — whether a single actor, or a coordinated group of actors, is systematically probing American government networks — or whether they are separate incidents that happen to have occurred at the same time. The government has not said. What is clear is that the attacks have exposed a vulnerability that policymakers have warned about for years: that the US government's own networks, for all the money spent on defending them, remain penetrable, and that the consequences of a successful intrusion are growing as more of the government's work moves online.

The Treasury's other problem: five subdomains for three dollars each

On the same day that FinCEN disclosed its breach, security researchers published a separate finding that embarrassed the Treasury Department in a different way. Researchers from Intruder Systems reported that they had been able to take over five Treasury Department subdomains for three dollars each, by claiming the Amazon Web Services hosts that the subdomains continued to point to — a technique known as a dangling DNS takeover.

The researchers, led by Intruder's head of security Dan Andrew, built a target list by looking for dangling DNS records in public bug bounty reports. Over 48 hours, they ran through 450,000 unique IP addresses and found 28 likely takeovers involving government domains, including five Treasury subdomains. The technique is well known: when an organisation stops using a cloud server but forgets to delete the DNS record that points to it, an attacker can claim the abandoned address and use it to host phishing pages, steal credentials or launch deeper intrusions. The researchers reported the Treasury takeovers through a bug bounty programme, and the issue was fixed — but the demonstration that it was still possible in 2026, on the Treasury's own domains, on the same day that FinCEN disclosed a breach, was a damaging coincidence.

The two incidents are not the same, and there is no evidence that they are connected. The subdomain takeovers were a demonstration by security researchers, not an attack by a hostile actor, and they were reported and fixed. But they illustrate the same underlying problem: that the attack surface of the US government is vast, that it includes forgotten corners and abandoned infrastructure, and that the people responsible for defending it are engaged in a constant struggle to keep track of what they own. In that sense, the FinCEN breach and the subdomain takeovers are two symptoms of the same disease.

Who is behind it — and why no one will say yet

The question everyone in Washington is asking is who did it. The Treasury has not said. FinCEN has not said. The FBI and CISA, which are investigating alongside third-party forensic examiners, have not said. And the reporting so far has been careful not to speculate beyond what the evidence supports. The Register, which has covered the incident in detail, noted that FinCEN has not stated who may be involved or for what purpose, and that the investigation is ongoing.

That caution is appropriate, because the possibilities are wide. The intrusion could be the work of a nation-state intelligence service, seeking access to the financial intelligence that FinCEN holds — information about who moves money, where it goes, and which transactions the US government considers suspicious. That information would be extraordinarily valuable to a foreign government: it would reveal what American investigators know, which of its nationals or companies are under scrutiny, and how the US financial surveillance system works from the inside.

It could also be the work of a criminal group, seeking the same information for a different purpose. Organised crime networks, sanctions evaders and money launderers all have a direct interest in knowing what FinCEN knows about them, and in disrupting the system that reports on them. A criminal group that could see suspicious activity reports filed about its own transactions would know exactly how much the authorities knew, and could adjust its behaviour accordingly. A group that could disrupt the filing system itself would create a window — even a brief one — in which illicit transactions might go unreported.

Or it could be something less dramatic: an opportunistic intrusion, a test of the defences, a ransomware group probing for a foothold. The fact that the access was to a "limited subset" of systems, as FinCEN put it, suggests that the intruders did not have free rein over the network — or that they were detected before they could get further. Either way, the absence of attribution is itself a signal. In the early days of a major intrusion, the investigating agencies are usually reluctant to name a suspect until the forensic evidence is solid, because a wrong attribution is worse than no attribution at all.

The context of the other recent breaches adds to the speculation. The CIA and ODNI breach, the FBI database hack and now the FinCEN intrusion have all occurred within a short period, and early signs in the CIA case pointed to China — a claim that has not been confirmed and that Beijing would deny. Whether the FinCEN incident is part of the same campaign, a copycat, or an unrelated event will be one of the central questions of the investigation. For now, the only honest answer is that nobody outside the investigation knows.

What it means for banks, businesses and ordinary Americans

For most Americans, the FinCEN breach will have no direct or visible effect. They will not see their bank accounts compromised, their credit cards cancelled or their identities stolen — at least, not as a result of this incident. FinCEN does not hold consumer bank account details, credit card numbers or passwords. It holds reports about transactions, and the most sensitive of those reports are, according to the agency, untouched.

For banks and other financial institutions, the impact is more concrete. The institutions that file through the Secure Data Transfer Mode — the channel that appears to have been affected — have been given until December 30 to submit reports that were due between November 18 and December 29. That is a reprieve, but it is also a disruption. Compliance teams that plan their work around filing deadlines now have to replan. Reports that should have been filed in November will be filed in December. And the intelligence those reports contain — about transactions that may indicate money laundering or terrorist financing — will reach law enforcement later than it should have.

For the millions of small businesses that are required to file beneficial ownership information, the disruption is a different kind of problem. The beneficial ownership regime is already unpopular with many small business owners, who regard it as an unwelcome bureaucratic burden. A breach at the agency that holds their ownership information — even a breach that the agency says did not reach the database — will not increase their confidence in the system. The fact that the FinCEN Identifier website was unreachable, returning "This site can't be reached," will have been the first thing many of them noticed, and it will have confirmed their worst suspicions about a system they never wanted in the first place.

There is also a longer-term question about trust. FinCEN's entire mission depends on trust: banks trust the agency to protect the information they file; law enforcement trusts the agency to deliver it accurately and on time; foreign partners trust the agency to share it responsibly. A breach — even a limited one, even one that did not reach the most sensitive databases — erodes that trust. The next time FinCEN asks banks to file more information, or asks Congress for more funding, or asks a foreign partner to share intelligence, the question will be asked: can the agency protect what it already holds? The answer, for now, is that it is still investigating.

The bigger question: can the system be trusted while it is being investigated?

As the analysis site NextFin put it in its coverage of the incident, the suspected intrusion raises a question larger than whether a government website is temporarily unavailable: can the financial system's central reporting channel remain trusted while the agency investigates unauthorised access? The distinction between an operational disruption and a confirmed theft of financial intelligence is, as NextFin noted, the central fact of the story — and it is a distinction that will only be resolved when the forensic investigation is complete.

That investigation will take time. Forensic examiners need to determine which systems were accessed, what data was viewed or taken, how the intruders got in, and how long they were inside. They need to determine whether the intrusion is ongoing, whether any backdoors were left behind, and whether the systems that were taken offline can be safely restored. They need to do all of that while the agency continues to operate — while banks continue to file, while law enforcement continues to query, while the deadlines extended by FIN-2026-R002 continue to run. It is a difficult task, and it is one that the Treasury, the FBI, CISA and the third-party examiners will be working on for weeks, if not months.

In the meantime, the agency has done what agencies in this position usually do: it has taken systems offline, extended deadlines, assured the public that the most sensitive databases are untouched, and promised to restore normal operations as soon as possible. Those are the right steps. But they are also the steps that every agency takes after a breach, and they do not answer the questions that matter most. What did the intruders see? What did they take? And how do we know they are gone?

The answers to those questions will emerge slowly, in the way that answers to these questions always do: in a forensic report, in a congressional hearing, in a statement issued months after the event, when the news cycle has moved on. Until then, the FinCEN breach will stand as the latest in a series of incidents that have exposed the vulnerability of the institutions America depends on to keep its financial system clean and its secrets safe — and as a reminder that the people who are supposed to catch the criminals are themselves a target.

What to watch next

Several things will determine how serious this incident turns out to be. The first is the forensic finding on the databases. If the examiners confirm that the Bank Secrecy Act and beneficial ownership databases were not accessed, the incident will be remembered as a serious but contained disruption. If they find otherwise, it will become one of the most significant breaches of US financial intelligence in history.

The second is attribution. If the intruders are identified as a nation-state actor, the incident will become a diplomatic and national security issue, with consequences that extend far beyond FinCEN. If they are identified as a criminal group, the response will be a law enforcement matter. Either way, the identification will shape the policy response — and the political debate that follows.

The third is the restoration of the systems. FinCEN has said it is working to return all systems to normal operations as soon as possible, but it has not given a date. The longer the disruption lasts, the greater the backlog of unfiled reports, the greater the delay in the intelligence reaching law enforcement, and the greater the opportunity for those who move illicit money to exploit the gap. The December 30 deadline in FIN-2026-R002 gives the agency a little over a month. What happens after that — whether the order is extended, whether the systems are restored, whether the filings flow normally again — will be the test of whether the agency has recovered or is still struggling.

And the fourth is the response from Congress. The Treasury's financial intelligence apparatus has been breached before — FinCEN itself was the victim of a major leak in 2020, when thousands of suspicious activity reports were disclosed to journalists in the FinCEN Files investigation. That leak came from inside. This incident came from outside. Together, they suggest a pattern of vulnerability that Congress will want to examine, and that the agency will have to answer for. Hearings, funding debates and calls for reform are likely to follow, and they will shape the future of the system that sits at the centre of America's fight against financial crime.

For now, the systems are partly offline, the deadlines are extended, the investigation is ongoing, and the most important questions are unanswered. The agency that watches the money is itself being watched — and the watchers do not yet know what they are looking at.
