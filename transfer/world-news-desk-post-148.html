TITLE: 6 Students Killed and 97 Injured as Dynamite Stored Beside Their School Explodes in Afghanistan's Kunar Province LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: At least six students were killed and 97 injured when dynamite stored in a house beside a middle school exploded in Kunar, Afghanistan, on Saturday. ---BODY---

Naray, in Afghanistan's eastern Kunar province, which borders Pakistan. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0).

At least six students were killed and 97 others injured on Saturday morning when dynamite stored in a house beside a middle school exploded in Afghanistan's eastern Kunar province, according to a provincial hospital official. The blast, at about 10 a.m. local time, tore through a residential property in the Andar Ser area, next to the school, and the casualties were still being counted hours later. The figures are preliminary, the officials said, and the toll could rise.

Mawla Dad, the head of Kunar Provincial Hospital, told Al Jazeera on Saturday that at least six students had died and 97 others were injured, and he cautioned that the figures were initial and could change as more information became available. A later account from an Afghan government spokesperson, reported by Khaleej Times, put the toll higher: seven students killed and up to 99 injured. The two counts have not been reconciled, and the hospital's figure remains the one most outlets are carrying. What is not in dispute is the scale: more than a hundred children and adults hurt, in a single explosion, beside a school, on a Saturday morning.

The explosion occurred in a house beside a middle school in the Andar Ser area of Kunar province, at about 10 a.m. (05:30 GMT) on Saturday, Mawlawi Sami-ul-Haq, the director of Information and Culture in Kunar, told Al Jazeera separately. The house, he said, belonged to people involved in mining operations who had stored dynamite there. An investigation later found that the blast occurred at a depot near the school where mining companies stored explosives, according to the Afghan government spokesperson. Provincial investigative teams have reached the scene, and the authorities have said the casualty figures are preliminary.

What happened at 10 a.m.

The first accounts were confused, as first accounts of a blast usually are. Provincial police initially said the blast had taken place at a middle school in the Andar Ser area. Later reports, based on the hospital official and the provincial information director, corrected that: the explosion was in a house beside the school, not in the school itself — but beside is close enough when the explosive is dynamite. The blast happened at about 10 a.m. local time, during school hours, and the force of it reached the children inside the school building. Farid Dehqan, spokesperson for the Kunar Provincial Police Headquarters, said the explosion occurred at a school in the Andar Ser area, adding that several students had been killed or wounded, and that investigative teams from the province had reached the site to examine the circumstances.

One report named the school as Shagi Middle School. Another described the property as a depot storing explosives belonging to mining companies. The two descriptions are not contradictory: a house used by people in mining, holding dynamite, beside a school, is both a house and a depot, depending on who is describing it. What matters is what the investigators have not yet established: what triggered the detonation. The available accounts have not established precisely what caused the explosion or how the dynamite went off. There is no immediate confirmation of a trigger, and no group has claimed responsibility — because, on the evidence so far, there is nothing to claim. This was not, on the current reporting, an attack. It was an accident waiting in a house full of explosives next to a school full of children.

The injured were taken to Kunar Provincial Hospital, where Mawla Dad gave the first casualty figures to international media. The hospital is the province's main medical facility, and 97 injured — many of them children — is a large number for a provincial hospital to absorb at once. The reports do not yet say how many of the injured are in a critical condition, or how many have been transferred elsewhere. The numbers are preliminary, and the hospital's head said so twice: the toll could change as more information becomes available.

A province where mining and daily life sit side by side

Kunar province lies in eastern Afghanistan, along the country's border with Pakistan. It is a mountainous province, cut by the Kunar River, and it has been a security and humanitarian hotspot for years. It is also a mining province. The dynamite in the house beside the school was there because people in Kunar mine — and mining needs explosives. The house belonged to people involved in mining operations, according to the provincial information director, and the depot, according to the government spokesperson's investigation, belonged to mining companies. The explosives were not smuggled in for a single purpose. They were stored, as a matter of routine, in a residential property, beside a school.

That is the fact that will dominate the investigation. Dynamite is explosive material used in mining, quarrying and other industrial activities, but improper storage or handling can be dangerous — particularly in densely populated areas, as one report put it. The questions the investigators will have to answer are straightforward: who owned the materials, whether they were stored legally, whether safety precautions were taken, and what caused the explosion — accidental ignition, improper handling, or something else. The answers will determine whether anyone is held responsible, and whether anything changes. The history of such investigations in Afghanistan suggests that the answers may be slow, and that the change may be slower still.

The blast also raises a question that goes beyond one house and one school: how many other depots, houses and storerooms in Kunar — and in Afghanistan's other mining provinces — hold explosives beside schools, clinics, and homes. The incident has brought attention to the risks posed by the storage of mining explosives in residential areas, particularly in the vicinity of educational institutions, as Republic World put it. That attention is new. The risk is not.

Schools in the line of fire

Afghanistan's schools have been struck before, and the pattern is grim. In August, a hand grenade attack at a school complex in the capital, Kabul, injured more than 40 children aged between nine and 14. In May 2021, three explosions struck a school in Kabul, killing dozens of people, most of them young girls from the Hazara community. That attack took place just months before the Taliban returned to power following the withdrawal of US-led forces from Afghanistan. The two incidents are different in kind — one an attack, one, on current reporting, an accident — but they share a setting: children, in a school, in a country where the school gate has never been a guarantee of safety.

The Taliban returned to power in August 2021, and the education system they inherited has been reshaped since. The details of that reshaping are a separate story, but the basic fact is that Afghan children still go to school, in large numbers, in buildings that are often old, crowded, and close to the hazards of daily life — including, as Saturday showed, a neighbour's dynamite. The middle school in Andar Ser was in session on a Saturday morning, because the Afghan school week runs Saturday to Thursday. The children were where they were supposed to be. The dynamite was where it should never have been.

The contrast is the story. A school is supposed to be the safest building in a village. On Saturday, in Andar Ser, the safest building in the village was next door to the most dangerous one. The children who died were not the victims of a war, or a bombing campaign, or a sectarian attack. They were the victims of a storage decision — somebody's decision to keep dynamite in a house beside a school — and that is what makes the deaths so hard to accept, and so hard to prevent by the usual means. You cannot negotiate with a badly stored explosive. You can only move it, or regulate it, or stop storing it beside children.

The response so far

The official response, as reported, has been the standard one: investigative teams deployed, casualty figures described as preliminary, no further details offered. Mawlawi Sami-ul-Haq declined to comment on the exact number of casualties or the full extent of the structural damage, noting that further details were not yet available. The provincial police spokesperson confirmed that teams had been sent to the site. The government spokesperson's later account — seven killed, up to 99 injured, a mining companies' depot — suggests that an investigation is under way and producing findings, but the findings so far are limited to the location and the ownership of the explosives, not the cause.

There has been no statement, in the reporting so far, from the Taliban government in Kabul about the incident, and no announcement of any action against whoever stored the explosives. That may change. It may also not. The pattern of official responses to such incidents in Afghanistan is that the investigation is announced, the figures are updated once or twice, and the story fades — until the next one. The families in Andar Ser will not have the luxury of fading. Six or seven children are dead. Ninety-seven or ninety-nine people are injured. A school has been damaged, and a village has been reminded, in the most brutal way possible, of what sits next door.

The international response, so far, has been the reporting itself. Al Jazeera, which carried the first account from the hospital official, has kept the story in its news cycle. The wire services and the regional press have followed. There is no sign yet of an international aid response, or of a statement from the United Nations mission in Afghanistan, or from the aid agencies that work in Kunar. That may come as the casualty figures settle. The hospital will need supplies, the injured will need treatment, and the school will need repair. The province's needs are the same as they always are after a disaster in Afghanistan: immediate, and largely unmet by the state.

What the investigation must answer

The investigation now under way has four questions to answer, and the answers will decide whether Saturday's deaths lead to any change. First, who owned the dynamite, and was it stored legally? The house belonged to people involved in mining operations, and the depot belonged to mining companies — but "belonged to" is not the same as "licensed to store". Afghanistan's mining sector is a mix of licensed companies, small-scale operators, and informal diggers, and the regulation of explosives in that mix is, at best, patchy. If the dynamite was stored without a licence, or in breach of one, somebody is responsible. If it was stored legally, then the law itself is the problem.

Second, what triggered the detonation? The reports are explicit that this has not been established. Dynamite does not usually detonate on its own; it needs heat, shock, or a blasting cap. Was somebody handling it? Was there a fire? Was it old and unstable? The answer matters, because it decides whether this was carelessness, or bad luck, or something worse. Third, how much explosive was there? The scale of the damage — a house destroyed, a school hit, more than a hundred casualties — suggests a substantial quantity, not a few sticks. The quantity will tell the investigators how long the depot had been operating, and how many people knew about it.

Fourth, and most important: who knew? A depot beside a school is not a secret. The neighbours knew. The school knew. The village knew. The question is whether anyone with authority knew, and whether anyone with authority did anything about it. If the answer is that the depot was an open secret, then the failure is not just the storage. It is the system that allowed storage beside a school to become normal. That is the question that will decide whether Saturday's deaths are treated as an accident, or as a warning.

The children

The reports do not name the dead children, and they do not give their ages. They were students at a middle school, which in Afghanistan means roughly the ages of eleven to fourteen, and they were in class on a Saturday morning. The injured, 97 of them, are also mostly unnamed, and their injuries are not described. The numbers are the story for now: six dead, ninety-seven injured, or seven and ninety-nine, depending on which official you ask. The names will come later, if they come at all. In Afghanistan, they often do not.

What can be said, without waiting for the names, is that the dead were children who had done nothing more dangerous than go to school. They were not soldiers. They were not miners. They were not handling the dynamite. They were sitting in a classroom, in a building that was supposed to be safe, next to a house that was not. The blast that killed them was not aimed at them. It did not need to be. It only needed to be close enough, and it was.

The injured face a longer road. Ninety-seven people injured in a blast is a large number for a provincial hospital, and the injuries from an explosion — burns, fractures, shrapnel wounds, hearing damage — take weeks or months to heal. Some of the injured will carry the effects for life. The hospital's head has said the figures are preliminary, and the toll could change. It usually does, in both directions: some of the injured die, and some of the missing are found. The final number will be known in days. The consequences will be known for years.

Kunar: a province on the edge

Kunar province, where the blast happened, is one of Afghanistan's most distinctive and most troubled regions. It lies in the east of the country, along the border with Pakistan, and it has been described in reporting on the incident as a security and humanitarian hotspot for years. The province is mountainous, cut by the Kunar River as it runs south toward the Pakistani border, and its valleys have long been home to communities that live by farming, timber, and mining. Naray, the district pictured above, is one of its towns — a reminder that Kunar is not only a name on a map of conflict, but a place where people live, work, and send their children to school.

The border matters. Kunar sits against Pakistan's Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, and the movement of people, goods, and armed groups across that border has shaped the province's history for decades. The province was a centre of fighting during the war against the Soviet Union, during the civil war that followed, and during the twenty years of the American-led intervention. Since the Taliban returned to power in 2021, the fighting has eased, but the province's problems have not disappeared. They have changed shape: from war to the quieter hazards of daily life — a poorly stored explosive, a collapsing building, a hospital that cannot cope with a sudden influx of casualties.

The school in Andar Ser is part of that daily life. It is a middle school, in a rural area, serving the children of a farming and mining community. Its pupils walk to school on a Saturday morning because that is the school week in Afghanistan. They sit in classrooms that are, by the standards of the province, ordinary. What happened to them on Saturday was not ordinary, but the conditions that made it possible were: a mining economy that uses dynamite, a village where homes and workplaces sit side by side, and a state whose capacity to inspect, regulate, and enforce is thin on the ground. The blast was an event. The conditions are a system.

The numbers, and why they keep changing

Readers following this story will have noticed that the casualty figures have already changed once, and may change again. The first count, from the head of Kunar Provincial Hospital, was six students killed and 97 injured. The later count, from an Afghan government spokesperson, was seven killed and up to 99 injured. Neither figure is necessarily wrong. In the first hours after a blast, the count moves for several reasons, and understanding them is part of understanding the story.

First, the dead and the injured arrive at different places at different times. Some are taken to the provincial hospital. Some are taken to smaller clinics. Some are treated at the scene. The hospital's head counts the people who reach his hospital; the government spokesperson counts the figures that reach Kabul, which may include people counted elsewhere. Second, the definition of "injured" is not fixed. Some reports count everyone hurt, including those with minor injuries who are treated and released. Others count only those admitted. Third, the toll rises as the injured die, and falls as the missing are found alive — though in a blast of this kind, the movement is usually upward, as the critically injured succumb in the hours and days after.

The officials have been careful to say that the figures are preliminary, and they are right to say so. Mawla Dad said it twice. The government spokesperson's figure — "up to 99 injured" — carries the same caution in its wording. The final toll will be known in days, when the hospital has completed its count, when the injured have been triaged, and when the site has been cleared. Until then, the honest figure is a range: six or seven dead, ninety-seven or ninety-nine injured, and more than a hundred people hurt in a single explosion beside a school. The range is not a failure of reporting. It is the reporting catching up with the event.

What happens next

The immediate next steps are the investigation, the treatment of the injured, and the repair of the school. The longer next steps are the ones that matter: whether the authorities in Kunar, and in Kabul, treat the storage of mining explosives near schools as a problem to be solved, or as an accident to be mourned and forgotten. The history of Afghanistan's mining sector, and of its regulation, does not encourage optimism. But the scale of Saturday's toll — the largest single incident of its kind in the province in recent reporting — may force a response that smaller incidents did not.

For the families in Andar Ser, the next steps are simpler and harder: bury the dead, tend the injured, and decide whether to send the surviving children back to a school that sits beside a ruin. The school will reopen, or it will not. The children will return, or they will not. The dynamite is gone — detonated, in the most terrible way possible. The question is whether anything else changes, or whether the next house beside the next school is already full.

Saturday's blast in Kunar was not a battle, not a bombing, and not a mystery. It was dynamite, stored in a house, beside a school, going off at ten in the morning. Six students are dead. Ninety-seven people are injured. The figures are preliminary. The lesson is not.
