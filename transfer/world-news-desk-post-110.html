TITLE: IBM Just Gave Its H-1B Workers 3 Weeks to Leave America or Take an 80% Pay Cut — 1,125 of 1,278 Escape Jobs Are in India LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: IBM has ended US visa and green card sponsorship in its consulting arm. Affected H-1B workers have 3 weeks to relocate abroad or leave by December 31. ---BODY---

IBM chief executive Arvind Krishna in 2025. His company has ended US visa and green card sponsorship for most roles in IBM Consulting — and given affected workers three weeks to choose between relocating overseas or leaving the company by December 31. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

Imagine being called into a surprise meeting with your manager and being told that you have three weeks to make a decision that will change your life. You can take a job in another country — most likely India — at a fraction of your current salary. Or you can lose your job on December 31. That is the choice that IBM has given to some of its US-based employees on H-1B visas and green cards, according to reports published on November 26, 2026. The technology giant has ended US work visa and green card sponsorship for most positions in IBM Consulting, its largest business division, which employs more than 150,000 people around the world. The employees affected by the decision have been given three weeks to choose between relocating to an IBM role outside the United States or leaving the company — and the accounts emerging from inside the company suggest that the choice, for many of them, is not really a choice at all.

One IBM employee, writing on the anonymous workplace forum Blind, described the meeting in words that have now been quoted around the world. "I got pulled into a surprise meeting with my manager and was told I have just three weeks to choose between taking an 80 per cent pay cut to relocate to India or losing my job by December 31," the employee wrote. An 80 per cent pay cut. For a worker in the United States, on a visa that ties their right to live in the country to their employment, that is not a relocation offer. It is an ultimatum, dressed up as a choice — and it is the human face of a corporate decision that IBM first announced earlier in November, and that has now reached the people it affects.

The decision was first reported by Bloomberg on November 10, citing an internal company document shared with managers in IBM Consulting. The details of the three-week deadline, and the December 31 last working day, were reported by Rest of World, and picked up by the Economic Times, Moneycontrol, Business Standard, Deccan Herald and other outlets on November 26. IBM has not disclosed how many employees are covered by the decision. But the scale of the division involved — more than 150,000 people, the largest part of one of the world's biggest technology companies — and the scale of the relocation on offer — 1,278 consulting vacancies outside the United States, of which 1,125 are in India — give a sense of how many lives this decision touches, and how few of the options on offer are anywhere except India.

What IBM actually decided

To understand what has happened, it is important to be precise about what IBM has — and has not — done. The company has discontinued H-1B visa and green card sponsorship for the majority of roles in IBM Consulting, its consulting division. It has not ended visa and green card sponsorship across all of its business units. The policy applies to IBM Consulting, and to most positions within it. For the employees affected, the consequences are the same regardless of the fine print: the company will no longer sponsor the visa or the green card application that allows them to work, and to live, in the United States.

The H-1B visa is the main route by which skilled foreign workers come to work in the United States. It allows a US employer to hire a foreign worker in a speciality occupation — typically a job that requires a bachelor's degree or higher — for up to six years, and it is the visa that tens of thousands of technology workers, most of them from India, hold while they work for American companies. A green card — permanent residence — is the next step: it allows a worker to live and work in the United States permanently, without being tied to a single employer. For many H-1B workers, the green card application, sponsored by their employer, is the path from temporary worker to permanent resident. When an employer stops sponsoring green cards, it does not just end a job. It ends the path to permanence.

IBM's own explanation, given by a company spokesperson in response to queries from the media, was framed in the language of business strategy. "IBM Consulting participates in U.S. high-skilled visa programs to address skill shortages," the spokesperson said. "That participation has been declining for a number of years and as skill availability and client needs have evolved, we have opted to discontinue visa and green card sponsorship for the majority of such roles in IBM Consulting." The words are careful. The company is not saying it cannot find skilled workers in the United States, or that it is leaving the American market. It is saying that the skills it needs, and the needs of its clients, have changed — and that the change means it no longer needs to sponsor visas for most consulting roles. The employees who have been given three weeks to decide will read those words differently. For them, the change is not abstract. It is a deadline.

Three weeks, three months, December 31

The numbers that define the decision are stark, and they are worth setting out in full. Affected employees have been given three weeks to decide whether to accept a position outside the United States or to leave the company. Those who decline an overseas position will be offered three months of severance pay. Those who choose to leave — or who are forced to leave, because they cannot accept the relocation — have been informed that December 31, 2026, will be their final working day. Three weeks to decide. Three months of severance. A last working day that falls on New Year's Eve.

Three weeks is not a long time to decide whether to move to another country. For a worker on an H-1B visa, the decision is not just about a job. It is about a home, a lease, a car, a child's school, a spouse's job, a community built over years in the United States. It is about whether to accept a salary that, by one employee's account, could be 80 per cent lower than the one they earn now. It is about whether to uproot a family for a role in a country they may not have lived in for years — or to lose the job, take the severance, and try to find another employer in the United States willing to sponsor a visa, in a market where the largest consulting employer has just stopped doing so. Three weeks is not enough time to make that decision well. It is enough time to make it under pressure.

The three months of severance is, by the standards of the technology industry, a reasonable offer. It is three months of pay for a worker who is losing their job through no fault of their own. But for a worker on an H-1B visa, three months of severance does not solve the fundamental problem. An H-1B worker who loses their job has a limited window — 60 days, under US immigration rules — to find a new employer willing to sponsor them, or to leave the country. Three months of severance pay does not extend that window. It pays the bills while the clock runs down. And for a worker whose green card application was being sponsored by IBM, the loss of the job can mean the loss of years of progress towards permanent residence — progress that cannot be transferred to a new employer without starting over, or starting again from a different place in the queue.

The December 31 deadline adds a particular cruelty to the timing. The last working day of the year, on New Year's Eve, is the day the decision takes effect. For the employees affected, the holiday season — the weeks when families gather, when children are out of school, when the year turns — will be spent deciding whether to leave the country, or to leave the company. The timing was not chosen for cruelty; it was chosen because IBM's decision, first reported on November 10, works on a corporate calendar that ends on December 31. But the human effect is the same, whatever the reason. The workers have three weeks, in the middle of the holiday season, to decide the rest of their lives.

Why India — and why the numbers point there

The relocation on offer is, in theory, a choice of countries. In practice, the numbers tell a different story. According to Rest of World, IBM's careers portal listed 1,278 consulting vacancies outside the United States as of November 19. Of those, 1,125 were in India. Twenty-three were in Singapore. Eighteen were in the United Kingdom. The rest were scattered elsewhere. That means that 88 per cent of the overseas roles on offer are in India. For an employee who accepts relocation, the overwhelming likelihood is that they will be moving to India — and, by the account of the employee who wrote on Blind, taking a pay cut of around 80 per cent to do so.

The concentration of roles in India is not a coincidence. IBM has a huge presence in India — the country is one of the company's largest sources of talent, and one of its largest delivery centres for consulting work. The work that IBM Consulting does for its clients — much of it technology implementation, systems integration, and managed services — can be done from India, at Indian salary levels, by workers who are already familiar with the company's systems. The decision to end visa sponsorship in the United States and to offer relocation to India is, in business terms, a decision to move work to where it is cheapest to do it. The employees are being offered the chance to follow the work. The pay cut is the price of following it.

For Indian workers, the relocation is a return — to a country they may have left years ago, to pursue a career in the United States. For many of them, the return is not unwelcome in principle: India is home, and a job in India is a job at home. But the terms of the return matter. A worker who has built a life in the United States — who has a mortgage, a car loan, children in American schools, a spouse with an American job — cannot simply pick up and move to India on a salary that is a fifth of what they earn now. The 80 per cent pay cut is not a detail. It is the difference between a life in the United States and a life in India, and it is the reason that, for many of the affected workers, the choice between relocating and leaving is not a choice at all. It is a choice between two kinds of loss.

The numbers behind the decision

IBM's decision did not come out of nowhere. The numbers show a company that has been reducing its reliance on the H-1B programme for years. Federal records show that IBM had just over 1,600 H-1B petitions approved in fiscal year 2026, compared with more than 2,000 in fiscal year 2025 and nearly 3,000 in fiscal year 2024. That is a decline of almost half in two years — and it is a decline that happened before the company formally announced that it was ending sponsorship for most consulting roles. The formal decision is, in that sense, a recognition of a trend that was already under way: IBM has been sponsoring fewer and fewer H-1B workers, year after year, and the announcement simply makes the direction of travel official.

The wider context is the H-1B programme itself, and the workers who depend on it. Indian nationals accounted for 69.9 per cent of approved H-1B beneficiaries — roughly 283,000 people — while China accounted for 12.1 per cent, according to the data cited in the reports on IBM's decision. That means that a decision by a major employer to stop sponsoring H-1B visas falls, overwhelmingly, on Indian workers. It is Indian professionals who make up the largest share of H-1B visa holders, and it is Indian professionals who make up the largest share of the workers waiting for green cards — a queue that, for Indian nationals, can stretch for decades, because of the per-country limits on green cards. For an Indian H-1B worker, the loss of an employer's sponsorship is not just the loss of a job. It is the loss of a place in a queue that may have taken years to reach.

The decline in IBM's H-1B petitions — from nearly 3,000 to just over 1,600 in two years — also tells a story about the technology industry as a whole. IBM is not the only company reducing its use of the H-1B programme, and it is not the only company moving work to India. The consulting industry, in particular, has been shifting work offshore for years, as clients demand lower costs and as the technology for remote delivery improves. IBM's decision is a particularly visible example of that shift, because of the size of the company and the bluntness of the terms — three weeks, 80 per cent, December 31 — but it is not an isolated one. It is part of a wider movement of work, and of workers, that has been under way for a decade, and that has now reached the people who do the work.

What the workers can actually do

For the employees affected by IBM's decision, the options are limited, and each of them carries a cost. The first option is to accept relocation — most likely to India — and take the pay cut. For a worker with family in India, or with a reason to return, this may be the best available choice. It keeps the job, it keeps the income, and it keeps the worker inside IBM. But it means leaving the United States, giving up a life built there, and accepting a salary that may not support the financial commitments — a mortgage, a car loan, a child's education — that were taken on at an American salary. For a worker with a spouse who works in the United States, or with children in American schools, the relocation is not just a job change. It is a family decision, made in three weeks.

The second option is to decline relocation, take the three months of severance, and try to find another employer in the United States who will sponsor an H-1B visa. This is the option that keeps the worker in the United States, but it is a race against the clock. An H-1B worker who loses their job has 60 days to find a new sponsor or leave the country. The job market for sponsored workers is competitive, and the number of employers willing to sponsor is shrinking — IBM's decision is evidence of that. The worker must find a new job, with a new sponsor, in a market where the largest consulting employer has just exited the sponsorship business, and they must do it in 60 days, over the holiday season, while the rest of the industry is also slowing down for the end of the year.

The third option is to leave the United States altogether — to return to India, or to another country, without an IBM job, and to start again. For some workers, this is the option they will take, because the other two are impossible. For others, it is the option they will be forced into, because the 60 days ran out, or because the pay cut was too steep, or because the family could not move. The reports on IBM's decision did not say how many workers would take each option, and IBM has not disclosed how many workers are affected. But the accounts from Blind — the surprise meetings, the three-week deadline, the 80 per cent pay cut — suggest that the workers themselves are already doing the maths, and that many of them are concluding that none of the options are good.

A decision that reaches beyond IBM

IBM's decision matters beyond IBM, because IBM is not a small company, and because the consulting industry is not a small industry. When the largest division of one of the world's biggest technology companies stops sponsoring visas for most of its roles, it sends a signal to the rest of the industry — and to the workers who depend on sponsorship — about the direction of travel. Other companies will watch what IBM does, and some of them will follow. Other workers will watch what happens to IBM's employees, and they will draw their own conclusions about the security of their own jobs, and the value of their own sponsorship.

The decision also matters because of what it says about the H-1B programme itself. The programme was created to allow American companies to hire skilled foreign workers when they could not find the skills they needed in the United States. IBM's spokesperson said, in effect, that the company no longer needs the programme for most consulting roles — because the skills are available elsewhere, or because the clients' needs have changed. That is a business judgement, and IBM is entitled to make it. But it is also a judgement about the value of the workers who hold those visas, and about the future of the programme. If the largest consulting employer in the world no longer needs to sponsor visas, what does that mean for the hundreds of thousands of workers who hold them, and for the companies that still do?

There is also a question about the timing. The decision was first reported on November 10, and the three-week deadline was reported on November 26 — sixteen days later. The workers were told in surprise meetings with their managers, individually, not in a company-wide announcement. The process — individual meetings, a short deadline, a choice between relocation and departure — is the process of a company that wants the decision to be made quickly, and quietly. It is not the process of a company that wants a public debate about the future of visa sponsorship, or about the treatment of its foreign workers. The accounts on Blind are the reason the decision is public at all. Without them, the workers would have been given their three weeks in private, and the rest of the world would not have known.

The human cost of a corporate strategy

At the centre of this story is a simple fact: the workers affected by IBM's decision are people, not numbers. They are the 283,000 Indian professionals who make up 69.9 per cent of approved H-1B beneficiaries, and the thousands more from China and other countries. They are the workers who came to the United States on the promise that their skills were needed, who built lives there, who paid taxes there, who raised children there, and who waited — in some cases for years, in some cases for more than a decade — for a green card that would let them stay permanently. IBM's decision does not just end their sponsorship. It ends the promise.

The 80 per cent pay cut is the number that has travelled furthest, and it is the number that captures the human cost most vividly. A pay cut of that size is not a reduction in living standards. It is a different life. A worker who earns an American salary in the United States and is offered an Indian salary in India is being offered a different country, a different home, a different future — and being told to decide in three weeks. The worker who wrote on Blind — "I got pulled into a surprise meeting with my manager and was told I have just three weeks to choose between taking an 80 per cent pay cut to relocate to India or losing my job by December 31" — was not describing a business strategy. They were describing the moment their life changed, in a meeting they did not expect, with a deadline they did not choose.

IBM is a company with a long history — founded in 1911, a century of innovation, a name that is synonymous with American technology. Arvind Krishna, the chief executive, has led the company since 2020, and has spoken about IBM's role in the world, about artificial intelligence, about the future of work. The decision to end visa sponsorship in IBM Consulting is, from the company's perspective, a decision about the future of work — about where work is done, and by whom, and at what cost. From the perspective of the workers, it is a decision about their lives. Both perspectives are true. But only one of them has a three-week deadline.

What happens next will be decided, in part, by the workers themselves — by how many of them accept relocation, how many of them find new sponsors, how many of them leave. It will be decided, in part, by the rest of the industry — by whether other companies follow IBM's lead, or whether they see an opportunity to hire the workers IBM is letting go. And it will be decided, in part, by the US government — by whether the H-1B programme, and the green card system, continue to serve the workers they were created for, or whether they become a system in which a worker can spend years building a life, only to be told, in a surprise meeting, that they have three weeks to leave. The workers have until December 31. The rest of us have longer to decide what this decision means. But the workers do not have the luxury of time — and that, in the end, is the point.
