TITLE: One Year After Hong Kong's Deadliest Fire in 78 Years — 168 Dead, 40 Arrested, and Families Still Waiting for Justice LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A year after the Wang Fuk Court fire killed 168 people, Hong Kong marks its deadliest blaze since 1948 — and families still wait for justice ---BODY---

Wang Fuk Court in Tai Po, Hong Kong, burning on the night of November 26, 2025. The fire killed 168 people, including a firefighter, and displaced nearly 5,000 residents. Photo: am730 via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

One year ago today, on the afternoon of November 26, 2025, a fire broke out at the Wang Fuk Court housing estate in Tai Po, in the northern New Territories of Hong Kong, and within 90 minutes it had spread to seven of the estate's eight tower blocks. It burned for more than 43 hours. When it was finally extinguished, 168 people were dead, including a firefighter, 79 more were injured, and nearly 5,000 residents had lost their homes. It was the deadliest fire in Hong Kong since 1948, and, according to the news agency AFP, the world's deadliest residential building blaze since 1980. Today, on the first anniversary of the disaster, former residents are returning to the charred estate to mourn, churches across the city are holding special masses for the dead, and the families of the victims are asking the same question they have asked for twelve months: who will be held accountable, and when?

The answer, a year on, is unfinished. An official investigation by a judge-led committee has blamed the disaster on a chain of failures — substandard materials, deception in a building maintenance project, and inadequate regulatory oversight — and described the fire as "just like a perfect storm, exposing a series of deficiencies in every sector responsible for the execution and supervision of the works." Two companies and seven people have been charged with offences including manslaughter and conspiracy to defraud, and at least 40 people have been arrested. Hong Kong's housing secretary resigned last month to take political responsibility, and the city's leaders have bowed in apology before reporters. But almost a year after the fire, at least seven professionals named in the inquiry report still hold their licences and professional memberships, the criminal cases have not been resolved, and survivors say the system that failed them has barely changed. "The report named them but they can just keep doing what they do," said Betty Ho Pui-shan, a 61-year-old former resident, in an interview with the South China Morning Post. "I don't think the authorities are listening at all."

This is the story of the fire that changed Hong Kong, of the year that followed it, and of the families who are still waiting — for justice, for answers, and for a permanent memorial to the 168 people who never came home.

The afternoon the estate caught fire

Wang Fuk Court was, for more than four decades, an ordinary Hong Kong success story. Completed in 1983, the eight-block estate sits on Yuen Chau Tsai in Tai Po, where the Lam Tsuen River meets the sheltered inlet of Tolo Harbour, land that residents described as propitious. Part of the government's Home Ownership Scheme, it was the embodiment of middle-class Hong Kong in the 1980s: eight 31-storey towers, nearly 2,000 flats, home to about 4,800 residents, many of them elderly people who had lived there for decades. On the afternoon of November 26, 2025, the estate was in the middle of a HK$330 million (US$42.3 million) exterior renovation, and most of its towers were wrapped in scaffolding and dust netting, their windows walled up with foam boards.

The weather that day had cooled, and it was dry and breezy. Nobody in the estate knew that outside their blocks, encased in scaffolding and with windows covered in styrofoam boards, the first sparks of what would become Hong Kong's deadliest fire in decades were starting to catch. One resident, Lee, was inspecting her hob when a neighbour knocked on her door to warn of a fire at Wang Cheong House, six buildings away. She looked through a crack in her bathroom window and saw the flames swelling. Without grabbing anything, she and her helper headed down from their 23rd-floor flat to get a better look at ground level. By the time she got down, she later recalled, the fire was already raging fiercely, and the burning scaffolding nets were on the verge of collapsing. Within 15 minutes, Wang Cheong House had morphed into a fiery pillar of raging flames, and the blaze was soon spreading to a neighbouring block, inching towards her home in Wang Yan House.

What happened next was, by every account, terrifyingly fast. The fire spread to seven buildings within 90 minutes, carried by winds, by burning fragments and flying embers, and by the very materials that were supposed to be protecting the estate during its renovation. The scaffolding nets, which were not fire-retardant, caught fire and carried the flames up the faces of the towers. The foam boards covering the windows melted and fell away, producing burning droplets that set fire to whatever they touched below. The bamboo scaffolding, a common feature of Hong Kong construction, burned too. Residents fled down stairwells that were filling with smoke, because the windows in the staircases had been replaced with aluminium composite panels or wooden boards for the workers' convenience, allowing smoke to infiltrate the escape routes. The buildings' fire alarm system had been shut down. Most of the 168 people who died were killed not by flames but by smoke inhalation.

Firefighters battled the blaze for more than 43 hours straight. One of their own was among the dead. By the time the last flames were out, seven of the eight towers were gutted, thousands of people were homeless, and Hong Kong was in shock. The first reports, on the morning of November 27, spoke of at least 44 dead and 279 people still missing, as rescue teams searched the blackened towers. The final toll, when it came, was far worse: 168 dead, 79 injured, about 4,600 residents displaced. It was, by a wide margin, the worst fire Hong Kong had seen since 1948, when a blaze in a crowded tenement killed more than 170 people, and it was a disaster that, in the words of the city's own inquiry, should never have been allowed to happen.

A cigarette, a perfect storm

The official investigation, led by a judge and released last month, a year after the fire, found that the blaze was most likely caused by a cigarette. Evidence showed that the fire was likely started when a cigarette ignited paper materials on the flat roof outside two first-level apartments of Wang Cheong House. Investigators found cigarette ends and partly burnt materials, including cardboard boxes, at the scene, and there was substantial evidence of construction workers smoking on site, including on the scaffolding itself. Residents had, the report said, complained repeatedly about the fire risks, and about workers smoking, and their complaints had been ignored.

But the cigarette was only the spark. What turned a small fire into a catastrophe was, the inquiry found, a chain of failures that ran through every level of the project. The non-fire-retardant scaffolding nets and canvases used in the maintenance project played a key role in helping the flames spread rapidly, and experts told the inquiry that if the nets had been fire-retardant, the initial fire might have self-extinguished. The foam boards used to cover apartment windows were also a problem: tests showed they would melt and fall off when heated, producing burning droplets that assisted secondary ignition. Airborne burning fragments and flying embers, carried by winds, helped spread the fire to neighbouring buildings. And the buildings' own defences had been stripped away: staircase windows replaced with panels and boards, the fire alarm system shut down, escape routes filling with smoke.

The inquiry's conclusion was unsparing. "The inferno was just like a perfect storm," the report said, "exposing a series of deficiencies in every sector responsible for the execution and supervision of the works." The report found that the main contractor, Prestige Construction and Engineering Company, and the project consultant, Will Power Architects Company, had cut corners and were the "main culprits" responsible for the fire and the high death toll. Prestige, the report said, had procured non-fire-retardant scaffolding nets and failed to curb workers' smoking effectively, and Will Power had not stepped in. A month before the fire, Will Power had told Prestige about an upcoming government inspection of the scaffolding nets, and the contractor had then urgently ordered a batch of fire-retardant nets to replace those at the bottom of the scaffolding before the check — a deception that, the report said, was emblematic of the project. Evidence also showed that multiple registered contractors and professionals had done no substantive work and had signed statutory forms in return for a fee, and that the registered inspector responsible for supervising the repair works was "rubber-stamping whatever statutory forms" were put in front of him.

Nor did the inquiry spare the government. Four government bodies — the Housing Bureau's independent checking unit, the Fire Services Department, the Labour Department and the Buildings Department — "bear part of the responsibility" for the disaster, the report said. Regulators, it found, had been "deceived" by the contractors, but they had also failed to supervise a project that was, by any measure, high-risk. The report described a system in which Hong Kong had long relied on self-regulation in the construction industry, a system dating back to the colonial era, in which the government played a minimal supervisory role, and in which professional bodies were independent, self-governing outfits with limited external oversight. "The professional bodies must show their determination to weed out the bad elements from the system," the former transport and housing minister Anthony Cheung Bing-leung said, in comments that captured the inquiry's central finding: that the fire had exposed "the banality of evil within the bureaucratic system," in which bureaucrats were "too risk-averse" and, as the inquiry found, were "shrinking responsibility."

Forty arrests, seven charged — and still no trial

The criminal investigation moved quickly at first. Within days of the fire, police had arrested three men — the directors and an engineering consultant of the construction company responsible for the renovation works — on suspicion that some of the materials used on the exterior walls did not meet fire safety standards. By October of this year, at least 40 people had been arrested as part of the criminal investigation, police told AFP, and of those, 22 were arrested on suspicion of manslaughter and fraud. In June, prosecutors charged two companies and seven people with offences including manslaughter and conspiracy to defraud, among them the registered inspector overseeing the repair works. Authorities allege that some of the defendants concealed Prestige's previous litigation records, helping it win a renovation contract worth more than US$38 million.

The seven people charged include the people the inquiry named as the "main culprits": Hau Wa-kin and Gordon Ho Kin-yip, the people responsible for the management of Prestige Construction and Engineering, and Jason Wong Hap-yin and Wilson Ng Yeuk, from Will Power Architects. "They deserve the most severe criticisms from the committee," the report said, finding that they were "fully aware of the fire risks but ignored all complaints and safety concerns raised repeatedly by residents, used non-fire-retardant scaffolding nets and highly flammable foam boards to cover windows," and created access openings in escape routes. But the charges, filed in June, have not been resolved, no trial date has been set, and the defendants' pleas have not been reported. For the families of the 168 dead, the wait for a courtroom reckoning continues.

And there is a second, bitterer complaint. Almost a year after the fire, a check by the South China Morning Post found that at least seven professionals implicated in the disaster remain licensed or retain their professional memberships, even as the government promises tougher oversight and swifter disciplinary action. Some, the newspaper found, were even granted bail after their arrests, and can still operate. The harsh reality, the newspaper reported, is that the professional bodies have for decades been independent, self-governing outfits, with the government's ability to intervene limited, often, to the end of internal disciplinary processes that can take years to resolve. Unless a clear crime has been committed under other laws, the authorities have long lacked the legal teeth to act. For former residents like Betty Ho, that is intolerable. "The report named them but they can just keep doing what they do," she said. "I don't think the authorities are listening at all."

There was also, in the months after the fire, a darker turn. Authorities arrested, in the name of national security, some activists who were demanding accountability for the disaster, in a move that has been widely condemned by rights groups and by the families themselves. The arrests, which came as Hong Kong's leaders were apologising for the fire, sent a chilling message about the limits of public grief in a city where even mourning can be treated as a political act. For the families, who had lost everything, the message was unmistakable: demand justice, and you may find yourself in a cell.

The families who are still grieving

For the survivors, the year since the fire has been, in the words of many who spoke to the South China Morning Post, a year spent "in survival mode." The fire displaced nearly 5,000 residents, and many of them have spent the last twelve months in temporary housing, applying for donations, attending funerals, and trying to rebuild lives that were, in a single afternoon, reduced to ash. The estate, which was home to about 4,800 people, is now a scar on the landscape of Tai Po: eight barren buildings, visible from the MTR, that residents avoid looking at when they can. "Not a day has gone by that Lee does not think back to that tragic afternoon last November 26," the newspaper wrote of one survivor, who escaped from her 23rd-floor flat with her helper and nothing else. "By the time I got down, the fire was already raging fiercely," she recalled, tears welling up, "and the burning scaffolding nets were on the verge of collapsing."

Yip Ka Kui, 68, lost his wife, Pak Sui Lin, in the fire, and his home with her. A year later, he still sends her voice messages and photographs on WhatsApp, as if she were still there to receive them. "A year after the fire that took his wife's life, Yip Ka Kui still sends her voice messages and photos on WhatsApp," Reuters reported this week, in a story that has been carried around the world. Phyllis Lo, 49, lost her mother in the fire, and was photographed this week wiping away tears in front of Wang Fuk Court during an interview, a year after the blaze that claimed 168 lives. These are not statistics. They are people, and their grief, a year on, is as raw as it was on the day the towers burned.

Many former residents are expected to return to Wang Fuk Court today, on the first anniversary, to mark the disaster, to lay flowers, and to remember. The head of Hong Kong's Catholic diocese, Cardinal Stephen Chow Sau-yan, has asked all of the city's parishes to hold special masses and prayers for the dead on November 26, one year after the blaze broke out. And there is a campaign, led by survivors, for the government to build a permanent monument listing the names of all 168 victims on the original site of Wang Fuk Court, which is planned to become a park. "Let us, the residents of Wang Fuk Court, bear all the suffering on behalf of everyone," one survivor, Fong, said. "Because Wang Fuk Court isn't just tied to Tai Po. It's tied to all of Hong Kong."

That sentiment — that the fire belongs to the whole city, not just to one estate — is one reason the disaster has resonated so deeply in Hong Kong. The city is a place of tower blocks, and of renovation projects, and of scaffolding and netting on almost every street. Every Hong Konger who lives in a high-rise, which is to say almost every Hong Konger, looked at the burning towers of Wang Fuk Court and saw their own home. The fire exposed not just the failures of one project, but the fragility of a system on which millions of people depend every day. It is a system that, the inquiry found, had been allowed to drift, for decades, into a state in which contractors could cut corners, professionals could sign forms for a fee, and regulators could look the other way — until, one dry and breezy afternoon in November, the whole thing caught fire.

The minister who resigned — and the system that stayed

The political fallout was swift, and, in Hong Kong terms, dramatic. Nearly a year after the fire, Hong Kong's leaders bowed in apology before reporters, and the Secretary for Housing, Winnie Ho Wing-yin, resigned last month to take political responsibility for the disaster. It was a rare moment of accountability in a city where ministers seldom resign, and it was, for the families, a small and partial vindication. But it was also, they say, not enough. An "administrative accountability" process is still under way, and the inquiry's findings about the four government departments that bear part of the responsibility have yet to produce the kind of reckoning that the families want. "Hong Kong's siloed governance arrangements have long struggled with poor coordination and weak accountability," John Burns, a public administration professor, told AFP, in an assessment that captures the structural problem the fire exposed.

The government has promised reforms. Officials say they are looking to tighten building codes, to crack down on bid-rigging — the practice believed to have been misused by contractors to obtain project licences — and to strengthen oversight of renovation projects. The government had already announced plans, before the fire, to phase out bamboo scaffolding in public projects because of safety concerns, and the disaster has given that plan new urgency. But the families, and the inquiry itself, are sceptical that promises will be enough. The report noted that Hong Kong has long relied on self-regulation in the construction industry, and that the professional bodies that are supposed to police it have, for decades, been independent and self-governing, with disciplinary processes that can take years. "Unless a clear crime has been committed under other ordinances," the South China Morning Post reported, "authorities have long lacked the legal teeth to act." The seven professionals who still hold their licences a year after the fire are, in that sense, not an aberration. They are the system working as it was designed to work.

That is the deeper story of the Wang Fuk Court fire, and it is the story that will matter long after the anniversary masses are over. The fire was not, in the end, an accident. It was the predictable result of a system in which safety was treated as a cost to be cut, in which warnings were ignored, in which forms were signed for a fee, and in which nobody — not the contractor, not the consultant, not the inspector, not the regulator — took responsibility until 168 people were dead. The inquiry called it a "perfect storm." The families call it something simpler: a crime, and a crime for which, a year later, nobody has yet been punished.

What Hong Kong learned — and what it has not

There are, inevitably, lessons to be drawn from the fire, and some of them are already being applied. The first is the simplest, and the most damning: that the materials used on a building site can kill. Non-fire-retardant scaffolding nets, foam boards over windows, bamboo scaffolding, aluminium panels in stairwells — each of these, on its own, was a risk, and together they turned a cigarette into a catastrophe. Experts told the inquiry that if the nets had been fire-retardant, the initial fire might have self-extinguished, and that single fact, more than any other, captures the senselessness of the disaster. The difference between a small fire and 168 deaths was, in the end, a matter of the material of a net.

The second lesson is about warnings. Residents had complained, repeatedly, about the fire risks at Wang Fuk Court, and about workers smoking on site, and their complaints were ignored — not by one person, but by a chain of people, from the contractor to the consultant to the government departments that were supposed to be checking the work. The inquiry found that the people responsible for the project were "fully aware of the fire risks but ignored all complaints and safety concerns raised repeatedly by residents." That is not a failure of knowledge. It is a failure of care, and it is a failure that, in a city of tower blocks, should frighten everyone.

The third lesson is about the system itself. Hong Kong's construction industry, the inquiry found, had been allowed to operate on a model of self-regulation that dated back to the colonial era, in which the government played a minimal supervisory role and professional bodies policed themselves. That model, the inquiry said, had produced a culture in which "shrinking responsibility" was the norm, in which bureaucrats were "too risk-averse" to act, and in which the people who were supposed to be checking the work were, in some cases, being paid to sign forms without doing any. The fire, in that sense, was not just a failure of a building. It was a failure of governance, and it is a failure that will take more than a resignation, or an apology, or a promise of reform, to fix.

And there is a fourth lesson, one that the families themselves have taught the city. In the year since the fire, they have refused to let the disaster be forgotten, refused to accept that 168 deaths were an accident, and refused to stop asking who is responsible. They have returned to the estate, they have spoken to reporters, they have demanded a memorial, and they have, in the face of arrests and intimidation, continued to insist that the truth be told. Their grief is private, but their demand is public, and it is a demand that Hong Kong cannot, and should not, ignore. "Let us, the residents of Wang Fuk Court, bear all the suffering on behalf of everyone," Fong said. It is a generous sentiment, and a heartbreaking one, and it is a reminder that the cost of the fire was not borne by the people who caused it, but by the people who lived in the towers, and who trusted that the system would keep them safe.

A year on, the towers still stand — empty

Today, on the first anniversary of the fire, Wang Fuk Court stands empty, its eight towers blackened and barren, visible from the MTR as a reminder of what happened there. The estate, which was home to nearly 5,000 people, is now a ruin, and the plan is to turn the site into a park, with a permanent monument listing the names of all 168 victims. It is a fitting memorial, and it is one that the survivors have fought for. But it is also, in its way, a measure of what was lost: not just 168 lives, but a community, a neighbourhood, a place where people had lived for decades, and where they had expected to live for decades more.

The fire at Wang Fuk Court was, by the numbers, the worst in Hong Kong since 1948, and the world's deadliest residential building fire since 1980. But numbers, however stark, do not capture what it meant. It meant a husband sending voice messages to a wife who will never hear them. It meant a daughter wiping away tears in front of a blackened tower. It meant a woman fleeing down 23 floors with her helper and nothing else, and looking back to see her home in flames. It meant, for thousands of people, a year spent in survival mode, and a future that is still uncertain. And it meant, for Hong Kong, a reckoning with a system that had failed, catastrophically, and that has, a year later, still not been fixed.

That reckoning is not over. The criminal cases have not been tried. The professionals named in the inquiry still hold their licences. The reforms have been promised but not yet delivered. And the families are still waiting — for justice, for answers, and for the day when they can say, with confidence, that what happened at Wang Fuk Court will never happen again. Until that day comes, the towers will stand as a reminder, and the names of the 168 dead will stand as an indictment, of a city that knew the risks, and ignored them, and paid the price.
