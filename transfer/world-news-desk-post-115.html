TITLE: She Has Lived 1 Year With a Pig's Kidney — Molly Falco, 58, Just Set the Record That Could End the Organ Shortage LABELS: Health SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Molly Falco, 58, is the first patient to live more than a year with a genetically engineered pig kidney — free from dialysis since November 2025. ---BODY---

Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, where Molly Falco received her genetically engineered pig kidney on November 22, 2025. One year later, the kidney is still working — the longest a genetically engineered pig kidney has ever functioned in a living human. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

Molly Falco has lived for one year with a kidney from a genetically modified pig — longer than any patient in history — and she is still free from dialysis. The 58-year-old mother of two from Lyndeborough, New Hampshire, reached the milestone on Sunday, November 22, 2026, exactly one year after surgeons at Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston transplanted the organ. Her doctors say the pig kidney continues to filter waste from her blood, make urine, and keep her fluid and mineral levels in balance — doing, in other words, everything a human kidney is supposed to do.

The announcement, made by the biotechnology company eGenesis and Mass General Brigham, marks the longest sustained function of a genetically engineered pig kidney in a living human recipient, and the first time any patient has surpassed one year of dialysis independence after receiving one. For the tens of thousands of Americans waiting for a kidney transplant — 95,492 of them as of July 30, 2026, on a national waiting list where roughly 12 people die every day — the milestone is the strongest evidence yet that organs from genetically engineered animals could one day close the gap between the number of people who need a kidney and the number of kidneys available.

"I'm amazed every day that it's still working," Falco said after a recent appointment at Mass General Brigham. "I'm going to try to keep it as long as I can, and if it's forever, that's incredible." She has named the kidney Lily, after the flowers her relatives in Colorado sent following the surgery. A year ago, she said, she did not know what the future would hold. "Living with kidney disease had taken so much away from me, and I wanted the chance to get my life back."

Her doctor agrees the milestone matters — and that it is not yet a victory. "It gives us a lot of confidence that this is feasible — the kidneys can work," said Dr. Leonardo Riella, medical director of kidney transplantation at Massachusetts General Hospital, who oversees Falco's care. "But it is not a victory lap yet." Riella said he hopes the kidney will keep working for at least another year. Falco hopes it will last until a human donor kidney becomes available. If it does not, she will return to dialysis. For now, though, she is cooking family meals again, seeing friends, and driving — things she could not manage during the 11 months she spent on dialysis.

A lifetime of kidney disease, and 11 months on dialysis

Falco was born with a condition that damaged her kidneys. For most of her life, she managed. She raised two children, worked as a chef, and built a life in Lyndeborough, a small town in southern New Hampshire. But in her 50s, her kidney function deteriorated rapidly, progressing to end-stage kidney disease — the point at which the kidneys can no longer filter waste from the blood well enough to keep a person alive without treatment.

She began dialysis in 2024. Dialysis is a treatment that does the work of failed kidneys, filtering the blood several times a week, for hours at a time. It keeps people alive, but it takes over their lives. Patients on dialysis typically spend three days a week, four hours at a time, tethered to a machine. They must follow strict limits on fluids and food. They tire easily. Travel becomes difficult. Work becomes difficult. Falco spent 11 months on dialysis, and she has described it plainly: "The only thing I wanted when I started dialysis is to immediately get off dialysis."

The ordinary route off dialysis is a transplant from a human donor. But Falco faced a longer wait than most. Because of her rare blood type, doctors estimated she would have to wait five to seven years for a donor kidney, compared with the US average of two to five years. Five to seven years on dialysis is not just an inconvenience; it is a sentence. The longer a person spends on dialysis, the weaker they become, and the less likely a transplant is to succeed when an organ finally arrives. About half of the people on the kidney waiting list will either die or become too sick for a transplant before their five-to-seven-year wait is over.

That is what made the experimental option so compelling. When her doctors raised the possibility of a pig kidney — a genetically engineered organ from a pig, transplanted under a special authorisation from the US Food and Drug Administration — Falco barely hesitated. "I practically said yes before the doctor really asked the question," she told WMUR-TV. "I was willing to try anything. Really not afraid. I haven't had fear or nervousness or, you know, regret at all."

The surgery: November 22, 2025

On November 22, 2025, Falco became the fourth person to receive a pig kidney at Massachusetts General Hospital, under the FDA's Expanded Access pathway — the route that allows experimental treatment outside a clinical trial for patients with serious conditions and no satisfactory alternative. The kidney came from a pig developed by the biotechnology company eGenesis, and it had undergone 69 genetic edits.

The edits fall into three classes, each designed to solve one of the problems that has blocked animal-to-human organ transplants — xenotransplantation — for decades. Fifty-nine of the edits inactivated porcine endogenous retroviruses, viruses embedded in the pig's DNA that could, in theory, infect a human recipient. Three edits removed markers on the surface of the pig's cells that trigger immediate rejection by the human immune system — the violent, minutes-to-hours reaction that destroyed early attempts at xenotransplantation. And seven edits introduced human genes designed to moderate immune responses and inflammation and to improve compatibility with the human blood-clotting system.

The organ itself is called EGEN-2784, eGenesis's lead product. The company, based in Cambridge, Massachusetts, has spent years developing pigs whose organs are engineered, gene by gene, to be compatible with the human body. Its chief executive, Dr. Michael Curtis, called Falco's one-year milestone "a historic achievement in transplantation" and "the culmination of decades of scientific research and medical innovation." In a joint statement with Mass General Brigham, the company said: "For the first time, a genetically engineered porcine kidney has enabled a patient to remain free from dialysis for an entire year."

The surgery itself went well, but the weeks that followed were not smooth. Falco had complications soon after receiving the organ, and she was hospitalised. That is not unusual after a transplant of any kind — the body must be taught, with powerful immunosuppressant drugs, not to attack the new organ — and it is especially expected after an experimental one. What matters is what happened next: she recovered, she left hospital, and she has not needed a hospital stay since February 2026. Nine months without a hospital admission, for a patient with an experimental animal organ, is itself a kind of record.

One year on: what the kidney is doing now

A year after the transplant, Falco's kidney is doing the full job of a human kidney. According to Riella, it filters toxins from her blood, produces urine, and regulates her fluid and mineral balance. Those three functions sound modest, but they are the whole of what a kidney does, and they are what dialysis replaces, imperfectly, for hours at a time, three days a week. A kidney that does them continuously, day and night, is the difference between a life organised around a machine and a life organised around everything else.

The change in Falco's daily life is the real measure of the milestone. She is cooking family meals again — she was a chef before kidney disease took over, and the kitchen is where she feels most herself. She is seeing friends, which dialysis had made exhausting. She is driving again, which she was too sick to do during the 11 months she was on dialysis. "I feel great, I don't feel sick," she said a year after the surgery. She still tires quickly and can get out of breath easily, and she still has to wear a mask in public to prevent infections — the immunosuppressant drugs she takes to protect the kidney leave her vulnerable to illness — but she describes her life now as "almost normal."

That "almost" carries a lot of weight. A transplant recipient's life is never quite normal. The drugs that keep the body from rejecting the organ must be taken every day, for as long as the organ lasts, and they bring their own side effects: susceptibility to infection, fatigue, and long-term risks that doctors monitor constantly. Falco's doctors watch her closely, with regular appointments at Mass General Brigham, and every test is a small verdict on whether Lily — the name she gave the kidney — is still holding up. So far, every verdict has been good.

Riella's caution — "it is not a victory lap yet" — is the voice of a doctor who has seen experimental transplants fail. The history of pig kidney transplantation is short and sobering, and Falco's success stands out precisely because of what happened to the patients before her.

The six patients who came before — and beside — her

Massachusetts General Hospital has now given six patients kidneys from pigs developed by eGenesis, and their stories trace the arc of the field. The first patient died shortly after the transplant, of underlying heart disease — a reminder that the people who receive experimental organs are often desperately ill in more than one way. Two other patients had their pig kidneys for eight and nine months respectively before their bodies rejected them — long enough to prove the organs could work, not long enough to prove they could last. Two more patients received pig kidneys this year, and both still have them.

Falco is the one who broke through the one-year barrier, and the one whose kidney is still working. Her case has now smashed the record for the longest a pig kidney has functioned in a human, and the record she broke was set by the patients who came just before her. That pattern — each patient lasting a little longer than the last — is how a new medical technology becomes a real treatment. The first heart transplant patient lived 18 days. The first liver transplant patients lived weeks. The science advances one patient at a time, and each patient who lives longer than the last proves a little more of what is possible.

The comparison that matters most is with the alternative. A patient on dialysis waits, on average, two to five years for a human kidney — five to seven, with a rare blood type like Falco's. A pig kidney that lasts a year, and perhaps longer, could serve as a bridge: keeping a patient off dialysis, keeping them strong, until a human kidney becomes available. Or, if the organs prove durable enough, a pig kidney could become the transplant itself — an organ that lasts for years, available on demand, without a waiting list at all. Falco's case does not prove either future. It proves that the first step — a year of function — is possible, and that is the step everything else depends on.

The science: 69 edits and three problems solved

To understand why Falco's kidney works, it helps to understand why pig organs did not work for most of the last century. The idea of transplanting animal organs into humans is old — surgeons tried it with chimpanzee and baboon kidneys in the 1960s, and the organs were rejected within days. The human immune system is exquisitely good at recognising foreign tissue, and a pig's organ is about as foreign as tissue gets. Three problems had to be solved before a pig kidney could survive in a human body, and eGenesis's 69 edits address all three.

The first problem is hyperacute rejection: the immediate, violent immune reaction that destroys a transplanted organ within minutes or hours. It is triggered by markers on the surface of the donor organ's cells — molecules the human immune system recognises as foreign and attacks on sight. Three of the edits in Falco's kidney removed the genes that produce those markers, so the human immune system does not sound the alarm the moment blood starts flowing through the organ.

The second problem is subtler: even without hyperacute rejection, the human immune system will attack a foreign organ over days and weeks, and the blood-clotting system can clot inside it, choking it off. Seven edits introduced human genes into the pig's DNA — genes that moderate the immune response, reduce inflammation, and make the organ's blood vessels behave more like human blood vessels, so that clots do not form where they should not.

The third problem is one that only became visible with modern genetics: porcine endogenous retroviruses, or PERVs — viruses embedded in the pig's own genome, passed down through generations, that could in theory jump to a human recipient and cause disease. Fifty-nine edits inactivated those viral sequences, removing the risk at its source. It is the largest single class of edits in the kidney, and it is the one that makes regulators comfortable: the FDA's Expanded Access authorisation, and the planned clinical trial, both depend on the assurance that the organ cannot transmit a pig virus to its recipient.

Those 69 edits are the product of decades of work in gene editing — work that only became practical with the advent of tools like CRISPR, which allow scientists to make precise, multiple changes to a genome. "A few years ago, the question was whether a genetically edited pig kidney could function in a human at all," eGenesis said in its announcement. Falco's year answers that question. The question now is how long it can function, and whether the answer is long enough to change medicine.

The waiting list: 95,492 people, 12 deaths a day

The reason Falco's story matters beyond her own life is the scale of the shortage it might one day address. As of July 30, 2026, 109,817 people were on the United States national transplant waiting list, and 95,492 of them — nearly nine in ten — were waiting for a kidney. Government statistics report that about 12 people die each day while waiting for an organ. A new person is added to the waiting list about every seven minutes.

The numbers have been getting worse, not better, even as transplant medicine has improved. American hospitals performed a record 49,064 transplants in 2025, the fifth record year in a row. Yet 74,579 new candidates joined the waiting list in 2025, while 47,201 people received a transplant — a gap of 27,378 that has widened from 20,699 in 2021. In 2024, 5,171 people died while on the waiting list, and another 6,342 were removed because they had become too sick for a transplant — about 31 people a day, combined, who left the list without receiving an organ.

The shortage exists because the supply of human organs is limited by definition. A deceased donor can provide at most two kidneys. A living donor can give one. The number of people willing and able to donate has never kept pace with the number of people whose kidneys fail — and kidney failure is common. More than 37 million American adults are estimated to have kidney disease, and about 90 percent of them do not know it. Diabetes and high blood pressure, the two leading causes of kidney failure, are rising. The waiting list grows by thousands of people a year, and it will keep growing.

That is the arithmetic that makes xenotransplantation so important. If pig kidneys could be produced on demand — if every patient who needed one could receive one within weeks, rather than waiting years — the waiting list would, in effect, disappear. No one would die waiting for a kidney, because there would be no wait. That is the promise Falco's case points toward, and it is why her one year matters far beyond Boston. It is also why her doctors are careful: one year is a milestone, not a cure, and the distance between one patient and a treatment for 95,492 people is measured in clinical trials, regulatory approvals, and years of further evidence.

What comes next: a clinical trial in January

The next step is already scheduled. In January, a formal clinical trial will begin testing pig kidneys made by eGenesis — the first trial of its kind, moving the treatment from individual patients treated under the FDA's Expanded Access pathway to a structured study with defined criteria, controls, and endpoints. The trial will enrol patients with end-stage kidney disease and track how long the organs function, what complications arise, and whether the results Falco achieved can be repeated across a larger group.

The trial is the bridge between an experiment and a medicine. Expanded Access cases like Falco's are granted one patient at a time, for people with no other option. A clinical trial is how a treatment is tested rigorously enough for the FDA to consider approving it for general use — and approval is what would make pig kidneys available not to a handful of patients at one hospital, but to anyone who needs one, anywhere in the country. eGenesis is also advancing programmes for liver and heart failure, using the same engineering platform, and the kidney trial's results will shape those programmes as well.

The trial will also answer the questions Falco's case raises but cannot settle alone. How long can a pig kidney last — two years? Five? Ten? What happens when the immunosuppressant drugs are taken for years rather than months? Do the organs develop problems over time that a single year does not reveal? And how do the results vary across patients of different ages, blood types, and medical histories? Falco is one patient, and one patient — even a record-breaking one — is not a proof. The trial is the proof, and it will take years.

For Falco herself, the future is simpler and more personal. She hopes Lily will keep working until a human donor kidney becomes available — a kidney that, unlike a pig's, would not require her to live with the uncertainty of an experiment. If no human kidney comes, she will return to dialysis. She knows that, and she has made her peace with it. But for now, she is living the life dialysis took from her: cooking, driving, seeing friends, and waking up each morning amazed that the kidney is still working. "I'm amazed every day," she said. A year ago, she did not know what the future would hold. Now she knows a little more — and so, because of her, does the whole of medicine.

Why one patient changes everything

Medical history is full of firsts that looked small at the time and enormous in retrospect. The first patient to survive a heart transplant lived 18 days, and heart transplantation is now routine. The first patients to receive insulin died within years of their treatment, and diabetes is now a manageable condition. Falco's one year with a pig kidney belongs in that company: not a cure, not a guarantee, but a proof that something long thought impossible can happen, and keep happening, day after day.

The proof matters because it changes what doctors, regulators, and companies are willing to try next. A one-year result justifies a clinical trial. A successful clinical trial justifies approval. Approval justifies manufacturing — pigs bred and engineered at scale, organs available when patients need them, a waiting list that shrinks instead of grows. Each step depends on the one before it, and Falco's year is the step that makes the next one possible.

It also matters because of who she is. Falco is not a statistic; she is a 58-year-old former chef from New Hampshire who said yes to an experiment before her doctor had finished asking, who named her new kidney after flowers from her family, and who measures her recovery in family meals cooked and friends seen and drives taken. The science of xenotransplantation is about genes and immune systems and retroviruses. The point of it is people like her — people who spent 11 months tethered to a machine, who were told they would wait five to seven years for a kidney, and who decided to try something no one had ever survived for a year. She did survive it. And because she did, the next 95,492 people on the waiting list have a reason to believe that their wait might one day end.
