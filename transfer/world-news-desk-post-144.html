TITLE: 2 American Airlines Jets Just Clipped Wings at Reagan Airport — 1 Winglet Torn Off, 0 Injured, FAA Investigating LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Two American Airlines Boeing 737s clipped wings while taxiing at Reagan National on Friday, tearing off a winglet. No one was hurt. ---BODY---

An American Airlines Boeing 737-800 on final approach. Two of the airline's 737-800s clipped wings while taxiing at Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport on Friday, 27 November 2026. Photo: CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Two American Airlines passenger jets clipped wings on the ground at Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport on Friday evening, tearing the winglet clean off one of the aircraft and grounding both planes at the height of the Thanksgiving travel weekend. Nobody was hurt. But the sight of a severed winglet lying on the taxiway, and the pilot's stunned radio call that the two jets had "swapped paint", has put one of America's busiest and most scrutinised airports back under the spotlight — less than two years after the deadliest American aviation disaster in a generation happened in the same sky.

American Airlines Flight 638, bound for Dallas-Fort Worth, was lining up for departure at around 5:10 p.m. Eastern time on Friday, 27 November, when its wing came into contact with American Airlines Flight 2152, bound for Chicago O'Hare, which was waiting next in line. Both aircraft were Boeing 737-800s. The Federal Aviation Administration confirmed the collision in a statement on Friday evening and said it would investigate. American Airlines said both jets had returned to the gate and had been removed from service for inspection by its maintenance teams.

"American Airlines Flight 638 clipped the wing of American Airlines Flight 2152 while taxiing for departure at Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport around 5:10 p.m. local time," the FAA said in its statement. No injuries were reported, the agency said. The two departures could not go ahead as planned, and the damaged aircraft were taken out of service while the airline rebooked the passengers.

The incident happened just off Runway 19, the longest runway at Reagan National and one of the busiest in the country, as the two jets waited side by side on the taxiway for their turn to depart. It was, by the standards of aviation accidents, a minor event: a low-speed brush on the ground, at taxiing speed, with no one hurt and no fire, no evacuation and no runway closure. But in an airport still living with the memory of January 2025, when 67 people died in a mid-air collision over the Potomac, even a minor brush between two airliners is news — and the FAA treats every one of them as a warning worth understanding.

"We swapped paint"

The most vivid account of what happened comes from the pilots themselves, captured on air traffic control audio. According to a recording of the radio calls captured by ATC.com and reported by CNN, the pilot of Flight 638 told the tower that his aircraft had drifted slightly past the hold-short line — the painted boundary that keeps taxiing aircraft clear of an active runway — and made contact with the jet beside him.

"Yes air, we are slightly over the hold short line and unfortunately we swapped paint with the American behind us, next to us," the pilot told controllers, in the recording reported by CNN. "Swapped paint" is pilots' understated shorthand for two aircraft touching: the paint from one jet transfers to the other. In this case, the contact was rather more forceful than the phrase suggests.

Another pilot's voice on the recording reported debris on the ground. "We got debris off our right… the whole winglet is there," one pilot told the tower, according to CNN. The winglet — the upturned fin at the very tip of a wing — had been sheared off one of the aircraft and was lying on the taxiway. Airport operations workers were sent out to recover the broken piece, and only once it had been collected were both aircraft able to taxi back to their gates under their own power.

The control tower, meanwhile, had to manage the wider airport. According to CNN, the tower told a different aircraft that was arriving at the airport to abort its landing and circle around, while the situation on the taxiway was dealt with. The airport said the incident did not disrupt other flights, although the two affected departures were, inevitably, not going anywhere that evening.

The two flights

Flight 638 was scheduled to fly from Reagan National to Dallas-Fort Worth International Airport. Flight 2152 was scheduled to fly to Chicago O'Hare International Airport. Both were operated by Boeing 737-800 aircraft, the workhorse of American's domestic fleet. According to the aviation news site Simple Flying, the two aircraft involved were registered N955NN, operating Flight 638, and N875NN, operating Flight 2152.

The 737-800 is one of the most common airliners in the world, and its dimensions help explain how two jets waiting side by side can touch. The aircraft has a wingspan of 117 feet 5 inches — about 35.8 metres — including its winglets. When two of them line up abreast on a taxiway, the margin for error between their wingtips is measured in feet, not yards. Pilots are expected to maintain situational awareness and to keep sufficient clearance from other aircraft, but on a crowded taxiway at dusk, in the middle of the busiest travel weekend of the year, that margin can disappear quickly.

The taxiway leading to Runway 19 at Reagan National is wide enough to accommodate two aircraft lined up side by side, an arrangement both jets were attempting on Friday, according to Simple Flying. The question the FAA investigation will have to answer is a simple one: why did the spacing fail this time? Was Flight 638 too far forward, past the hold-short line, as its pilot's own radio call suggests? Was the second aircraft positioned too close? Or did a combination of the two leave no room at all?

American Airlines confirmed in a statement that "the wingtips of both Boeing 737-800s made contact" while taxiing for departure. "There were no injuries and both aircraft returned to the gate and have been removed from service for inspection by our maintenance team," the airline said. The passengers on both flights were taken back to the terminal. The airline said the affected flights were scheduled to depart later on Friday evening, on replacement aircraft — although for many passengers, the evening's travel plans were already in disarray.

What is a winglet — and why does losing one matter?

To a passenger looking out of the window, a winglet is the small, upward-curving fin at the very end of an aircraft's wing. It looks decorative. It is not. Winglets are carefully engineered devices that reduce the drag created by the swirling air at the wingtip, improving fuel efficiency by several per cent on a typical flight. On a 737-800, they are a prominent feature, rising several feet above the wing.

Losing one is not, in itself, a catastrophe. An aircraft can fly safely without a winglet — the wing still generates lift, and the loss of one winglet does not make the aircraft unflyable. But no airline will dispatch a passenger jet with a winglet missing. The aircraft is no longer in the condition its certification requires, the aerodynamics are asymmetric, and the fuel burn and handling change. The jet must be inspected, the damage assessed, and the winglet replaced or repaired before it flies again. That is why both aircraft were immediately removed from service: not because they were in danger of falling out of the sky, but because the rules say a damaged airliner does not fly until it has been fixed and signed off.

The extent of the damage will also determine whether the National Transportation Safety Board, the independent agency that investigates American aviation accidents, becomes involved alongside the FAA. As Simple Flying reported, the FAA has confirmed it is investigating, "while the extent of the damage will determine whether the National Transportation Safety Board also becomes involved". The NTSB typically investigates accidents and serious incidents; a low-speed ground collision with no injuries and limited damage may remain an FAA matter, but the board can step in if the damage is judged substantial.

The hold-short line

The pilot's radio call put the focus on one detail: the hold-short line. Hold-short lines are the markings painted across a taxiway before it meets a runway. They consist of four yellow lines — two solid, two dashed — and they mark the point beyond which an aircraft must not go without explicit clearance from the tower. They exist to prevent runway incursions, the dangerous situation in which an aircraft strays onto an active runway while another is taking off or landing.

"We are slightly over the hold short line," the pilot of Flight 638 said. Slightly, in this case, was enough. The nose of his aircraft had crept past the line, and the wing — which extends far to the side of the fuselage, beyond the pilot's line of sight — had swung into the space occupied by the neighbouring jet. It is a reminder of a basic fact of flying a large airliner on the ground: the pilot sits at the front of a machine more than 35 metres wide, and cannot see its wingtips. Judging clearances on the ground is a matter of training, markings, and trust in the geometry of the taxiway — and when any of those fail, metal touches metal.

The FAA's investigation will examine the markings, the taxiway layout, the instructions the tower gave, and the positions of both aircraft. It will also look at whether the crews followed the correct procedures, and whether anything about the airport's layout or the evening's traffic contributed. Investigations of this kind typically take months, and the FAA does not usually publish a detailed public report on a ground collision of this scale — but the findings will feed into the safety data the agency uses to decide whether procedures or infrastructure need to change.

The second time in less than 20 months

Friday's collision was not the first of its kind at Reagan National. According to Simple Flying, it was "the second time in less than 20 months that two American-branded aircraft have collided while waiting to depart from DCA, following a similar wingtip collision in April 2025". That earlier incident, also involving two American Airlines aircraft waiting to depart, ended the same way: no injuries, damaged winglets, grounded aircraft, and an investigation.

Two similar events at the same airport in under two years is a pattern the FAA will not ignore. It raises an obvious question: is there something about the taxiway layout near Runway 19, or about the way departures are queued there, that makes wingtip contact more likely than it should be? The airport's taxiways were designed for an earlier generation of smaller aircraft, and Reagan National is famously constrained — hemmed in by the Potomac River on one side and by the city on the other, with little room to widen anything. The airport handles far more traffic than its 1940s footprint was ever intended to carry, and its taxiways are tight by modern standards.

That is not, by itself, an explanation. Thousands of aircraft taxi through Reagan National every week without touching each other. But when the same kind of incident repeats at the same place, investigators look hard at the place as well as the people. The FAA's inquiry will have to decide whether Friday was a one-off error by one crew, or a symptom of a layout that leaves too little room for error.

An airport living in the shadow of January 2025

Any incident at Reagan National is now read through the lens of 29 January 2025, when an American Airlines regional jet preparing to land collided in mid-air with a United States Army Black Hawk helicopter over the Potomac River, killing all 67 people aboard both aircraft. It was the deadliest aviation disaster in the United States in a generation, and it happened on final approach to the same airport where Friday's two jets touched wings on the ground.

The two events are very different in scale and in kind. January 2025 was a mid-air collision at night, in the landing pattern, involving a military helicopter and a passenger jet. Friday's was a low-speed brush between two airliners on a taxiway, with no one hurt. But they share an airport, an airline, and a public that is now acutely sensitive to anything that goes wrong at Reagan National. As Gulf News noted in its report on Friday's incident, the collision "has renewed attention on aviation safety at Reagan National Airport, where a deadly mid-air collision in January 2025 killed 67 people".

The January 2025 disaster led to months of investigation, hearings in Congress, and changes to the rules governing helicopter traffic around the airport. The NTSB's investigation into that crash is still the defining aviation safety story in the United States. Friday's incident will not be investigated on anything like the same scale, but it will be read, by the public and by the press, as a test of whether the lessons of that disaster have been learned — and whether the airport is safe.

It is worth stating plainly what the evidence shows. A ground collision in which two wingtips touch at taxiing speed is a common category of aviation incident worldwide. Aircraft touch each other on the ground more often than the public realises — at gates, on taxiways, on ramps — and the overwhelming majority of these events end with no injuries and modest damage. The aviation safety system records them, investigates them, and uses them to improve procedures precisely because they are the low-consequence warnings that can prevent high-consequence accidents. That is how the system is supposed to work. The question is whether the system is working at Reagan National, and the repeat of a similar event in less than 20 months is the reason the FAA will look closely.

Thanksgiving travel chaos

Friday's collision came at the worst possible moment for the passengers involved — and at a moment of extraordinary strain for the American aviation system as a whole. The Thanksgiving holiday is the busiest travel period of the year in the United States, and this year it coincided with a powerful storm system that battered the East Coast. According to AAA, roughly 6 million Americans were projected to fly domestically during the Thanksgiving holiday travel period, between 24 and 30 November.

The storm had already caused havoc. As reported in the days before, the nor'easter that swept the East Coast knocked out power to nearly a million homes, flooded coastal towns in New Jersey, and forced the postponement of the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade in New York for the first time in its 100-year history. Flights were cancelled and delayed across the region, and by Friday evening, as millions of Americans began the journey home, the system was running at full stretch — full aircraft, crowded terminals, and crews working long days.

That context does not explain a wingtip collision. The investigation will focus on the positions of the two aircraft and the decisions of the crews, not on the weather or the holiday. But it is part of the story all the same, because it explains why the incident mattered to so many people beyond the two crews: hundreds of passengers, already facing delays and cancellations, had their Friday evening travel plans thrown into further disarray, and had to be rebooked onto later flights at the busiest time of the year.

For the passengers on Flight 638 and Flight 2152, the evening ended not in Dallas or Chicago but back at the gate at Reagan National, collecting their bags and joining the queues to be rebooked. American Airlines said the affected flights were scheduled to depart later on Friday evening, on other aircraft. Some passengers will have made it home that night. Others will not have done — and for them, a few feet of misjudged clearance on a taxiway became the reason Thanksgiving weekend ended a day late.

What happens next

The immediate next steps are procedural, and they are already under way. Both aircraft have been removed from service and will be inspected by American's maintenance teams. The damage to the winglet — and to the wing structure beneath it — will be assessed. If the damage is limited to the winglet itself, the repair is a matter of replacing a component: winglets are designed to be removable, and a replacement can be fitted in a maintenance hangar in a matter of days. If the impact damaged the wing structure, the repair will be longer and more complex.

The FAA's investigation will run in parallel. Investigators will interview both flight crews and the air traffic controllers on duty, review the radio recordings and the radar and ground-movement data, and examine the taxiway markings and the positions of the aircraft. They will also look at whether the crews had received the correct taxi instructions, and whether anything about the traffic flow that evening contributed to the two aircraft ending up so close together.

The longer question is whether anything changes at Reagan National as a result. After the April 2025 wingtip collision, the airport and the FAA evidently did not change the taxiway layout or the departure queueing procedures enough to prevent a repeat. If Friday's investigation reaches the same conclusion as the last one — that the crews misjudged the clearance — then the fix will be a matter of training and reminders. If it finds that the layout itself leaves too little margin, then the airport faces a harder problem, because widening a taxiway at one of America's most constrained airports is neither quick nor cheap.

For now, the airport is operating normally, the two aircraft are in the hangar, and the FAA is investigating. The passengers have been rebooked. And the winglet that was torn off on Friday evening — the "whole winglet", as the pilot put it on the radio — has been recovered from the taxiway, and will be examined as evidence of exactly how hard two airliners have to touch before the metal gives way.

Why this matters

It would be easy to dismiss Friday's incident as a non-event: no one was hurt, no one was in danger, and the aircraft were barely moving. That would be a mistake, for two reasons. First, because the aviation safety system is built on the principle that small incidents are warnings, and that the time to learn from them is before they become large ones. A wingtip collision on the ground is a warning. A mid-air collision is a catastrophe. The distance between the two is not as great as it looks, and the industry knows it.

Second, because public confidence in aviation safety is itself a safety issue. After January 2025, every incident at Reagan National is watched closely, and every incident that is not explained clearly feeds the anxiety that the system is not under control. The FAA and American Airlines have a duty, in this case as in every case, to explain what happened, why it happened, and what will change so that it does not happen again. The facts, so far, are reassuring: a low-speed brush, no injuries, both aircraft safely back at the gate. The repeat, in under 20 months, is the part that is not reassuring — and it is the part the investigation must address.

For the millions of Americans who flew this Thanksgiving weekend, the lesson is a familiar one: air travel remains, by any statistical measure, extraordinarily safe, and the system caught this incident exactly as it was designed to — on the ground, at low speed, with no one hurt. But safety is not a statistic. It is a practice, repeated every day, on every taxiway, by every crew. On Friday evening at Reagan National, that practice failed by a few feet. The investigation will say by how many, and why.
