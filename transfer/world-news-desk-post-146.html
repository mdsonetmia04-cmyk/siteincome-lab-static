TITLE: Andrea Stewart-Cousins Is Dead at 76 — the First Woman to Lead New York's Senate, Found After a Wellness Check LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: New York Senate Majority Leader Andrea Stewart-Cousins has died at 76. She was the first woman and first Black woman to lead the chamber. ---BODY---

Andrea Stewart-Cousins in 2020. The New York Senate Majority Leader has died at the age of 76. Photo: New York State Senate, via Wikimedia Commons.

Andrea Stewart-Cousins, the woman who broke the highest glass ceiling in New York politics and then held the gavel longer than any Democratic majority leader in the history of the State Senate, is dead at 76. She was found dead in her apartment in Yonkers on Friday afternoon, after her daughter tried and failed to reach her since Thursday night and called for a wellness check. Police found no signs of foul play. No cause of death has been announced. She had been re-elected as majority leader less than two weeks earlier.

The announcement came on Friday, 27 November, from the Senate majority's communications director, Mike Murphy. "It is with profound sadness that I announce the passing of New York State Senate Temporary President and Majority Leader Andrea Stewart-Cousins, who died on November 27, 2026, at the age of 76," Murphy said in a statement. "Serving the people of the 35th Senate District was one of the greatest honours of her life. Throughout her decades in public service, Stewart-Cousins made a profound impact on the lives of New Yorkers. She was a groundbreaking leader who shattered one of New York's highest glass ceilings, becoming the first woman to lead the New York State Senate."

She was, by every measure, a historic figure. The first woman — and the first Black woman — to lead the New York State Senate. The first woman to lead a conference in the New York State Legislature. The first Black woman to serve as New York's lieutenant governor. The longest-serving Democratic majority leader in the Senate's history. A state senator for two decades, representing Yonkers and the Westchester County suburbs. A woman who began her life in public housing in Manhattan and the Bronx, became a single parent at 19, and rose, step by step, to the second most powerful office in New York State.

Her death, sudden and unexplained, has stunned Albany. Tributes poured in within hours from the governor, from the Speaker-designate of the United States House of Representatives, from the Speaker of the State Assembly, and from colleagues across the political spectrum. The Senate she led for nearly eight years must now choose a successor. The district she represented for twenty years is now vacant. And the session that begins in January — the session she was re-elected to lead — will open without her.

Found on Friday afternoon

The circumstances of her death are, so far, simple and sad. According to the New York Post, citing Yonkers police and law enforcement sources, Stewart-Cousins was found dead in her Yonkers apartment on Friday afternoon, after her daughter tried and failed to get in touch with her since Thursday night and called for a wellness check. Yonkers Police Commissioner Christopher Sapienza said her cause of death was not immediately known, but investigators found "no signs of foul play or unusual circumstances".

Wikipedia's account, updated on Friday, records that she died on 27 November 2026 at her home in Yonkers, aged 76, and that her body was discovered after police conducted a wellness check following a request by a family member. Some reports, including People magazine, gave the date of death as Thursday, 26 November — the day her daughter last heard from her. The Senate's official statement gives 27 November. What is certain is that she was found on Friday, that she was 76, and that her death was sudden and unexpected.

The suddenness is part of the shock. Less than two weeks before her death, she had been unanimously re-elected as Senate majority leader, to lead one of the largest Democratic conferences in state history into the new session in January. She was, by all accounts, active and engaged. There had been no public announcement of illness. Her colleagues in Albany learned of her death the way the public did: from a statement, on a Friday afternoon, in the middle of the Thanksgiving holiday weekend.

Governor Kathy Hochul confirmed the death on X, the platform formerly known as Twitter, and the tributes began within the hour. The Assembly Speaker, Carl Heastie, said he was "heartbroken to learn of the passing of my good friend and partner in government". The Speaker-designate of the House, Hakeem Jeffries, called her "indomitable". The words that recurred, in statement after statement, were "trailblazer", "groundbreaking", and "first". She was all of those things. But she was also, to the people who worked with her, a colleague, a mentor, and a friend — and the grief in Albany on Friday was personal as well as political.

From public housing to the gavel

Andrea Alice Stewart was born on 2 September 1950 in New York City. She was the daughter of Bob Stewart, a decorated Second World War veteran and repairman, and Beryl Stewart, a stenographer and community activist. The family lived in public housing in Manhattan and the Bronx, and Andrea suffered from chronic asthma as a child. She became a single parent at the age of 19. She married Thomas Cousins in 1979. He died on 26 November 2007 — nineteen years to the day, almost, before her own death. They had three children. She had four grandchildren.

Her path to politics was not the usual one. She spent twenty years working in the private sector, including thirteen years in sales and marketing with New York Telephone. After the company was acquired through a merger with NYNEX in 1984, she received a buyout and pursued a college degree while working for the Gannett newspaper, the Herald Statesman. She earned her Bachelor of Science degree from Pace University in 1986 and her teaching credentials in business education from Lehman College. She received her Master of Public Administration degree from Pace University in May 2008, and was a member of Pi Alpha Alpha, the public administration honour society.

She entered public service in 1992, when she was appointed Director of Community Affairs for the City of Yonkers during the mayoralty of Terence Zaleski. In that role, she created an internship programme for the hearing-impaired and for children in working families, and was a founder of the original "Art on Main Street" and a co-creator of "River Fest", a widely attended multicultural citywide celebration on the Hudson River in Yonkers.

She served as a Westchester County legislator from 1996 to 2006, rising to majority whip and vice-chair. In that decade, she authored living wage laws, smoke-free workplace laws, tougher gun laws, laws that prosecute predatory lenders, tax cuts for seniors and veterans, and Westchester County's first human rights laws. It was a record that marked her out as a legislator who got things done — and as a politician who understood that the laws that matter most are often the ones that never make the front page.

The eighteen-vote defeat — and the comeback

She first ran for the New York State Senate in 2004. The incumbent Republican, Nicholas Spano, defeated her by a margin of eighteen votes — eighteen, out of more than 114,000 cast. It was one of the closest elections in the history of the New York Senate, and it could have ended her political career. Instead, it made it. She challenged Spano again in 2006, and this time she defeated him. She took her seat in the Senate on 1 January 2007, and she held it for the next twenty years.

On 17 December 2012, she was elected Senate Democratic Leader. She was the first woman in history to lead a conference in the New York State Legislature. At the time, the Democrats were in the minority, and the Senate was controlled by a Republican-led coalition. She led the conference through six years in the minority, building the organisation, recruiting the candidates, and waiting for the moment when the numbers would turn. That moment came in the 2018 elections, when the Democratic Party won a Senate majority. On 9 January 2019, she was elected Senate Majority Leader — the first woman to hold the post in New York history.

The 2018 victory ended a political arrangement that had defined Albany for a generation: the "three men in a room" — the governor, the Assembly Speaker, and the Senate Majority Leader, all men — who negotiated the state budget behind closed doors. As the New York Post noted in its obituary, Stewart-Cousins "broke up the 'three men in a room' budget negotiations that Albany was long reviled for". She was not just the first woman to hold the gavel. She was the woman who ended the era in which the gavel was always held by a man.

The majority leader

Her first year as majority leader was one of the most productive legislative sessions in New York's modern history. In 2019, she sponsored the Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act, which overhauled the rules governing rent-controlled apartments in New York City. And under her leadership, the Senate passed a sweeping package of progressive laws: on climate change, voting rights, abortion rights, criminal justice reform, gender equality, gun control, marijuana decriminalisation, LGBT rights, and immigration.

Her style was distinctive. According to City & State New York, she employed a "consensus-driven approach" to leading the Senate Democratic Conference that set her "apart from her predecessors". She was, by reputation, a listener — a leader who built agreement rather than imposing it, and who understood that a majority of 40-odd senators, with different districts and different politics, could only be held together by patience and by trust. In 2019, she was named to Crain's New York Business biennial list of the "Most Powerful Women in New York".

She also, twice, became the state's Acting Lieutenant Governor. When Governor Andrew Cuomo resigned in 2021, and Lieutenant Governor Kathy Hochul succeeded him as governor, Stewart-Cousins — as Senate Majority Leader — became Acting Lieutenant Governor until Hochul appointed a replacement. She was the first Black woman to serve in the role, and the first time New York was governed by both a female governor and a female lieutenant governor. When the replacement, Brian Benjamin, resigned in April 2022 after being arrested in a corruption scandal, she assumed the role again, serving from 12 April to 25 May 2022, until Antonio Delgado was sworn in.

She was, in short, the most powerful Democrat in the State Senate, the longest-serving Democratic majority leader in the chamber's history, and a figure of national significance. She was elected chair of the Democratic Legislative Campaign Committee board in 2019, boosting her national profile. And she was, to the end, a working legislator — re-elected as majority leader less than two weeks before her death, to lead the conference into the session that begins in January.

The tributes

The tributes that followed the announcement were immediate, and they were striking in their breadth. Governor Kathy Hochul confirmed the death on social media. Assembly Speaker Carl Heastie said: "I am heartbroken to learn of the passing of my good friend and partner in government Senate Majority Leader Andrea Stewart-Cousins. Andrea was a trailblazer — the first woman and the first Black woman to hold the gavel as majority leader in the Senate." He added: "She led the Senate into the majority in 2019 and her impact was immediate. I am devastated that she won't be there fighting side by side with me as we head into our new session in January."

Speaker-designate Hakeem Jeffries, the leader of the House Democrats, said: "I am incredibly saddened to learn of the passing of my good friend, the indomitable Senator Andrea Stewart-Cousins. She broke barriers as the first woman to lead a conference in the New York State Legislature, the first woman to serve as Senate Majority Leader and the longest-serving Democratic Majority Leader in New York State history. May Leader Stewart-Cousins forever rest in power."

State Senator Lea Webb, who called Stewart-Cousins a mentor, said: "I am absolutely heartbroken and devastated about the loss of my Leader Senator Andrea Stewart Cousins. She was a mentor, a great friend, and so much more to me and to many others. A trailblazer for women in the halls of government, she led our conference every day with wisdom, extraordinary strength, kindness, generosity, grace, and a true dedication to bettering the lives of others."

Even the Assembly's Republican minority leader, Ed Ra, paid tribute, saying: "As the first African American woman to lead a legislative conference in Albany, Sen. Stewart-Cousins left an indelible mark on New York State and set a historic standard for others to emulate. While our policy perspectives differed, I respected her professionalism, thoughtfulness and the historic achievements that defined her career."

The praise crossed party lines because the achievement did. Stewart-Cousins was a Democrat, and a progressive one, but the barriers she broke — the first woman, the first Black woman — belonged to no party. They belonged to the state. And the respect she commanded in Albany was not a matter of ideology. It was a matter of character: of the patience, the preparation, and the steadiness that had carried her from public housing to the gavel.

What happens now

Her death leaves two vacancies. The first is her Senate seat, the 35th District, which she held for twenty years. The seat is now vacant, and a special election will be needed to fill it — a process that the governor will set in motion in the coming weeks. The district covers Yonkers and parts of Westchester County, and the contest to succeed her will be one of the most closely watched local races in the state.

The second vacancy is the majority leadership itself. Her deputy was Michael Gianaris, and the Senate Democratic Conference will have to elect a new leader — a choice that will shape the politics of Albany for years. The conference she built is large and, by New York standards, disciplined, but the succession will be contested. The new leader will take office in a Senate that Stewart-Cousins remade, and will inherit both her achievements and her challenges: a housing crisis, a budget under pressure, and a session that begins in January with a new governor's agenda to consider.

The arrangements for her funeral have not yet been announced. The Senate has not yet said whether there will be a lying in state at the Capitol in Albany, the honour traditionally given to the state's most senior leaders. What is certain is that the tributes will continue, and that the state will mark her passing in the formal ways it reserves for those who have shaped its history. She shaped it. She was the first woman to lead the Senate, and she led it for nearly eight years. That is not a footnote. It is a chapter.

Why she mattered

It is tempting, in writing about a political leader, to reduce a life to a list of firsts. Stewart-Cousins had many of them: the first woman to lead a conference in the Legislature, the first woman to be Senate Majority Leader, the first Black woman to be lieutenant governor, the longest-serving Democratic majority leader in Senate history. The list is impressive, and it is accurate. But it does not capture why she mattered, and it does not explain why the grief in Albany on Friday was so raw.

She mattered because she changed what power looked like in New York. For two centuries, the Senate had been led by men. The budget had been negotiated by three men in a room. The idea that a Black woman from public housing in the Bronx — a single mother at 19, a telephone company saleswoman, a Yonkers community affairs director — could hold the gavel was, for most of that history, unthinkable. She made it thinkable, and then she made it normal. The women who follow her into the Senate will not have to be first. They will only have to be good. That is the difference she made, and it is the difference that will outlast her.

She mattered, too, because of how she led. The consensus-driven style that City & State praised was not a softness. It was a discipline: the discipline of listening, of building agreement, of holding a fractious majority together through patience rather than force. In an age of political shouting, she led by talking quietly and by working hard. Her colleagues trusted her because she had earned their trust, over twenty years, one vote at a time.

And she mattered because she never forgot where she came from. The living wage laws, the smoke-free workplace laws, the tax cuts for seniors and veterans, the housing protections — these were not abstract policies. They were the laws of a woman who had lived in public housing, who had raised a child alone at 19, who had worked in sales and marketing and known what it was to live on a wage. Her politics were the politics of her life, and her life was the life of millions of New Yorkers who never get to hold the gavel.

Andrea Stewart-Cousins died on 27 November 2026, at the age of 76, in the apartment in Yonkers where she lived, two weeks after being re-elected to lead the Senate she had already led for nearly eight years. She leaves three children, four grandchildren, a vacant seat, a leaderless conference, and a state that is poorer for her absence. She also leaves a record that will not be forgotten: the first woman to lead the New York State Senate, and the leader who showed the state what that could mean.
