TITLE: 13 Dead, 13 Still Trapped 100 Metres Underground — the Flood That Swallowed Indonesia's Illegal Gold Mine LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Thirteen miners are dead and 13 more are trapped after a river flooded an illegal gold mine in Bogor, Indonesia — rescuers were told 16 hours late. ---BODY---

A river and forest in Bogor, West Java, Indonesia. In the Pongkor area of Bogor regency, 26 miners were trapped on Monday when the Ciseureuh River overflowed and flooded a gold mine shaft about 100 metres deep. Photo: Mohd Fahmi Mohd Azmi via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0.

Thirteen men are dead. Thirteen more are still underground. And the rescue teams working in the dark, in the mud, on a mountainside in West Java, are racing a clock that started ticking on Monday — and that nobody in authority even knew about for 16 hours. On Thursday, November 26, 2026, Indonesian rescuers confirmed that they had recovered the bodies of 13 miners from a flooded illegal gold mine in the Pongkor area of Bogor regency, and that 13 more were still believed to be trapped about 100 metres underground, in shafts filled with water, in darkness, in terrain so steep and slippery that every step is a risk.

The mine is in Bogor, a mountainous area 60 kilometres south of Jakarta, the Indonesian capital, where the state miner Antam runs gold-mining operations. The shaft the men were working in was not part of those operations. It was an unauthorised mine — dug without a licence, worked without oversight, and invisible to the authorities until it was too late. On Monday afternoon, 26 workers entered the excavation pit, near a river in Bogor regency. Heavy rain later that day caused the river to overflow and flood the pit. Several miners managed to escape from one section of the excavation before the floodwaters filled the shaft. The rest did not.

The river was the Ciseureuh, and the rain was torrential. It turned a narrow, hand-dug shaft — estimated to be about 100 metres, or 330 feet, deep — into a trap. The water came in faster than the men could climb out, and by the time the flooding was done, 26 men were underground and the mine was full of water. One of the dead was Rais, 50, who had been working as an illegal gold miner in Bogor. His story, like the stories of the other 25, is the story of men who went underground for gold and did not come back up.

Sixteen hours in the dark

What makes this disaster different from an ordinary mining accident — and what makes it, in many ways, worse — is how long it took anyone in authority to learn it had happened. The authorities said they were not immediately notified, because the miners were operating illegally, and local miners and residents initially tried to pump the water from the site themselves before the situation became unmanageable. Police first received reports of trapped miners about 16 hours after the flooding occurred. It was only shortly after midnight on Wednesday — hours after the water had filled the shafts — that the nearby state-owned mining company, PT Antam, forwarded a formal report to the Jakarta Search and Rescue Agency, triggering a full-scale rescue operation.

Sixteen hours. In a flooded mine, sixteen hours is the difference between a rescue and a recovery. The men underground had no air supply, no communication, no way of knowing whether anyone above even knew they were there. The people on the surface, meanwhile, were pumping water with whatever equipment they had, in the dark, in the rain, without the pumps, the ropes, the lights or the training that a professional rescue team brings. By the time the professionals arrived, the water had had a night to settle, and the odds had already narrowed.

Desiana Kartika Bahari, the chief of the Jakarta Search and Rescue Agency, confirmed the death toll in a statement on Thursday, as rescuers battled high water, darkness and slippery, narrow terrain in search of the missing workers. On Thursday, the search operation involved 325 personnel, focused on two adjacent mine shafts, using rope-access techniques to reach the victims. The team used submersible water pumps to suck water from the hole and tried to close off openings where groundwater seeped in. It is slow, dangerous work: the shafts are narrow, the ground is unstable, and the water keeps coming back.

Hengki, West Java's police chief, told Reuters that of the 13 fatalities, six had already been identified. The search would continue on Thursday, he said, barring bad weather conditions. The rain, which caused the disaster, remains the greatest threat to the rescue: more rain means more water in the shafts, more unstable ground on the slopes, and more danger for the 325 people working on the mountainside.

The families waiting on the mountain

For the families, the wait is measured in hours that feel like days. Rani, identified in footage from the scene as the daughter of a missing miner, wept as she expressed hope that her father would be found alive. He had insisted on going to the mine, she explained, despite her mother's objections, because he wanted to work with his friends instead of harvesting fruit at home. It is a detail that captures the whole economy of the illegal mines in a single sentence: the choice, for a man in rural West Java, between the certain, small income of farm work and the uncertain, potentially large income of gold.

The rescue scene, captured in footage released by Indonesia's National Search and Rescue Agency and broadcast around the world, shows the scale of the operation and the difficulty of the terrain. Rescuers in orange uniforms and helmets with headlamps carry orange body bags through muddy terrain in the dark. A drone view shows ambulances parked near the mining site, waiting at the end of a track that winds through dense forest. Night-time video shows rescue teams working with ropes and lights, in rain and mud, around a shaft that is little more than a hole in the ground. One handout photograph, released by the National Search and Rescue Agency on November 25, shows rescue workers and local volunteers carrying a body bag containing the remains of a victim recovered from the flooded mine in Nanggung, Bogor district.

The bodies that have been recovered were brought out in the dark, in the rain, by men working with ropes on a slope that is itself a hazard. The 13 who are still missing are, in the words of the rescue agency, "believed trapped" — a phrase that holds open the possibility of life while acknowledging how slim it has become. Every hour that passes in a flooded shaft makes survival less likely. The rescue teams know this, and they work anyway, because the alternative is to stop, and no one on that mountain is ready to stop.

Why men dig for gold without a licence

To understand why 26 men were in an unlicensed shaft on a rainy Monday, you have to understand the gold rush that has swept Indonesia — and the price of gold that drives it. In early 2026, gold prices reached an all-time high, spurring a gold rush that prompted mine operators across the country to expand their operations. When the price of gold rises, the hills of West Java fill with men carrying picks and shovels, digging where the geology says gold might be, with or without a piece of paper from Jakarta.

Illegal small-scale mining is a persistent problem in Indonesia, and it is one the authorities have struggled to control for years. The mines are small, remote, and numerous; they appear in forests and on mountainsides, far from the roads and the inspectors. They are worked by men who have no other way to earn the money that gold promises, and they are worked without the safety measures that licensed mines are required to have: without ventilation, without reinforced shafts, without emergency plans, and without anyone to call when the water comes in.

The Pongkor area, where this mine was dug, is home to a legitimate, state-run gold mining operation — PT Antam's Pongkor gold mining unit — and the illegal shaft was dug near its facilities. Antam said the incident was unrelated to its operations, and that the mining where the incident occurred was not part of its official operations. The distinction matters legally, but on the mountainside it is almost invisible: the same geology, the same gold, the same men, divided only by a licence that one operation holds and the other does not.

The economics are stark. A licensed mine employs workers, pays taxes, follows safety rules, and answers to inspectors. An illegal mine employs whoever turns up, pays no taxes, follows no safety rules, and answers to no one. The men who work in them are, in most cases, not criminals in any meaningful sense: they are farmers, labourers and fathers, drawn by the price of gold and the promise of a better income than the fields can offer. Rais, 50, was one of them. The daughter of another, Rani, described a father who chose the mine over harvesting fruit at home — not out of recklessness, but out of hope.

A country that keeps burying its miners

Indonesia has buried illegal miners before, and it will, in all likelihood, bury them again. The archipelago's geology is rich in gold, and its regulation of small-scale mining is thin, underfunded and unevenly enforced. The mines are often in remote areas where the state's reach is weakest, and the men who work them are often among the poorest in the country. When a shaft floods, or a tunnel collapses, or a landslide buries a mountainside, the same pattern repeats: a delay before the authorities are told, a rescue hampered by terrain and weather, and a death toll that is counted in the teens or the dozens.

The Bogor disaster follows that pattern with terrible precision. The flooding came from a river, swollen by torrential rain — a hazard that is not unusual in West Java in the rainy season, and that a licensed operation would be required to plan for. The delay in notification came from the mine's illegality — a hazard that is built into the very nature of unlicensed work. The rescue is hampered by high water, darkness and slippery, narrow terrain — the conditions of a mountainside that was never meant to be a workplace. Every element of the disaster was, in a sense, foreseeable. None of it was prevented.

There is a broader question here, and it is one that Indonesia has been wrestling with for years: what to do about the illegal mines. Cracking down is difficult, because the mines are remote and the miners are poor, and because enforcement in the forests of West Java requires resources the state does not always have. Formalising the mines — bringing them into the licensed system, with safety rules and oversight — is the solution that experts most often propose, and it is also the hardest to deliver, because it requires the state to reach into places it has never effectively governed. In the meantime, the men keep digging, and the mountains keep taking them.

What happens next on the mountain

On Thursday, the search continued. The 325-person team, working with submersible pumps and rope-access techniques, focused on the two adjacent shafts where the men are believed to be trapped. The operation is, in the words of the Jakarta Search and Rescue Agency, a race against time — and it is a race that the rescuers are losing with every hour that passes. The water must be pumped out faster than it seeps back in. The shafts must be made safe enough for rescuers to enter. And the weather must hold, because more rain would undo what the pumps have achieved.

The six identified dead have been named to their families. The seven others await identification. The 13 missing remain, for now, missing — a word that, in the language of disaster, is both a fact and a hope. Their families wait on the mountain, in the rain, for news that grows less likely with every hour. Rani waits for her father, who chose the mine over the fruit harvest, and who has not come home.

The disaster in Bogor is, in the end, a story about gold and about water, and about the distance between the two. The gold is 100 metres down, in a shaft dug by hand, in a mountain 60 kilometres from Jakarta. The water came from a river, swollen by rain, in a matter of hours. The men who went down for the gold are still there, and the men on the surface are pumping water and praying that the mountain gives them back. Thirteen are dead. Thirteen are missing. And the rain, in West Java, has not yet stopped.

The rescue, hour by hour

The timeline of the Bogor disaster is, in itself, an indictment of the system that allowed it to happen. It begins on Monday afternoon, when 26 workers entered the excavation pit near the Ciseureuh River in Bogor regency. The pit was an open excavation, about 100 metres deep, with two shafts. The men were digging for gold — grains of it, in the rock and the soil — working in narrow tunnels that had been cut by hand, without the reinforcement, the ventilation or the emergency exits that a licensed mine would have.

Later on Monday, the rain came. It was torrential, the kind of downpour that West Java sees in the rainy season, and it swelled the Ciseureuh River until it overflowed its banks and poured into the pit. The water filled the shafts fast. Several miners managed to escape from one section of the excavation before the floodwaters filled the shaft, scrambling out while they still could. The other 26 — or the 26 who had entered, minus those who escaped — were trapped underground, in the dark, in water that was still rising.

What happened next is the part of the story that rescuers and officials keep returning to. The local miners and residents, realising what had happened, tried to pump the water from the site themselves. They worked with whatever equipment they had, for hours, without telling the authorities — because the mine was illegal, and because telling the authorities meant admitting to the illegal work. It was only about 16 hours after the flooding that the police first received reports of trapped miners. And it was only shortly after midnight on Wednesday that PT Antam, the state-owned company whose legal Pongkor operation sits nearby, forwarded a formal report to the Jakarta Search and Rescue Agency. By then, the men had been underground for the better part of a day and a night.

Once the formal report arrived, the response was swift and large. The Jakarta Search and Rescue Agency — known as Basarnas — mobilised a full-scale operation, and by Thursday morning the search involved 325 personnel. The team brought submersible water pumps, the heavy equipment needed to suck water from a 100-metre hole faster than it seeps back in, and rope-access specialists, the climbers trained to descend into narrow shafts and work in confined spaces. They focused on two adjacent mine shafts, using ropes to reach the victims, and they tried to close off the openings where groundwater seeped in — a constant battle, because a flooded mine is not a bathtub that can be emptied once; it is a hole in water-bearing ground, and the water keeps coming.

On Thursday morning, the team recovered the bodies of 13 miners. The operation continued through the day, with the search for the remaining 13 going on into the afternoon and the evening. Desiana Kartika Bahari, the rescue agency chief, said the teams were battling high water, darkness and slippery, narrow terrain — three conditions that make every part of the work slower and more dangerous. The high water means the pumps must run constantly. The darkness means the work must be done by headlamp, in shafts where a slip can be fatal. The slippery, narrow terrain means that even reaching the shaft is a climb, and carrying a body out is a climb with a stretcher.

The photographs released by Basarnas on November 25 show what that looks like: rescuers in orange uniforms and helmets with headlamps, carrying orange body bags through muddy terrain at night, their lights cutting through the dark and the rain. A drone view shows ambulances parked near the site, waiting at the end of a track through dense forest. It is an operation conducted in the worst possible conditions, by people who know that the chances of finding anyone alive are shrinking with every hour — and who keep working anyway.

Gold's record year — and the rush it unleashed

The reason 26 men were in that shaft on a rainy Monday is written in the price of gold. In early 2026, gold prices reached an all-time high, and the surge set off a gold rush across Indonesia. Mine operators expanded their operations, new pits were dug, and men who had never mined before picked up shovels and headed for the hills. When gold is at a record price, the arithmetic of the illegal mine becomes irresistible: a few grams of gold, found in a day's digging, can be worth more than a week's wages in the fields.

The Pongkor area, where the disaster happened, is gold country. It is a mountainous region 60 kilometres south of Jakarta, where the state miner Antam runs a legitimate gold-mining operation — the Pongkor unit — and where the geology has drawn miners for decades. The illegal shaft was dug near Antam's facilities, in the same gold-bearing ground, but outside the company's licence and outside its safety regime. Antam was quick to say that the incident was unrelated to its operations, and that the mining where it occurred was not part of its official operations. That is, in a legal sense, true. In a human sense, the distinction is thinner: the same mountain, the same gold, the same men — divided by a boundary that exists on a map in Jakarta and nowhere else.

The gold rush of 2026 is not the first Indonesia has seen, and it will not be the last. Gold prices rise when the world is uncertain — when wars, inflation and financial turmoil drive investors towards the metal that has held its value for thousands of years — and when prices rise, the hills fill with diggers. The pattern is the same in country after country: a price spike, a rush, a wave of small, unlicensed mines, and then, sooner or later, a disaster. The only question is where, and how many die.

For the men who dig, the calculation is brutally simple. Legal mining jobs are scarce, and they go to those with connections, qualifications or luck. Farm work — harvesting fruit, tending rice — pays little and is seasonal. The illegal mine pays in gold, and gold pays in cash, on the day. Rani's father chose the mine over the fruit harvest not because he was reckless, but because the mine offered what the harvest could not: a chance, however slim, of a genuinely better income. That is the calculation that puts men 100 metres underground in a hand-dug shaft, in the rainy season, with no safety equipment and no one to call for help.

Indonesia's illegal mines: a problem without an easy answer

The disaster in Bogor is not an isolated tragedy. It is the latest episode in a problem that Indonesia has lived with for years: a vast, decentralised, largely unregulated small-scale mining sector, operating in some of the most remote and difficult terrain in the archipelago, beyond the effective reach of the state. The mines are small, numerous and mobile — a shaft here, a pit there, abandoned when the gold runs out and dug somewhere else when it does not. They are almost impossible to police in the conventional sense, because there are too many of them, in too many places, and the people who work them have every reason not to be found.

The dangers are not limited to flooding. Illegal mines in Indonesia have collapsed, caught fire, and been buried by landslides. The shafts are dug without engineering, the tunnels without support, the slopes without stabilisation. The miners work without helmets, without ventilation, without any of the equipment that licensed mines are required to provide — and when something goes wrong, there is no alarm, no emergency plan, and, as Bogor showed, often no call for help for hours. The 16-hour delay in this case was not an aberration. It is the normal consequence of illegality: the first instinct of people working outside the law is not to call the law.

What, then, is to be done? The experts who study the sector tend to give the same answer: formalisation. Bring the small mines into the licensed system, give the miners legal status, require basic safety measures, and provide the oversight that currently does not exist. It is an answer that sounds simple and is, in practice, enormously difficult. Formalisation requires the state to reach into remote forests and mountainsides, to survey the land, to issue licences, to inspect the mines, and to enforce the rules — all of which require money, personnel and political will that have, so far, been in short supply. It also requires the miners themselves to want to be formalised, which means the licensed system must offer them something better than the unlicensed one: not just rules, but access to credit, to equipment, and to fair prices for their gold.

In the meantime, the enforcement that does exist tends to be sporadic — a crackdown here, a closure there — and the mines reappear as soon as the inspectors leave. The price of gold remains high, the hills remain full of it, and the men remain willing to dig. The disaster in Bogor will, in all likelihood, prompt statements from officials, promises of action, and perhaps a crackdown in the Pongkor area. Whether it prompts the sustained, expensive, difficult work of formalisation is another question — and it is a question that will be answered not in the days after the disaster, but in the months and years that follow, when the cameras have gone and the mountain is quiet again.

For now, the mountain is not quiet. The pumps are running, the ropes are rigged, and 325 people are working in the rain and the dark, trying to bring 13 men home. Thirteen are already dead. Thirteen are still missing. And the gold, 100 metres down, is still there — waiting, as it always has, for the next men willing to dig for it.
