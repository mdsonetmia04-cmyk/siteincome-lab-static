The Nasdaq MarketSite in Times Square, New York. On Wednesday, November 25, the technology-heavy Nasdaq Composite fell as much as 4.15% in a single session, and the Nasdaq 100 briefly touched bear-market territory. Photo: ajay_suresh, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Wall Street had its ugliest day in months on Wednesday, November 25, and the numbers were the kind that make even seasoned traders sit up straight. The Nasdaq Composite, the technology-heavy index that has carried the American stock market for three years, fell 4.15% in morning trading, shedding 985.93 points to 22,747.61, and at its worst it was down 4.7% on the day. The Nasdaq 100, the index of the hundred largest non-financial companies on the exchange, briefly fell 20% from its record closing high, which is the formal definition of a bear market. The S&P 500 fell as much as 3.21%, sinking below the psychologically important 7,000 level for the first time in the sell-off. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1,100 points in early trading before fighting back. And at the centre of it all was Nvidia, the chipmaker that has become the symbol of the artificial intelligence boom, which fell 8.6% at its worst point of the day and closed down 3.9%. The message from the market was unmistakable: the great AI trade, the rally that has defined Wall Street for three years, is now being questioned in a serious and sustained way, and the questioning is coming from every direction at once.

Three forces collided on Wednesday to produce the sell-off, and each of them, on its own, would have been enough to make it a bad day. The first was inflation. The Commerce Department released the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index, the Federal Reserve's preferred measure of inflation, for October, and it showed prices rising 3.8% over the previous year, up from 3.7% in September and nearly twice the Fed's 2% target. Core PCE, which strips out volatile food and energy prices and is the number the Fed watches most closely, rose 3.3% year over year, up from 3.1% in September and slightly hotter than the 3.2% that economists had expected. The monthly increase was 0.3%, the third consecutive month at that pace. The data told investors that inflation is not only still too high, but that it is accelerating again, and that the Federal Reserve may have to keep interest rates higher for longer, or even raise them. That is bad news for technology stocks in particular, because their valuations depend on the assumption that future profits are worth a great deal today, and higher interest rates make future profits worth less.

The second force was tariffs. The Trump administration on Wednesday proposed a new round of tariffs on 14 major trading partners, aimed at what it calls structural excess industrial capacity, with the highest rates reserved for China. According to Reuters, China would face an additional 20% levy on top of the roughly 25% in duties imposed during President Donald Trump's first term, taking the total tariff burden on many Chinese goods to around 45%. The proposal also covers the European Union, Japan, India, South Korea and other economies. The announcement revived the trade-war fears that have haunted markets all year, and it came at the worst possible moment, because tariffs push up the cost of imported goods and raw materials, which pushes up prices for businesses and consumers, which makes the inflation problem worse. Investors were looking at a situation in which the two biggest risks to the market — inflation and trade — were reinforcing each other, and they sold.

The third force, and the one that matters most for the long term, was doubt about artificial intelligence itself. For three years, investors have poured money into AI stocks on the assumption that the enormous spending on data centres, chips and power would eventually generate enormous returns. That assumption is now being questioned openly. Traders have grown anxious about the massive investments being poured into AI, and about whether companies will ever generate enough revenue from it to justify the spending. The Democrats' sweep of the midterm elections has added to the worry, because it threatens to pause data centre projects around the country, which could stifle revenue at AI firms. And on Wednesday, S&P Global Ratings added a new and specific worry: it placed Nvidia's senior unsecured debt on CreditWatch with negative implications, saying that the departure and subsequent reinstatement of chief executive Jensen Huang had exposed weaknesses in the chipmaker's management and board oversight. The rating agency was, in effect, telling the market that even Nvidia, the company at the very centre of the AI boom, has governance problems that investors should take seriously.

Nvidia: from hero to headache in three days

No company illustrates the whiplash of this week better than Nvidia. On Monday, the stock surged 31% after Jensen Huang, the co-founder and chief executive who had been ousted in a boardroom coup, was dramatically reinstated, in a move that the Trump administration had pushed for. The rally was one of the biggest single-day moves in the history of a large American company, and it briefly looked as if the AI trade was back. It lasted about a day. On Tuesday, the stock gave back more than 5% of Monday's gain, and on Wednesday it fell 8.6% in intraday trading, closing down 3.9%. In three days, Nvidia had gone from the biggest winner in the market to the biggest loser, and the reason was not any single piece of news but a growing sense that the company's story had become too complicated, and too political, for comfort. The S&P CreditWatch action was the clearest expression of that sense: the rating agency said, in plain terms, that the boardroom chaos around Huang's departure and return had raised questions about how the company is run, and that those questions could affect its creditworthiness.

Nvidia was not alone. Advanced Micro Devices, its main rival in AI chips, fell 9.56% by midday on Wednesday. Oracle, which has become one of the biggest spenders on AI infrastructure, declined more than 8%. Alphabet, the parent company of Google, lost more than 5%. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, which tracks the chip industry, plunged 5.8%. And Anthropic, the AI company that had listed on the stock market only days earlier at $83 a share and briefly been valued at more than $1 trillion, tumbled nearly 10% in early trading, briefly pushing its market capitalisation below $1 trillion. The selling was broad, it was heavy, and it was concentrated exactly where the market's gains had been concentrated for three years: in the technology stocks that had carried the rally. The Nasdaq Composite recorded only 12 new highs on Wednesday, against 329 new lows, which is the kind of ratio that tells you the selling was not selective but indiscriminate.

The scale of the damage is worth setting out in full, because it shows how fast sentiment has turned. At 9:53 in the morning, the Dow Jones Industrial Average was down 995.52 points, or 1.99%, at 49,023.18. The S&P 500 had lost 205.89 points, or 2.86%, to 6,995.20. The Nasdaq Composite had shed 985.93 points, or 4.15%, to 22,747.61. By the close, the market had recovered much of the ground — the Nasdaq finished down just 0.62% and the S&P 500 down 0.35%, and the Dow had wiped out its entire 1,100-point loss — but the recovery does not erase what happened in the morning. The Nasdaq 100 had touched bear-market territory, even if only temporarily, and the S&P 500 had traded below 7,000. The intraday lows were a warning, and the warning was that the market's confidence in the AI trade is fragile, and that it can break very quickly when the news turns bad.

The worst November since 2008

The deeper context is that Wednesday's sell-off was not an isolated event but the latest chapter in a month that has been brutal for technology stocks. The Nasdaq and the S&P 500 were on track, after Wednesday's trading, for their worst November since 2008, the month when the global financial crisis reached its peak. That is a comparison that should make anyone pay attention, because 2008 was the worst financial crisis since the Great Depression, and November 2008 was one of the worst months in the history of the American stock market. The fact that November 2026 is now being compared to it tells you how much damage has been done, and how quickly. The Nasdaq Composite is now within approximately 3% of entering bear-market territory, which is defined as a 20% decline from its previous high, and the Nasdaq 100 has already touched that level intraday. The market that was, only weeks ago, celebrating record highs is now flirting with a bear market, and the turn has been extraordinarily fast.

The selling has not been confined to the United States. China's Shanghai Composite Index closed sharply lower on Wednesday, hitting its lowest level since July 2025, as concerns about the outlook for AI compounded tariff jitters, and semiconductor and other AI-related stocks tumbled across Asia, mirroring the losses among their American peers. The global nature of the sell-off matters, because it shows that the doubt about AI is not an American phenomenon but a worldwide one, and that the tariff proposals, which affect trade between the United States and its biggest partners, are being felt in every major market. When the Shanghai market falls to a 16-month low on the same day that the Nasdaq falls 4%, the message is that the two stories — the AI doubt and the trade war — are now one story, and that it is playing out everywhere.

There is, however, a counterpoint to the gloom, and it is worth stating fairly, because the economic data released on Wednesday was not all bad. The United States economy grew 4.3% in the third quarter, according to a preliminary estimate, which was higher than the 3.5% that economists had expected, and was the fastest growth in three years. Consumer confidence jumped to 103.3 in November, according to the Conference Board, the highest level in nearly two years and much stronger than the 92.4 that had been expected, amid optimism about the labour market and the overall economy following the midterm elections. And the market's recovery from its morning lows — the Dow erasing a 1,100-point loss, the Nasdaq paring a 4.7% fall to 0.62% — showed that there are still buyers willing to step in when prices fall. The bulls will say that the economy is strong, that consumers are confident, and that the sell-off is a buying opportunity. The bears will say that the economy's strength is exactly the problem, because it means the Fed cannot cut rates, and that the AI spending is a bubble that is now deflating. Both sides have evidence, and the market will decide which of them is right in the weeks ahead.

What happens next: the Fed, the tariffs and the AI question

The most immediate question is what the Federal Reserve does next. The PCE data released on Wednesday gave the Fed more fuel to keep interest rates higher, and it bolstered expectations of a December rate hike, which would be the opposite of what the market had hoped for only a few weeks ago. The Fed's preferred inflation measure is now at 3.8%, nearly twice the target, and core PCE is at 3.3% and rising. If the Fed raises rates in December, it will be doing so into a market that is already falling, and that has already seen its most important stocks lose a fifth of their value. That is a difficult position for any central bank, and it is one that the Fed has faced before, in 2008 and in 2022, and in both cases the outcome was painful for investors. The Fed's decision in December will be one of the most consequential of the year, and the PCE data has made it harder, not easier.

The second question is what happens to the tariff proposals. The administration's plan to impose new duties on 14 trading partners, including China, the European Union, Japan and India, is still a proposal, not a final decision, and it will go through a process of consultation and review before it takes effect. But the direction of travel is clear, and the market has understood it: the administration is determined to replace the import duties that the Supreme Court struck down earlier this year, and it is willing to do so even at the cost of higher inflation and lower stock prices. The tariff proposals are, in that sense, a statement of intent, and the market's reaction on Wednesday was a statement of its own: that it does not like the trade-off. Whether the administration moderates the proposals in the face of market pressure, or whether it presses ahead regardless, will be one of the defining questions of the next few weeks.

The third question, and the most important one, is whether the AI boom is over or merely pausing. The doubt that drove Wednesday's selling is not a new doubt — investors have been asking, for more than a year, whether the hundreds of billions of dollars being spent on data centres and chips will ever generate a return — but it has now become the dominant question in the market, and it is being asked by the people who matter most: the rating agencies, the chipmakers' own customers, and the politicians who control the permits for new data centres. The Democrats' midterm sweep has given the sceptics a political voice, because it threatens to pause data centre projects, and the S&P CreditWatch action on Nvidia has given them a financial voice, because it questions the governance of the company at the centre of the boom. The AI trade has survived doubts before, and it may survive this one, but the sell-off on Wednesday showed that the doubt is now deep enough, and broad enough, to move the entire market by 4% in a single morning. That is not a routine wobble. That is a market asking, in the loudest possible way, whether the story it has believed for three years is still true.

The answer will not come in a day, or in a week, but in the earnings reports, the data centre announcements and the policy decisions of the next few months. What is clear now is that November 2026 has become a month that investors will remember, and not fondly. The Nasdaq has had its worst November since 2008, Nvidia has lost 8.6% in a day and been put on downgrade watch, inflation is at 3.8% and rising, and the United States has proposed tariffs on 14 of its biggest trading partners. The market that went into the month at record highs is coming out of it on the edge of a bear market, and the question that every investor is asking is the same one that the market asked on Wednesday morning, when the Nasdaq was down 4.7% and the S&P 500 was below 7,000: how much further does this go? The honest answer is that nobody knows, but the warning signs are now too clear to ignore, and the next few weeks will tell us whether Wednesday was a buying opportunity or the beginning of something much bigger.

A shortened week, and a market that never really sleeps

There is one more detail about Wednesday's sell-off that matters, and it is the calendar. Wednesday was the last full trading day before Thanksgiving, and the American stock market was closed on Thursday for the holiday, with only a shortened session on Friday. That matters, because a sell-off on the eve of a holiday is, in some ways, worse than a sell-off on an ordinary day: it leaves investors with a day and a half to sit and think about their losses, with no market open in which to act on them, and it means that the Friday session, which is traditionally thin and volatile because so many traders are away, will be the first chance for the market to show whether Wednesday's recovery was real or merely a pause. The traders who bought the dip on Wednesday afternoon did so knowing that they would not be able to sell again until Friday, and that the news, in the meantime, would not stop: the tariff proposals will still be there, the inflation data will still be there, and the questions about AI will still be there. A holiday does not pause the news cycle, and it does not pause the worry.

The thin trading that follows a holiday is also, historically, a time when markets can move sharply on very little news, because there are fewer buyers and sellers in the market, and a single large order can move prices further than it would on a normal day. That is a risk for investors who are hoping for a calm end to the week, and it is a risk that the market's professionals are well aware of. The Friday after Thanksgiving is, traditionally, one of the quietest days of the year on Wall Street, but this year it will be watched more closely than usual, because it will be the first test of whether Wednesday's sell-off was a one-day event or the start of something longer. If the market falls again on Friday, on thin volume, the bears will say that the selling has further to go. If it recovers, the bulls will say that Wednesday was the bottom. Either way, the answer will come quickly, and it will set the tone for the final month of the year, which is, for the market, the most important month of all.
