TITLE: OpenAI's GPT-6 Just Designed Brand-New Proteins — and 35 of 322 Actually Worked in the Lab LABELS: Science SEARCH DESCRIPTION: OpenAI says GPT-6.2 designed new proteins, with 35 of 322 minibinders binding their targets in lab tests. General AI is now doing specialist biology. ---BODY---

OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman, pictured with SoftBank founder Masayoshi Son in Tokyo in February 2025. OpenAI announced on November 24, 2026, that its GPT-6 models can design new proteins that work in laboratory tests. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

A general-purpose chatbot has just designed brand-new proteins — and some of them actually worked. On Monday, November 24, 2026, OpenAI announced that its GPT-6 family of artificial intelligence models can design new protein structures and amino-acid sequences using only reasoning and general-purpose code, without calling on specialised biology models to generate the designs — and that, when those designs were built and tested in a real laboratory, they showed evidence of binding to their intended targets. In the headline experiment, the company asked a pre-release version of GPT-6.2 to design 322 small proteins, called minibinders, aimed at five biological targets — and 35 of them, or 10.9 per cent, bound to the target they were designed for. In separate experiments, the model redesigned existing proteins and antibodies with even higher success rates: 75 of 88 redesigned sequences for one target kept working, an 85 per cent hit rate, and 128 of 182 redesigned antibody loops retained their binding, a 70 per cent rate. It is the first time OpenAI has published wet-lab numbers for a protein-design campaign run without a specialised biology model in the loop — and it is, in the judgement of the scientists who have seen it, a genuine milestone on the road to AI-designed medicines. "We want to see a world where a scientist can just describe in natural language what they want a ChatGPT model to do, and the model can just be infinitely flexible and design exactly what the person wants," said Joy Jiao, who leads life-sciences research at OpenAI in San Francisco. On Monday, that world got a little closer.

The announcement, published as a research post and a preprint titled "GPT-6 as a Universal Foundation Model for Protein Biology: Beyond Specialized Models", is the most ambitious claim yet for what a general-purpose AI can do in biology. Proteins are the machines of life: they are the molecules that carry out almost every function in a living cell, from fighting infection to digesting food to transmitting signals between neurons. Designing a new protein — one that binds to a specific target, such as a protein involved in cancer or an immune disorder — is one of the hardest problems in biotechnology, and it has traditionally required specialised tools: software built specifically for protein folding, for molecular docking, for sequence design, each of them the product of years of dedicated research. OpenAI's claim is that a general model, trained primarily on text and code, can now do parts of that work — that it can reason about protein shapes and interactions, draw on its knowledge of mathematics, physics, chemistry and biology, and produce designs that, when they are made in a laboratory, actually bind to their targets. The claim is not that GPT-6 has replaced the specialist tools. It is that it no longer needs them to generate its designs — and that, in some tests, it matches or outperforms them.

The experiment: 322 designs, five targets, 35 hits

The centrepiece of OpenAI's announcement is a laboratory experiment carried out with its research partner, Onava, and it is worth understanding in detail, because the numbers are the evidence for the claim. OpenAI gave GPT-6.2 the three-dimensional structures of five target proteins and marked the spot where each new protein — each minibinder — should attach. The five targets were PD-L1, a protein involved in cancer's ability to evade the immune system; IL-7Rα, a receptor involved in immune cell development; TrkA, a receptor involved in pain signalling and nerve growth; VEGF-A, a protein that drives the growth of blood vessels and is a major target in cancer and eye disease; and IL-17A, a protein involved in inflammation and autoimmune disease. For each target, the model was asked to design small proteins that would bind to it — proteins that did not exist before, designed from scratch, with specified shapes: how many coils, how many strands, and where they should attach. Onava then made 322 of those designs in the laboratory and tested whether they bound to their intended targets. Thirty-five of them did — a hit rate of 10.9 per cent.

The breakdown by target tells its own story. Eighteen of the hits were for IL-17A, the inflammation target — the model's strongest result. Eight were for VEGF-A, five for PD-L1, three for IL-7Rα, and one for TrkA. In every case, at least one design worked — which is to say, for each of the five targets the model was given, it produced at least one protein that bound to it in the laboratory. That is a meaningful result, because it shows that the model's success was not a fluke confined to a single, easy target. It worked, to varying degrees, across five different proteins, involved in five different biological processes, with five different shapes and chemistries. The hit rate of 10.9 per cent may sound modest — nine out of ten designs failed — but in the context of protein design, where the space of possible proteins is astronomically large and the vast majority of random designs would bind to nothing at all, a double-digit hit rate from a general-purpose model, on its first attempt, is a result that specialists have described as striking. The preprint notes that an earlier study using RFdiffusion, a specialist protein-design tool, reported higher hit rates — but adds that results depend heavily on how strictly designs are filtered before laboratory testing, and on how binding is defined, making direct comparisons difficult.

The redesign experiments, in which the model was given an existing protein structure and asked to propose a new amino-acid sequence for it, produced higher hit rates still — and they are, in some ways, the more immediately practical result. When GPT-6.2 was given the known structure of a minibinder for MDM2, a protein involved in cancer, and asked to redesign its sequence, 75 of 88 designs — 85 per cent — showed binding. For VEGF-A, 26 of 42 redesigned sequences — 62 per cent — bound. For a known PD-1 minibinder, the rate was lower: 8 of 59, or 14 per cent. When the model redesigned antibody binding loops aimed at IL-4Rα, a receptor involved in allergy and asthma, 128 of 182 designs — 70 per cent — retained binding. And when it redesigned fragments from five therapeutic antibodies, 179 of 272 — 66 per cent — kept their binding to PD-1 or PD-L1. These are not the results of a model that is guessing. They are the results of a model that has learned, from its training, something real about how proteins work — about the relationship between a protein's sequence, its shape, and its function — and that can apply that knowledge to new problems, in new contexts, with a reliability that is already useful.

How it worked — and what the model actually did

The question that will be asked, by scientists and by sceptics alike, is what the model actually did — and what it did not do. OpenAI's framing is that the GPT-6 models drew on their own reasoning and code to design the proteins, without calling specialised biology models to generate those designs. The models' written reasoning and code, the company says, suggest they draw on mathematics, physics, chemistry and biological knowledge to reason about protein shapes and interactions with other molecules. The researchers asked the models to do things that a protein designer would do: to design a small protein that binds to a given target, to build a protein around specified structural features, to predict where and how a small molecule would bind to a protein, to rank how changes in a sequence would affect properties such as stability and binding. And the models did those things, in natural language and in code, producing designs that were then tested — computationally, and then in the laboratory. The vision, as Joy Jiao put it, is a world in which a scientist can describe in ordinary language what they want, and the model designs it — a world in which protein design becomes as accessible as writing a sentence.

But the campaign was hybrid in practice, and OpenAI is open about that. GPT-6.2 designed the protein backbones — the three-dimensional shapes — but specialised tools were involved in the steps around it. SolubleMPNN, a specialist sequence-design tool, assigned the amino-acid sequences for the first minibinder run. Boltz-2, OpenDDE and ESMFold2 — specialist structure-prediction models — filtered the candidates before any design reached a laboratory bench. The pipeline, in other words, was not GPT-6 alone, from start to finish. It was GPT-6 generating the designs, and specialist tools helping to turn those designs into testable proteins and to select the most promising candidates for testing. That matters, because it is the difference between a model that can design proteins by itself and a model that can design proteins as part of a system — and because it is the honest account of where the technology stands. The company's claim is not that the specialist tools are obsolete. It is that the hardest part of the work — the creative act of designing a new protein shape — can now be done by a general model, and that the specialist tools are increasingly playing a supporting role, rather than the leading one.

The computational results, reported across 14 protein tasks, reinforce that picture. OpenAI says its latest internal models matched or outperformed specialised models on many of those tasks — including designing binders for target proteins, creating proteins around specified structural features, predicting structures and molecular interactions, and estimating how sequence changes affect stability and binding. On one benchmark, PoseBusters, which tests molecular docking, GPT-6.2 scored 93 per cent, against 50 per cent for DiffDock-L, a specialist tool. On the Scarpino dataset, it scored 92 per cent, against 60 per cent. But there is one task where the general model is still clearly weaker, and OpenAI does not hide it: predicting a complete protein structure from its amino-acid sequence. On that task, GPT-6.2 scored a TM-score — a measure of structural similarity, where 1.0 is a perfect match — of 0.38, against 0.93 for ESMFold2, a specialist structure-prediction model. The average TM-score rose from 0.16 for the earlier GPT-5.6 to 0.26 for GPT-6.2 and 0.38 for the latest internal model — a clear trajectory of improvement, but still a long way from the specialist's 0.93. OpenAI acknowledges that "predicting a complete protein structure from its amino acid sequence remains substantially harder" for its models than for the specialists. The gap is narrowing, but it has not closed.

What it means for medicine — and for drug discovery

The reason this announcement matters, beyond the laboratory, is what protein design is for — and what it could become. Proteins designed to bind to specific targets are the basis of many modern medicines. Antibodies — the immune system's own binding proteins, now engineered in laboratories — are among the most successful drugs ever made, treating cancers, autoimmune diseases, asthma, arthritis and more. The targets in OpenAI's experiment are not random: PD-L1 is the target of some of the most important cancer immunotherapy drugs in the world. VEGF-A is the target of drugs for macular degeneration and for cancer. IL-17A is the target of drugs for psoriasis and arthritis. IL-7Rα and TrkA are targets in immune disorders and pain. If a general-purpose AI can design proteins that bind to these targets — even at a 10.9 per cent hit rate, even as a starting point for further refinement — then the process of discovering new drugs could become faster, cheaper and more accessible. A scientist with an idea for a new medicine would not need a team of protein-design specialists and a suite of dedicated software. They would need a model, a description of the target, and a laboratory to test the results.

The potential for speed is what has caught the attention of the pharmaceutical industry. The chief executive of Novartis, Vas Narasimhan, predicted this week that AI could cut four years from drug development timelines — while questioning predictions that AI will cure diseases such as cancer within a decade. The two halves of that statement capture the mood of the moment: genuine excitement about what AI can do to accelerate the slow, expensive process of drug discovery, tempered by realism about how far there is still to go. Designing a protein that binds to a target in a laboratory assay is not the same as making a medicine. Binding is not biological function, and the OpenAI post publishes no data on affinity — how strongly the proteins bind — or on potency, or on toxicity. A protein that binds weakly, or that binds but does nothing, or that binds and is toxic, is not a drug. It is a starting point. The journey from a binding protein in a dish to a medicine in a pharmacy takes years, costs billions, and fails far more often than it succeeds. AI can shorten the first steps of that journey. It cannot, by itself, complete it.

But the first steps are where the bottleneck has been, and shortening them matters. Traditional drug discovery begins with a target — a protein involved in a disease — and then searches, often for years, for a molecule that binds to it. That search has traditionally been a matter of screening: testing thousands, or millions, of candidate molecules, one by one, in the hope that one of them works. AI-designed proteins offer a different approach: instead of searching through what already exists, design what you need, from scratch, for the target you have chosen. That is the promise of OpenAI's announcement — not that GPT-6 has discovered a new drug, but that it has shown it can design the raw material from which new drugs are made, and that it can do so for targets that matter, with a reliability that is already measurable. For the pharmaceutical industry, which spends billions on drug discovery and fails more often than it succeeds, that promise is worth paying attention to. For patients waiting for new treatments, it is worth hoping for — cautiously.

The bigger picture: general AI moves into biology

OpenAI's announcement does not stand alone. It is part of a broader movement, in which general-purpose AI models — the same models that write essays, answer questions and generate code — are moving into biology, and doing things that were, until recently, the exclusive domain of specialists. The same week as the protein announcement, the science journal Nature reported on the reaction of scientists to the new biology skills of the leading AI models, noting that on November 24, OpenAI announced that its latest language models had successfully designed new proteins, some of which behaved as expected when made and tested in the laboratory. The article also noted the work of Anthropic's Claude, which has been developing its own biology capabilities, and the reaction of researchers who have been, in the words of the report, "wowed" by what the models can do. The comparison that is being drawn, explicitly, is with AlphaFold — DeepMind's protein-structure prediction system, which revolutionised biology when it was released, and which remains the gold standard for predicting what a protein looks like from its sequence. The new claim is not that GPT-6 has replaced AlphaFold. It is that general models are now doing things that, a few years ago, only AlphaFold and its specialist cousins could do — and that they are doing them in a different way, through reasoning and language, rather than through dedicated training on protein data alone.

The same GPT-6.2 model has been showing up in other scientific contexts, too. According to reports this week, OpenAI's Astra — a version of the GPT-6.2 family — helped amateur astronomers identify potential exoplanets, using Codex and publicly available observations from NASA's Kepler and TESS space telescopes. The model also, reportedly, investigated an unresolved historical mystery through autonomous research, hypothesis formation and evidence analysis, and OpenAI published a mathematical preprint claiming a new result on matrix multiplication — an exponent of 9/4, a claim that, if independently validated, could have implications for the computational efficiency of multiplying matrices. These are very different domains — biology, astronomy, history, mathematics — and the fact that the same family of models is producing results in all of them is itself the point. The argument OpenAI is making, across all of these announcements, is that a general model, trained to reason, can be applied to specialist problems in many fields — and that the era of needing a separate, dedicated AI for every scientific discipline may be coming to an end. Whether that argument holds up, in biology and elsewhere, will depend on what the models do next — and on whether independent scientists, working with the published preprint, can reproduce what OpenAI has reported.

That question of independent validation is, for now, the most important caveat on the announcement. The findings come from an OpenAI preprint and company-run research, which means the strongest performance claims are vendor-reported and still subject to independent validation. The laboratory work was carried out with Onava, a research partner, and the results have not yet been through the peer review that would subject them to the scrutiny of independent experts. The hit rates — 10.9 per cent for new minibinders, 85 per cent for MDM2 redesigns — are real numbers, from real laboratory tests, but they are numbers that OpenAI has chosen to report, from experiments that OpenAI designed. The comparison with RFdiffusion, the specialist tool that reported higher hit rates in its own study, is a reminder that the field is competitive, and that different groups, using different filtering rules and different definitions of binding, will get different numbers. The test of OpenAI's claim will not be this announcement. It will be what happens when other laboratories, with other targets, using the published methods, try to do the same thing — and whether they get the same results.

The limits — and what comes next

For all the excitement, the limits of the work are clear, and OpenAI has been candid about them. Binding in an assay is not biological function, and the post publishes no affinity constants, no potency data and no toxicity data. A protein that binds to PD-L1 in a dish may or may not block PD-L1's function in a cell, and may or may not be safe, stable or deliverable as a drug. The models' structure prediction remains weak — a TM-score of 0.38 against the specialist's 0.93 — which means that for the task of working out what a protein looks like from its sequence, the general model is still far behind. The pipeline is hybrid, not end-to-end: specialist tools supplied sequences and filtered candidates, and the laboratory work was done by a partner, not by the model itself. And the release lands four days after OpenAI's MedChemBench, a chemistry benchmark, suggesting that the company is on a deliberate campaign to establish its models' credentials in the life sciences — a campaign that will be judged, ultimately, not by announcements but by results, reproduced by others, in the open literature.

There are also questions that the announcement raises and does not answer — questions that will be asked, loudly, in the months ahead. If a general-purpose AI can design proteins that bind to targets involved in disease, can it also design proteins that bind to targets involved in harm — toxins, pathogens, bioweapons? The dual-use question is not hypothetical: the same capability that designs a medicine can, in principle, design a poison, and the same model that reasons about protein shapes for drug discovery can reason about them for other purposes. OpenAI has not, in this announcement, addressed the biosecurity implications of its work in detail — and the scientists and policymakers who think about those implications will be reading the preprint closely, and asking what safeguards are in place. The history of AI in biology is, in part, a history of capabilities arriving faster than the governance around them, and GPT-6's protein-design skills are the latest example. The question is not whether the capability is real — the laboratory results suggest it is — but whether the world is ready for it, and what rules should govern who can use it, and for what.

What comes next, for OpenAI and for the field, is a period of testing, replication and extension. Other laboratories will try the methods. Other targets will be attempted. The models will improve — the trajectory from GPT-5.6's TM-score of 0.16 to GPT-6.2's 0.26 to the latest internal model's 0.38 suggests that the next version will be better still, and that the gap with the specialists will continue to narrow. The preprint will be scrutinised, the numbers will be challenged, and the claims will be refined. And somewhere, in a laboratory, a scientist will describe in natural language the protein they want — a binder for a target that has resisted every drug so far — and a model will design it, and it will be made, and tested, and it will either bind or it will not. That is the world Joy Jiao described: a world in which a scientist can just describe what they want, and the model designs it. On Monday, November 24, that world arrived — in a preprint, in a laboratory, in 35 proteins out of 322 that did what they were designed to do. It is a beginning, not an ending. But it is a beginning that changes what is possible.

The bottom line

OpenAI announced on Monday, November 24, 2026, that its GPT-6 family of models can design new protein structures and amino-acid sequences using reasoning and general-purpose code, without relying on specialised biology models to generate the designs — and that laboratory tests with research partner Onava found evidence of binding across five targets. But the result is a milestone all the same: the first wet-lab evidence that a general-purpose AI can design new proteins that work — and a glimpse of a future in which a scientist describes the medicine they want in plain language, and an AI designs it.
