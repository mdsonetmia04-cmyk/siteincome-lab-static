TITLE: 9 Hurt, 3 Critical as a Huge Blast Rocks Bangkok's Only City Refinery — Flames Visible Across the Capital LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A huge explosion at Bangkok's Bangchak refinery injured 9 people, 3 critically, rattled doors kilometres away and shut the 120,000-barrel-a-day plant. ---BODY---

The Bang Chak refinery in Bangkok's Phra Khanong district. On Thursday, November 26, 2026, a huge explosion ripped through the plant — Thailand's only refinery located inside a city — injuring nine people, three of them critically. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

It was a Thursday afternoon in Bangkok, and the sky over the Thai capital suddenly turned black. At about 2:45 in the afternoon local time, a huge explosion ripped through the Bangchak oil refinery in the Phra Khanong district of eastern Bangkok, sending a column of flame high into the air and a tower of thick black smoke drifting across the skyline of one of Asia's biggest cities. The blast was so powerful that it rattled doors several kilometres away, according to reporters from the AFP news agency who watched the fireball rise above the sprawling industrial site. Within minutes, the authorities were urging residents in the surrounding neighbourhoods to leave their homes immediately. By the time the evening came, nine people had been injured — three of them critically — and Thailand's only refinery inside a city had shut down every one of its production units.

The explosion happened at about 2:45pm local time, which is 0745 GMT, according to Bangchak Corporation, the Thai energy conglomerate that runs the site. The Bangkok Post reported that the incident was first reported to firefighters at 2:51pm, with crews from the Phra Khanong fire station dispatched immediately. Al Jazeera's Tony Cheng, reporting from Chiang Mai, said the explosion was heard at about 3pm local time, or 08:00 GMT. The slight differences in the reported times reflect the chaos of the first minutes — a blast heard across a district, a fireball visible from far away, and a city trying to work out, in real time, what had just happened in its own backyard.

What happened next was fast. Bangchak said it responded immediately to contain the incident, shut down all production units at the refinery, and had the fire extinguished by 3:15pm — roughly half an hour after the blast. By 3:45pm, the company said, the situation was fully under control. The Bangkok authorities sent a city-wide alert to mobile phones confirming that the fire was under control, while still urging people to avoid the area. The Bang Na district office issued its own alert, warning residents that an explosion had occurred at the Bangchak oil depot and urging those in the surrounding areas to leave immediately for their own safety. The highway and most of the major traffic routes in the area stayed open, and the authorities said the fire had been brought under control quickly enough that the wider city kept moving.

But the human cost was real, and the numbers changed as the afternoon went on. The Erawan Emergency Medical Centre, which is operated by the Bangkok city authorities, first reported seven people injured, four of them in critical condition. Later, the centre updated its assessment: nine people were injured, three of them classified as critical. The change from the initial assessment — more people hurt in total, but fewer in the most serious category — is the kind of revision that happens as casualties are counted properly in the hours after an industrial accident, when the first figures come from the scene and the later ones come from the hospitals. Either way, the toll was serious: nine people hurt, three fighting for their lives, in an explosion at a refinery that sits in the middle of a residential area of a city of more than ten million people.

The only refinery inside a city

The reason this explosion mattered so much — the reason it rattled a whole capital city, and not just the workers inside the fence — is where it happened. The Bangchak refinery at Phra Khanong is, according to the Channel News Asia report on the blast, Thailand's only refinery located in an urban area. It sits on Sukhumvit Soi 62 in the Phra Khanong district of eastern Bangkok, a part of the city that mixes industry with homes, shops, offices and warehouses, and it sits near Bangkok's main port. Bangchak Corporation's own website describes the site as a modern complex refinery with a maximum capacity of 120,000 barrels per day. That is a large industrial plant by any measure — and it is surrounded by a city.

Thailand's Energy Minister, Akanat Promphan, visited the scene on Thursday afternoon, and his first words to reporters captured the fear that had gripped the area. He said the blast sparked concern precisely because the sprawling Phra Khanong site was, in his words, "also a residential area". A refinery explosion in a remote industrial zone is a serious industrial accident. A refinery explosion in a residential district of a capital city is a different kind of event altogether — one that raises questions about evacuation, about air quality, about how close homes and schools should ever be to a plant that processes 120,000 barrels of oil a day.

The minister also brought the first piece of reassurance. He told reporters that it appeared the fire had broken out at a distillation tower, which was at least 100 metres — about 330 feet — away from the oil depot itself, limiting the damage. That distance mattered enormously. A fire in a distillation tower is dangerous; a fire that reaches an oil depot, with its storage tanks, is a catastrophe on a completely different scale. The fact that the tower stood at least 100 metres from the depot is, according to the minister, what kept Thursday's explosion from becoming something far worse. Bangchak's own preliminary investigation found that the incident occurred at the refinery's hydrocracking unit, the company said — a part of the plant where heavy oil fractions are broken down into lighter, more valuable products under high pressure and high temperature, using hydrogen. It is one of the most technically demanding parts of any refinery, and one of the places where a failure can be the most violent.

The distinction between a distillation tower and a hydrocracking unit may sound technical, but it is the kind of detail that investigators will spend weeks on. A distillation tower separates crude oil into its fractions by boiling point. A hydrocracking unit takes the heaviest of those fractions and cracks them apart. Both operate at high temperatures and pressures, and both are places where a leak, a pressure failure or a spark can turn into a fireball in seconds. The company's statement — that the fire was at the hydrocracking unit — and the minister's account — that it broke out at a distillation tower — are not necessarily contradictory: the units in a refinery are connected, and a fire that starts in one place can spread to another. What is clear from both accounts is that the fire was in the processing heart of the plant, not in the storage tanks, and that this is what kept the damage contained.

Thirty minutes that decided the day

The speed of the response is one of the clearest facts in every report of the explosion. Bangchak said it brought the situation under control within 30 minutes. The fire was extinguished at 3:15pm, about half an hour after it began. By 3:45pm, the company said the situation was fully under control. For a refinery fire — the kind of fire that can burn for days if it reaches the wrong part of a plant — half an hour is fast. It is the difference between an incident and a disaster, and it is the reason the Bangkok authorities could tell residents, within the hour, that the fire was under control, even while they were still urging people to stay away from the area.

That speed did not happen by accident. A refinery of this size has its own firefighting systems, its own emergency procedures, and its own trained crews — and the Phra Khanong fire station is close by. The Bangkok Post reported that firefighters from the Phra Khanong station were dispatched quickly after the incident was reported at 2:51pm. The combination — the company's own response, the city's firefighters, and the decision to shut down all production units immediately — is what stopped the fire before it could spread from the processing units to the storage tanks. Shutting down all production units is itself a major decision: it stops the flow of oil through the plant, removes the fuel that a fire needs to keep growing, and it means the refinery produces nothing until it can be inspected and restarted. Bangchak took that decision within minutes of the blast.

The evacuation order tells its own story about how seriously the authorities took the first minutes. The Bang Na district office told residents in the surrounding areas to leave immediately for their own safety. The Erawan Emergency Medical Centre's casualty figures, the city-wide mobile phone alert, the minister's visit to the scene — all of it happened within hours of a blast that, by the evening, was already under control. The authorities treated the explosion as a potential catastrophe first, and as a contained incident second. That is the right order of operations when a refinery explodes in a residential district. The residents who were told to leave did not know, in those first minutes, whether the fire would reach the storage tanks. The authorities did not know either. The evacuation was the precaution that a city takes when it cannot be sure.

The smoke over Bangkok — and the air underneath it

For millions of Bangkok residents who were nowhere near Phra Khanong, the explosion announced itself as a column of black smoke. Videos posted on social media showed flames rising high above the site and thick black smoke sweeping across the Thai capital's skyline. AFP reporters saw a column of flames above the site followed by thick black smoke. The smoke was visible from a long distance, and it kept rising intermittently as flames flared up again after the first blast. In a city that already struggles with air pollution — Bangkok regularly records unhealthy air quality, particularly in the dry season — a tower of black smoke from a refinery fire is not just a dramatic sight. It is a public health question.

The authorities moved quickly to answer that question. Bangchak later said that air quality monitoring around the refinery showed normal levels. Energy Minister Akanat Promphan said the authorities had tested for carbon monoxide, sulphur dioxide and other contaminants in a one-kilometre radius around the facility, and found no irregularities. Carbon monoxide and sulphur dioxide are two of the gases that a refinery fire can release in dangerous quantities: carbon monoxide from incomplete combustion, sulphur dioxide from the sulphur in crude oil. Testing within a one-kilometre radius — and finding nothing abnormal — was the evidence the authorities needed to tell residents that the smoke, however frightening it looked, had not poisoned the air around the plant.

That finding mattered for another reason. The refinery sits in a residential area, and the people who live closest to it are the people who breathe its air every day. If the fire had released dangerous levels of contaminants, the evacuation would have had to last longer, and the health consequences would have extended far beyond the nine people injured in the blast itself. The normal readings did not erase the explosion, and they did not answer the question of why it happened. But they did mean that the thousands of people who live around the refinery could go home, and that the hospitals did not have to prepare for a wave of poisoning cases on top of the burn injuries they were already treating.

Nine injured, three critical — and the numbers that kept changing

The casualty figures from Thursday's explosion deserve a closer look, because they changed during the day, and the changes tell a story about how casualty numbers work in the first hours after a disaster. The first reports, carried by Channel News Asia and the Bangkok Post, said seven people were injured, four of them in critical condition. The later reports, carried by Al Jazeera, Dawn and The Straits Times, said nine people were injured, three of them in critical condition. Both sets of figures came from the same source: the Erawan Emergency Medical Centre, operated by the Bangkok city authorities. The centre's initial assessment was seven injured, four critical. Its later assessment was nine injured, three critical.

That is not a contradiction, and it is not an error. It is what happens when injured people are counted twice — once at the scene, in the confusion of the first hour, and once at the hospital, when the full list of admissions is known. Two more people were found to have been injured than the first count suggested, and one person first classified as critical was reclassified as less seriously hurt, or vice versa. The final figure, as reported by the later accounts, is nine injured and three critical. What the figures do not say is who the injured people were — whether they were refinery workers, firefighters, or residents of the surrounding area — and the reports available on Thursday did not name them. In an industrial accident of this kind, the injured are most often workers at the plant and the emergency crews who respond first, but that is an inference, not a reported fact, and the reports did not confirm it.

What the figures do establish is the scale of the human cost relative to the scale of the explosion. A blast powerful enough to rattle doors several kilometres away, at a refinery processing up to 120,000 barrels a day, in a residential district of a city of more than ten million people, injured nine people and killed none — at least, none were reported dead on Thursday. That is a remarkable outcome, and it is largely a matter of where the fire started and how fast it was stopped. Had the fire reached the oil depot, 100 metres away, the casualty figures would almost certainly have been very different. Had the explosion happened at a shift change, or in a part of the plant where more workers were present, the numbers would have been different again. Nine injured, three critical, is a serious toll. It is also, given what could have happened, a toll that the city can be grateful was not worse.

A site with a history

This was not the first explosion at the Bangchak refinery. The Channel News Asia report on Thursday's blast carried a section headed "2012 BLAST", a reminder that the Phra Khanong site has exploded before. The 2012 incident is part of the plant's history, and it is part of why Thursday's explosion will be investigated so closely. A refinery that has had a serious explosion once, and then has another one fourteen years later, raises questions that go beyond the immediate cause of the second blast: questions about maintenance, about safety systems, about whether the lessons of the first incident were fully learned, and about whether a refinery of this size should still be operating inside a city at all.

Those questions will be asked in the coming days and weeks, by the Thai authorities, by Bangchak's own investigators, and by the residents who live around the plant. The cause of Thursday's explosion was under investigation on Thursday evening, according to Bangchak's statement, and the company has not yet said what went wrong. The preliminary finding — that the incident occurred at the hydrocracking unit — is a starting point, not a conclusion. A full investigation of a refinery explosion typically takes weeks or months, and it examines everything from the condition of the equipment to the maintenance records, the operating procedures, the training of the staff on duty, and the safety systems that were supposed to prevent exactly this kind of failure.

The 2012 precedent matters because it shows that the risk was known. A refinery in a city is a known risk, managed by safety systems and procedures that are supposed to keep the risk contained. When those systems fail twice at the same site, the question is no longer just about what went wrong on the day. It is about whether the systems themselves are adequate, and whether the decision to keep a 120,000-barrel-a-day refinery in a residential district of Bangkok remains the right one. Those are questions for the Thai government, for Bangchak, and for the people of Phra Khanong — and they will not be answered by the fire being extinguished in thirty minutes, however welcome that was.

What happens to Bangkok's fuel now

Beyond the immediate human cost, Thursday's explosion raises a practical question: what happens to the fuel supply of a city of more than ten million people when its only urban refinery shuts down all of its production units? Bangchak said it had shut down all production units at the Phra Khanong refinery following the fire. The company did not say, on Thursday, when the refinery would restart, or how long the shutdown would last. A refinery that has had an explosion and a fire cannot simply be switched back on. Every unit has to be inspected, the damaged equipment has to be repaired or replaced, the safety systems have to be checked, and the authorities have to be satisfied that it is safe to resume operations. That process can take days, weeks, or — if the damage is extensive — months.

Thailand has other refineries, and the country's fuel supply does not depend on a single plant. But the Phra Khanong refinery, with its capacity of 120,000 barrels a day, is a significant part of the country's refining capacity, and its shutdown will have to be covered by other plants, by imports, or by drawing down stocks. The Bangkok Post and the other reports on Thursday did not say whether fuel supplies would be affected, and there were no reports of shortages or price rises on the day of the explosion. The highway and the major traffic routes in the area stayed open, which meant that fuel tankers could keep moving. But the longer the refinery stays shut, the more the question of supply will matter — and the more the authorities will have to explain how the country's fuel system copes without one of its major plants.

There is also the question of the refinery's future. A plant that has exploded twice, in 2012 and again in 2026, in a residential district of the capital, will face pressure — from residents, from the media, from politicians — to explain why it is still there, and whether it should be moved, modernised, or closed. That pressure will grow if the investigation finds that the explosion was caused by a failure that should have been prevented. For now, the refinery is shut, the fire is out, the air is normal, and nine people are in hospital. The investigation has only just begun, and the answers — about the cause, about the safety systems, about the future of the plant — will take time. What Thursday proved, beyond doubt, is that a refinery inside a city is a risk that the city lives with every day, and that when the risk becomes real, the whole capital sees it in the sky.

The wider picture: industrial safety in Asia's megacities

Bangkok is not the only Asian megacity with a refinery, a chemical plant or a fuel depot inside its borders, and Thursday's explosion is not the first time an industrial accident has reminded a city of the danger in its midst. Across Asia, the rapid growth of cities has swallowed the industrial sites that were once built on their outskirts. Plants that were on the edge of town fifty years ago are now in the middle of residential districts, surrounded by homes, schools and offices that were built up around them. The Bangchak refinery is a textbook example: a plant that was once on the edge of Bangkok, now in the middle of Phra Khanong, one of the city's most densely built-up districts.

The tension is familiar in every fast-growing city. The plant provides jobs, fuel and tax revenue. The homes around it provide votes, and their residents provide the political pressure that follows an explosion. Moving a refinery is enormously expensive, and finding a new site for one is almost impossible in a country where every piece of coastline is already contested. Keeping it where it is means accepting a risk that, on Thursday, became real again. The Thai authorities will have to weigh those competing pressures in the coming weeks — and the nine injured people, three of them critically, will be at the centre of that calculation.

For the residents of Phra Khanong, the calculation is simpler. They live next to a refinery that has now exploded twice in fourteen years. They were told on Thursday to evacuate their homes immediately, and they watched black smoke rise over their neighbourhood while the authorities tested the air for poison. The fire was out in thirty minutes, the air was normal, and the evacuation was lifted. But the memory of Thursday afternoon — the blast that rattled doors kilometres away, the fireball, the smoke — will stay with them. And the next time they hear a loud noise from the direction of the refinery, they will remember what it sounded like the last time. That is the real cost of an explosion in a residential district: not just the injuries, not just the shutdown, but the knowledge, for everyone who lives nearby, that the plant that powers their city can also, without warning, explode in their backyard.
