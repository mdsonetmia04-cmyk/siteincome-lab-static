TITLE: DOJ Just Charged 800+ Scammers and Recovered $8 Billion in the Largest Anti-Scam Crackdown in American History LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The Justice Department charged more than 800 defendants and recovered $8 billion in the largest anti-scam enforcement surge in US history. ---BODY---

US Attorney General Todd Blanche, who announced the crackdown in Washington on November 24. Photo: US Department of Justice / Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

The United States Justice Department has charged more than 800 people and recovered more than $8 billion in what officials are calling the largest anti-scam enforcement surge in the history of the country. Attorney General Todd Blanche announced the results on Tuesday, November 24, at a press conference in Washington, flanked by FBI Director Kash Patel and Assistant Attorney General A. Tysen Duva. The numbers are staggering: more than 400 criminal cases filed across 85 federal districts, hundreds of defendants charged, extradited or sentenced, and billions of dollars in intended victim losses clawed back. Behind the headline figures lies a darker story — a global industry of fraud built on forced labour, human trafficking and industrial-scale deception, much of it aimed squarely at ordinary Americans. "This is the largest anti-scam enforcement surge in history," Blanche said. "We are identifying and prosecuting criminals who have deceptively and maliciously stolen from Americans of every age and income bracket across the country."

The announcement matters because the scale of the problem has become almost impossible to grasp. Blanche cited a recent Gallup study finding that 10 per cent of Americans reported that they or a member of their household had fallen victim to a scam in 2025 — and that those scams cost an estimated $68 billion in a single year. That is more than the annual budget of many countries, stolen one phone call, one text message and one fake website at a time. The Justice Department's answer, eight months in the making, has been a government-wide campaign ordered by President Donald Trump in March, when he signed an executive order directing federal agencies to crack down on fraud and predatory schemes targeting Americans. The results announced on Tuesday are the first full accounting of what that campaign has produced — and officials say it is only the beginning.

The numbers behind the crackdown

Start with the figures Blanche put on the table. Since the President's executive order in March, the Justice Department has filed more than 400 criminal cases in 85 federal districts — which is to say, in nearly every corner of the federal court system. Those cases produced charges against more than 800 defendants. Some have been charged. Some have been extradited from abroad. Some have pleaded guilty. Some have already been sentenced to prison. And the money: more than $8 billion in intended victim losses recovered. That phrase — "intended victim losses" — matters, because it means the money the scammers were trying to steal, including funds seized before they could be spent or moved beyond reach. It is not the same as money returned to victims, and officials did not say how much of the $8 billion will actually reach the people who were defrauded. But as a measure of the scale of the criminal enterprise, it is extraordinary: $8 billion is roughly the size of the economy of a small country, and it was being siphoned out of American households by organised fraud.

The FBI's parallel operation, called Operation Blackout, produced numbers that are larger still. According to Patel, federal agents and international partners have seized nearly $17 billion in illicit funds, confiscated more than 14,000 digital devices, and dismantled 29 scam compounds across four continents. The operation also freed more than 2,200 human trafficking victims and directly warned more than 11,000 Americans that they were in the middle of being scammed — interventions that, in many cases, stopped the theft before it was complete. "29 scam compounds across four continents no longer exist," Patel said in a post on social media after the announcement. The FBI, he said, had made dismantling predatory scam enterprises "a top priority using a whole-of-government approach."

The distinction between the two sets of figures is worth understanding. The Justice Department's $8 billion is the money recovered through its own criminal cases — the prosecutions, the guilty pleas, the sentences. The FBI's $17 billion is the broader haul from Operation Blackout, including funds seized in joint operations with foreign partners and in actions that may not yet have produced charges. The two figures overlap in part and diverge in part. What they agree on is the scale: this is not a collection of small-time con artists. It is an industry, and it is worth tens of billions of dollars a year.

Operation Blackout and the scam compounds

The most striking part of the announcement was not the money. It was the description of where the money comes from. Patel described the 29 dismantled scam compounds as self-contained towns — in some cases, effectively small cities — where criminal networks force human trafficking victims to run online fraud schemes against Americans. The victims, many of them lured from other countries with promises of legitimate work, are held in guarded compounds, stripped of their passports, and forced to sit at banks of computers and phones for 12, 14, 16 hours a day, running romance scams, investment scams, fake tech-support schemes and cryptocurrency frauds against people they will never meet. Those who refuse, or who fail to meet quotas, face beatings, starvation and worse. More than 2,200 of these workers have now been freed, according to the FBI — a number that is both a triumph and a measure of how many people remain trapped. For every compound dismantled, there are others still operating.

The compounds are the industrial heart of the modern scam economy, and they explain why the fraud has grown so fast. A generation ago, a scam was a single person with a telephone. Today it is a factory: hundreds of workers, scripts, targets, quotas and managers, operating around the clock, in multiple languages, across multiple time zones. The 14,000 digital devices seized in Operation Blackout — phones, laptops, servers, SIM banks — are the factory floor. The $17 billion seized is the factory's output. And the compounds themselves, spread across four continents, are the reason the problem cannot be solved by arresting a few people in one country. It requires the kind of international cooperation the FBI says it used: joint operations with foreign partners, intelligence sharing, and coordinated raids.

The compounds also explain the human cost that does not show up in the dollar figures. The people forced to work in them are victims twice over: first of the traffickers who brought them there, and then of the criminal justice systems that may treat them as criminals rather than as captives. The FBI's emphasis on the 2,200 freed workers is a signal that the operation was designed, at least in part, as a rescue mission as well as a law enforcement action. But the number also raises an uncomfortable question: if 29 compounds held more than 2,200 people, how many more are still inside the compounds that have not been found?

One in ten Americans

The reason the crackdown has become a priority is that the scams have become almost universal. The Gallup study cited by Blanche found that 10 per cent of Americans — one in ten — reported that they or someone in their household had been scammed in 2025. The estimated losses: $68 billion in a single year. To put that in perspective, $68 billion is more than Americans spend on home heating in a year, more than the entire budget of the Department of Homeland Security, and roughly $200 for every man, woman and child in the country. And the Gallup figure is almost certainly an underestimate, because many victims never report what happened to them — out of shame, out of fear, or because they do not know where to report it.

The victims are not who most people imagine. Blanche stressed that the criminals "have deceptively and maliciously stolen from Americans of every age and income bracket across the country." The stereotype of the scam victim is an elderly person confused by a phone call. The reality, according to the Justice Department, is much broader: young people targeted by sextortion and fake investment schemes, working-age adults targeted by job scams and impersonation fraud, seniors targeted by sweepstakes and grandparent scams, widows targeted by romance scams. The scams are tailored to the target, and the targeting is done with data — bought, stolen or scraped — that tells the scammer who you are, how old you are, what you care about and what you are afraid of.

The breadth of the victim pool is also why the political response has been so forceful. A crime that touches one in ten households is not a niche problem. It is a national one, and it cuts across every demographic and every political divide. That is part of the reason the announcement was made by the Attorney General himself, flanked by the FBI Director, and amplified by the Vice President on social media. The administration is treating the crackdown as a signature achievement — and, given the scale of the losses, it is easy to see why.

How the scams work

Blanche gave several concrete examples of what the crackdown has actually dismantled, and they illustrate the range of the fraud. Agents dismantled websites that impersonated federal courts — fake court sites designed to trick people into paying non-existent fines or handing over personal information. They arrested suspects involved in an alleged sweepstakes scheme that defrauded seniors of millions of dollars, promising prizes that did not exist in exchange for "fees" that did. And they charged 14 people in three indictments in federal and state courts for schemes involving contraband mobile phones smuggled into Georgia prisons — phones that inmates then used to run fraud schemes from behind bars — while another 17 defendants were charged in state court for smuggling the phones themselves.

The prison-phone cases are a reminder of how adaptable the fraud industry is. Inmates with smuggled phones can run the same scams as a compound in another country: impersonating government officials, running romance scams, operating fake investment schemes. The phones are smuggled in by corrupt staff, by visitors, by drones, and the profits are split between the inmates and the people who supply the phones. The Justice Department's decision to highlight these cases is a signal that the crackdown is not only aimed at the international compounds. It is aimed at every link in the chain, including the ones inside American prisons.

The other schemes named in the announcement — sextortion, extortion, impersonation — are the ones that do the most damage to the most vulnerable people. Sextortion, in which a victim is tricked into sharing intimate images and then blackmailed, has become one of the fastest-growing crimes in the country, and it disproportionately targets teenagers and young adults. Impersonation scams, in which the caller pretends to be from the IRS, the Social Security Administration, a bank or a tech company, exploit trust in institutions. And romance scams, in which a fraudster builds a relationship with a victim over weeks or months before asking for money, exploit loneliness. Each of these is a different kind of cruelty, and each of them is now being run at industrial scale.

The March executive order

None of this happened by accident. The crackdown follows an executive order signed by President Trump in March, directing a government-wide campaign against fraud and predatory schemes targeting Americans. The order set in motion the coordination that produced Tuesday's announcement: the Justice Department filing cases, the FBI running Operation Blackout, and other agencies — including, reportedly, the Treasury, Homeland Security and the State Department — contributing intelligence, sanctions and diplomatic pressure. Blanche was explicit that the order was the starting point. "Since President Trump signed the executive order, the Department of Justice has taken action to protect Americans from scams," he said, and the figures he announced were the results of that action.

The order matters for a second reason: it gave the campaign a legal and bureaucratic framework that previous efforts lacked. Fraud enforcement in the United States has historically been fragmented — the FBI investigates, the Justice Department prosecutes, the Federal Trade Commission handles consumer complaints, state attorneys general run their own cases, and foreign partners operate under their own laws. The executive order was designed to force those pieces to work together, and the announcement on Tuesday was, in part, a demonstration that they had. The presence of the FBI Director alongside the Attorney General, and the emphasis on international partners and interagency cooperation, was the point: this was not one agency's work. It was the whole government's.

Whether that coordination lasts is another question. Executive orders can be reversed, priorities can shift, and the fraud industry adapts quickly. The scammers who ran the 29 dismantled compounds will build new ones. The websites that impersonated federal courts will be replaced by new websites. The phones smuggled into Georgia prisons will be replaced by new phones. The question is whether the enforcement effort can keep pace — and whether the political will that produced Tuesday's announcement will still be there in a year.

What happens to the money

One question the announcement did not fully answer is what happens to the $8 billion. Recovering money is not the same as returning it, and the process of getting seized funds back to victims is slow, complicated and often incomplete. Some of the money was seized in cryptocurrency, some in bank accounts in multiple countries, some in cash. Some of it may be subject to forfeiture proceedings that take years. Some of it may never be traced to specific victims at all. The Justice Department did not say how much of the $8 billion will be distributed to victims, or when. For the people who lost their savings, that is the question that matters most — and it is the one that remains unanswered.

The $17 billion seized in Operation Blackout raises the same question on a larger scale. Much of that money was seized abroad, in joint operations with foreign partners, and its legal status is even more complicated. Some of it may be held in countries whose laws do not provide for returning it to American victims. Some of it may be tied up in litigation for years. The announcement was, in that sense, a statement of what has been stopped rather than what has been repaired. The theft has been interrupted. The damage has not yet been undone.

That does not make the announcement meaningless. Stopping $8 billion in intended losses is a real achievement, even if the money is never returned — because every dollar that was not stolen is a dollar that stayed in an American household. And the 11,000 Americans who were directly warned that they were being scammed are 11,000 people who may have kept their savings because the FBI called them in time. But the gap between "recovered" and "returned" is one that victims' advocates will be watching closely in the months ahead.

How to protect yourself

The Justice Department's announcement was also, implicitly, a warning: the scams are still out there, and the crackdown has not ended them. The most effective protection remains the simplest. No government agency — not the IRS, not the Social Security Administration, not the FBI, not a federal court — will call you and demand immediate payment by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency. No legitimate sweepstakes will ask you to pay a fee to collect a prize. No tech company will call you out of the blue to tell you your computer has a virus. And no romantic partner you have never met in person should be asking you for money, however convincing the story.

If you are contacted by someone claiming to be from a government agency or a company, hang up and call the organisation back on a number you find yourself — not one the caller gives you. If you receive a text or email with a link, do not click it; go to the organisation's website directly. If someone pressures you to act immediately, that is itself a red flag: urgency is the scammer's most powerful tool. And if you think you have been scammed, report it — to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center, to the Federal Trade Commission, and to your local police. Reporting is how the next crackdown gets its targets, and it is how victims get counted.

For families, the most important step may be the simplest: talk about it. The scams work in part because victims are ashamed to admit they were fooled, and because families do not discuss the risk until after the money is gone. A conversation now — with an elderly parent, a teenager, a spouse — is worth more than any warning after the fact. The Gallup figure, one in ten households, means that almost everyone knows someone who has been scammed. The question is whether they know it before or after it happens to them.

What happens next

The Justice Department says the campaign will continue. Blanche said the department is "not just going after individuals" but is "dismantling the criminal networks that have been stealing from the American people for far too long." Patel said the FBI had made dismantling predatory scam enterprises "a top priority." The language is the language of a campaign that intends to keep going — more cases, more extraditions, more compounds dismantled, more victims warned. Whether that happens will depend on resources, on international cooperation, and on whether the political attention that produced Tuesday's announcement is sustained.

There are also questions the announcement did not address. How many of the 800 defendants will actually be convicted? How many will be extradited from countries that do not cooperate with the United States? How much of the $8 billion will reach victims? And how quickly will the fraud industry rebuild what was destroyed? The history of organised crime suggests that it will rebuild quickly — that new compounds will replace the 29 that were dismantled, that new websites will replace the ones that were taken down, and that new phones will find their way into prisons. The crackdown has shown that the government can hit the industry hard. It has not yet shown that it can keep it down.

For now, though, the numbers stand: 400 cases, 85 districts, 800 defendants, $8 billion recovered, $17 billion seized, 29 compounds dismantled, 2,200 trafficking victims freed, 11,000 Americans warned. It is, as Blanche said, the largest anti-scam enforcement surge in American history. The test will be what happens next — whether the surge becomes a sustained campaign, or whether it is remembered as a single day of impressive numbers in a war that is still being fought.
