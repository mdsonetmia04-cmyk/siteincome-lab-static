TITLE: Judges Tell Prosecutors to Drop Netanyahu's $500 Million Bribery Case — "Chances of Conviction Are Low" After 6 Years LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Israeli judges urged prosecutors to drop the bribery case against Benjamin Netanyahu, saying a conviction is unlikely after six years of trial ---BODY---

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu with US House Speaker Mike Johnson in Washington on July 8, 2025. Judges in Jerusalem have urged prosecutors to drop the bribery case against him. Photo: Office of Speaker Mike Johnson via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

Israel's longest-serving prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu, won the biggest legal victory of his life on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, when the three judges hearing his corruption trial told prosecutors that the most serious case against him should be dropped. In an extraordinary hearing at the Jerusalem District Court, the panel — presided over by Judge Rivka Friedman-Feldman, with Judges Moshe Bar-Am and Oded Shaham — summoned Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara to court in a highly unusual step, and told her that a conviction in Case 4000, the bribery case at the heart of the prosecution, was unlikely, and that the case should be abandoned. "The chances of conviction are low — to an extent that warrants reconsidering the matter," Friedman-Feldman told the attorney general. "The bribery charge presents considerable difficulties," the court said, according to a spokesperson. "There is serious doubt as to whether the evidentiary foundation supports this allegation."

It was, by any measure, a stunning development, and it was one that Netanyahu seized on within hours. In a televised address from the Israeli parliament, the Knesset, he declared that the "fabricated cases" against him were falling apart, and that the trial, which has now run for more than six years, must end immediately. "Ten years of terrible suffering for me, my family," he said. "Ten years of incitement to hatred that wounds the soul. Who will give those years back to us?" His allies were even more triumphant. The defence minister, Israel Katz, called on President Isaac Herzog to act at once to bring the trial to a close. The national security minister, Itamar Ben-Gvir, called the prosecution a "deep state campaign of persecution." The finance minister, Bezalel Smotrich, said the development was "an earthquake measuring 4,000 on the Richter scale." But the judges were careful to say what they had not done: they had not issued a verdict, they had not acquitted Netanyahu, they had not found that he had committed no wrongdoing, and they had not concluded that the prosecution was politically motivated. What they had done was something narrower, and, for the prosecution, something devastating: they had told the attorney general, in open court, that the state's most serious case was, in their assessment, unlikely to succeed.

This is the story of a trial that has dominated Israeli politics for seven years, of the three cases that make it up, of the hearing that changed it, and of what happens next — for Netanyahu, for the prosecution, and for a country that is, once again, deeply divided over its prime minister.

The trial that has shadowed Israel for seven years

Benjamin Netanyahu, 77, has been in power on and off for three decades, and he is, by a wide margin, Israel's longest-serving prime minister. He was first charged in November 2019, while still in office, and his trial opened in May 2020, in a courtroom in Jerusalem, with the prime minister sitting in the dock. He has, throughout, denied any wrongdoing, and denounced the cases as a politically motivated plot against him. The trial has run, in fits and starts, for more than six years, through wars, elections, and a political crisis that has, in recent months, left Israel with a caretaker government. It has, in the words of one Israeli newspaper, "divided Israeli society and exacted a heavy price from the nation."

The prosecution's case rests on three separate indictments, known, in the shorthand of Israeli politics, as Cases 1000, 2000 and 4000. In each of them, Netanyahu faces charges of fraud and breach of trust. In Case 4000, and only in Case 4000, he also faces a charge of bribery, which carries the most serious potential prison sentence, and which has, from the beginning, been the backbone of the prosecution's case. The three cases are, in their details, very different, but they share a common theme: that Netanyahu, as prime minister, used the power of his office to benefit himself, and the people who could help him, and that he did so in ways that crossed the line from politics into crime.

Case 4000, the most serious of the three, alleges that Netanyahu granted regulatory favours worth around 1.8 billion shekels — about US$500 million, according to Reuters — to Bezeq Telecom Israel, the country's largest telecommunications company, and to its then-controlling shareholder, the millionaire Shaul Elovitch, in exchange for favourable coverage on the Walla news website, which Elovitch also controlled. The alleged exchange was, in the prosecution's telling, a straightforward quid pro quo: regulatory decisions that were worth hundreds of millions of shekels to Elovitch, in return for a news website that would treat the prime minister kindly. Netanyahu, the prosecution said, had advanced regulatory decisions benefiting Bezeq and Elovitch, and had done so while Elovitch's news site was, in return, giving him the coverage he wanted.

Case 2000 concerns a different media bargain. Prosecutors allege that Netanyahu held recorded conversations with Arnon Mozes, the publisher of Yedioth Ahronoth, one of Israel's top newspapers, in which the two men discussed a deal: favourable coverage for Netanyahu in Yedioth Ahronoth, in exchange for steps by the prime minister to limit the circulation of the newspaper's main rival, Israel Hayom, a free daily owned by the late American casino magnate Sheldon Adelson, a Netanyahu supporter. The conversations were recorded, and they are, in the prosecution's case, the central evidence. But the judges, on Wednesday, questioned whether the conduct they described constituted a criminal offence at all.

Case 1000, the third and smallest of the three, alleges that Netanyahu received expensive gifts — fine wines, jewellery, and other luxury goods worth hundreds of thousands of shekels — from the Hollywood producer Arnon Milchan and the Australian businessman James Packer, and that he helped them with their interests while in office. It is, by the judges' own account, the case that remains the most legally viable, and it is the case that they suggested should be resolved quickly, either by a verdict or by a negotiated agreement.

The hearing that changed everything

The hearing on Wednesday was, by the standards of Israeli justice, extraordinary. The judges summoned Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara to appear in person at the Jerusalem District Court — a highly unusual step, taken, the court said, because she is the person with the authority to decide how the prosecution should proceed. With Netanyahu's testimony now complete, the presiding judge said, the trial had reached a point at which the court needed to consider how to move forward. What followed was a frank and, for the prosecution, brutal assessment of the state of its case.

On Case 4000, the judges were blunt. They said that the bribery charge "presents considerable difficulties," and that the quid pro quo at the heart of the allegation — the exchange of regulatory favours for favourable coverage — rested, in the main, on a single meeting at which instructions were allegedly given. They expressed serious doubt that prosecutors had established the alleged "unusual responsiveness" of the Walla news site to Netanyahu's coverage requests, questioning the comparison against which that responsiveness was being measured. And they identified difficulties on both sides of the alleged exchange: on the side of the regulatory favours, and on the side of the coverage. "Regarding this count, we assess that the prospects of conviction are low, to a degree that justifies reconsidering the matter," they wrote. "In our view, the public interest requires a rethink," Friedman-Feldman said.

The judges' doubts were, in part, the result of the testimony they had heard. A key witness for the prosecution, Shlomo Filber, the former director-general of the Communications Ministry, had given an account of the timeline of events that, the judges found, had proved contradictory, weakening the prosecution's central argument. The inconsistencies in Filber's testimony, combined with the narrow evidentiary base for the alleged quid pro quo, had, in the judges' view, left the bribery charge on shaky ground. It was, notably, the third time the judges had recommended that the prosecution drop the bribery charge. They had first made the recommendation in chambers in June 2023, and repeated it this past June. In September, after hearing Netanyahu's own testimony, they had said that their position remained as it was in June 2023. On Wednesday, they went further than they had before: their proposal to consider withdrawing the first count — the fraud and breach of trust charge in Case 4000 as well as the bribery — went beyond their earlier recommendations, which had concerned the bribery count alone.

On Case 2000, the judges were, if anything, more sceptical. They questioned whether the conduct alleged — Netanyahu's discussions with Mozes about favourable coverage in exchange for steps against a rival newspaper — constituted a criminal offence at all, and they recommended that the prosecution reconsider the case. Their doubts, the Jerusalem Post reported, extended beyond the bribery charge they had previously urged prosecutors to reconsider, and they suggested that the case, as a whole, was on uncertain legal ground.

On Case 1000, the gifts case, the judges took a different view. They indicated that the prosecution remained legally viable, and they suggested that it should move towards an expedited verdict or a negotiated resolution. The court, in other words, was not saying that Netanyahu had done nothing wrong. It was saying that, of the three cases against him, only one — the smallest — was likely to end in a conviction, and that the other two, including the most serious, should be reconsidered or abandoned.

The judges were careful, throughout, to stress the limits of what they were saying. Their comments, they said, reflected their impressions so far, and not a verdict. They concerned Netanyahu alone, since they had not yet heard the other defendants' testimony. And withdrawing the charges against Netanyahu would not automatically end the bribery prosecution against Shaul and Iris Elovitch, whose liability would require separate consideration. The court, in short, had not acquitted anyone. It had offered an assessment, and a recommendation, and it had left the decision to the attorney general. But the assessment was, in the words of the Times of Israel, "a massive blow to the prosecution," and the recommendation was, in the words of CNN, "an enormous win for Netanyahu."

"The trial must end immediately"

Netanyahu did not wait to claim victory. In a televised address from the Knesset later on Wednesday, he declared that the cases against him had effectively collapsed, and that the trial must end at once. "It took 10 years of terrible suffering for me, my wife and my sons for the truth to finally be revealed to everyone," he said. "Who will give us back those years, during which my sons couldn't go out to a restaurant or walk around the city?" He repeated his longstanding claim that the allegations were politically motivated, and that there had been nothing improper in his conduct, and he said, in words that were carried around the world, "The trial must end immediately."

His allies were quick to follow. The defence minister, Israel Katz, a Likud colleague, said in a statement on X that "after years of persecution against Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, the time has come to put an end to a trial that was fundamentally flawed from its inception, a trial that divides Israeli society and exacts a heavy price from the nation," and he called on President Isaac Herzog "to take immediate action to bring this trial to a close." The national security minister, Itamar Ben-Gvir, of the far-right Otzma Yehudit party, lauded the court's suggestion in a post on X, calling the trial a "deep state campaign of persecution," and writing that "the remarks the judges are directing at Attorney-General Gali Baharav-Miara, following nearly a full decade of persecution against the prime minister, solely because he leads the national camp, should shock every citizen of the State of Israel." The finance minister, Bezalel Smotrich, of the Religious Zionist Party, said that "the drama currently unfolding in the Netanyahu trial is an earthquake measuring 4,000 on the Richter scale," and that "the judges are confirming what we have been arguing for the past 10 years. There is nothing there, because there was never anything there." The head of the ultra-Orthodox Shas party, Arye Deri, called the recommendations "the Judiciary's Yom Kippur," a reference to the Jewish Day of Atonement. Even Netanyahu's son Yair joined in, saying that the judicial case against his father had collapsed.

But the celebrations, as several Israeli newspapers were quick to point out, went further than the judges had gone. What the judges did not do, the Jerusalem Post noted, was "issue a verdict, find that Netanyahu had not committed the acts attributed to him, endorse the alleged conduct, or conclude that the prosecution was fabricated or politically motivated, as several politicians claimed." The court had questioned the viability of particular charges, and it had urged the prosecution to reconsider them. It had not said that Netanyahu was innocent, and it had not said that the trial should be abandoned in its entirety. The distinction matters, and it is one that Netanyahu's critics were quick to draw. Weaknesses in particular charges, they argued, do not amount to an acquittal, and they do not bring an end to the wider political crisis surrounding the prime minister's government.

A caretaker prime minister fighting for survival

The judges' intervention came at a moment of acute political vulnerability for Netanyahu. Israel held legislative elections on October 27, 2026, and they were, by every account, inconclusive. Netanyahu, who has led the country for most of the past three decades, is now serving as caretaker prime minister, and his main rival, Gadi Eisenkot, a former military chief who now leads the parliamentary opposition, has been tasked with forming a government. Eisenkot's parliamentary bloc has voted in favour of conducting an independent investigation into the October 7, 2023, Hamas attack, a subject on which Netanyahu has long resisted scrutiny. If Eisenkot succeeds in forming a government and ousting Netanyahu and his far-right partners after nearly four years in power, Netanyahu would lose not only the premiership but also the leverage that, his critics say, has helped him avoid a potential jail sentence.

It is in that context that Netanyahu's response to the judges' recommendation must be understood. In his televised remarks on Wednesday, he repeated that he was prepared to form a unity government with the parliamentary opposition — an offer that, if accepted, would keep him in office and, his critics say, keep him out of prison. His allies' demands that the trial be dismissed outright, and that President Herzog intervene to end it, are, in the same way, not just legal arguments. They are political ones, made by a prime minister and a coalition that are fighting for their survival, and that see in the judges' words a chance to end not just a trial but a political crisis.

For Eisenkot and the opposition, the calculation is different. If Netanyahu is no longer prime minister, he would face the remaining charges — above all, Case 1000, the gifts case, which the judges said remained viable — as a private citizen, without the protections and the leverage of office. That is a prospect that Netanyahu's opponents have long awaited, and it is one that the judges' recommendation, paradoxically, may bring closer. By weakening the prosecution's case in the two most serious matters, the judges have, in effect, cleared the way for a quicker resolution of the trial as a whole — whether by a verdict in Case 1000, or by a negotiated plea agreement — and a quicker resolution is, for a caretaker prime minister whose political future is uncertain, a double-edged sword.

There is also a practical constraint that the judges themselves noted. Judge Friedman-Feldman, who heads the panel, is due to retire in March 2028, and the trial, which has already run for more than six years, cannot go on indefinitely. The judges said that the key evidence had been presented to the court, and that the case should be wrapped up quickly, alluding to some form of agreement between the prosecution and Netanyahu's defence team. Attorney General Baharav-Miara, for her part, told the judges that she would consider their recommendations, and that she was ready for either a verdict or a plea deal. "There are only two ways to bring the case to a conclusion," she said during the hearing, "a verdict or an appropriate plea agreement that reflects the nature of the acts and offenses."

What the three cases mean — and what they do not

To understand the significance of Wednesday's hearing, it helps to understand what the three cases were, and what they were not. The prosecution's case against Netanyahu was never, in the strictest sense, a case about money. It was a case about power — about the use of the prime minister's office to shape the media environment in which he operated, and about the exchange of favours, whether regulatory or political, for coverage that would help him stay in power. Case 4000, the bribery case, was the most ambitious version of that argument: that Netanyahu had, in effect, sold regulatory decisions worth hundreds of millions of shekels in exchange for a news website that would treat him well. Case 2000 was a smaller version of the same argument: that he had discussed a deal with a newspaper publisher in which favourable coverage would be traded for steps against a rival. Case 1000 was the most personal: that he had accepted expensive gifts from wealthy friends, and helped them in return.

The judges' assessment, on Wednesday, was that the first two versions of that argument had not been proved, and were unlikely to be proved. The evidence for the quid pro quo in Case 4000 was, in their view, too thin, too dependent on a single meeting and a single witness whose account had proved contradictory, and too uncertain in its measurement of what, exactly, the news site had done for the prime minister. The conduct alleged in Case 2000 was, in their view, of doubtful criminality. But the third version — the gifts — remained, in their view, a case that could be proved, and that should be resolved. It is, in that sense, a mixed verdict, and it is one that neither side can claim as a complete victory.

For Netanyahu's supporters, the judges' words were a vindication, and proof of what they have argued for a decade: that the cases were a political witch-hunt, mounted by an unelected legal establishment against a democratically elected leader. For his critics, the judges' words were a warning, and a reminder that a trial is not a referendum on a politician, and that the weaknesses of particular charges do not amount to an acquittal, and do not bring an end to the wider political crisis surrounding the prime minister's government.

What happens next

The decision now rests with Gali Baharav-Miara, and it is not an easy one. She has, in the judges' own words, "the authority to decide how the prosecution should proceed," and she has said that she will review the court's statements with the prosecution team. She has three broad options. She can accept the judges' recommendation and drop Case 4000, in whole or in part, and reconsider Case 2000, leaving only Case 1000 to be resolved. She can reject the recommendation and press on with all three cases, arguing that the judges' assessment, however forceful, is preliminary, and that the prosecution's case is stronger than the panel believes. Or she can seek a negotiated resolution — a plea agreement, in which Netanyahu would admit to some of the charges in exchange for a reduced sentence, and the trial would come to an end without a verdict.

Each option carries risks. Dropping the most serious case would be, for the prosecution, an admission of failure on the charge that it had presented, for seven years, as the centrepiece of its case, and it would be seen, by Netanyahu's critics, as a capitulation. Pressing on, in the face of the judges' assessment, would risk a humiliating acquittal, and a verdict that would vindicate Netanyahu more completely than any press conference could. A plea agreement, which Baharav-Miara said she was ready to consider, would bring the trial to a close, but it would require Netanyahu to admit to conduct that he has, for seven years, denied, and it would require the prosecution to accept a sentence that his supporters would call a victory and his critics would call a sell-out.

There is also the question of the other defendants. The judges were explicit that their assessment concerned Netanyahu alone, and that they had not yet heard the testimony of Shaul and Iris Elovitch, the owners of Bezeq and Walla, or of Arnon Mozes, the publisher of Yedioth Ahronoth. Withdrawing the charges against Netanyahu would not automatically end the prosecutions against them, and their cases, if they proceed, will keep the underlying allegations — of regulatory favours traded for coverage, of deals discussed in recorded conversations — in the public eye, long after Netanyahu's own case is resolved. The trial, in other words, is not over, even if the case against its most famous defendant is, in part, abandoned.

And there is the political clock. Netanyahu is a caretaker prime minister, fighting to form a government after inconclusive elections, and facing a rival, Gadi Eisenkot, who has been tasked with forming a government of his own. The judges' recommendation has given Netanyahu a powerful political weapon, and he has already used it, in a televised address, to declare that the trial must end. But it has also, by weakening the prosecution's case, brought the trial closer to a conclusion — and a conclusion, whether by verdict or by plea, is a moment that Netanyahu, for seven years, has managed to avoid. The next weeks will determine whether the judges' intervention was, as his allies claim, the beginning of the end of the trial, or, as his critics hope, the beginning of its endgame.

Seven years, three cases, one question

It is worth, at the end, stepping back from the legal arguments and the political manoeuvring, and remembering what this trial has been about, and why it has mattered for so long. For seven years, Israel has been divided over a single question: whether its longest-serving prime minister used the power of his office for his own benefit, and whether that use crossed the line into crime. The question has shaped elections, toppled governments, and divided families. It has been, in the words of the defence minister, a trial that "divides Israeli society and exacts a heavy price from the nation." And it has, for seven years, had no answer.

Wednesday's hearing did not provide that answer. What it provided was an assessment, by three judges who have spent six years listening to the evidence, that the prosecution's most serious case was unlikely to succeed, and that the second case was of doubtful criminality, and that only the third — the smallest — remained viable. It was, in that sense, a partial answer, and a provisional one, and it was one that the judges themselves were careful to qualify. But it was also, by any measure, a turning point, and it was one that will shape everything that follows: the attorney general's decision, the fate of the remaining charges, the political future of a caretaker prime minister, and the long, unresolved argument about what Benjamin Netanyahu did, and whether it was a crime.

For Netanyahu, the hearing was a vindication, and he claimed it as one. For the prosecution, it was a blow, and it will be felt for a long time. For Israel, it was a reminder that the trial, which has shadowed the country's politics for seven years, is not over, and that its ending, when it comes, will be as contested as its beginning. The judges have spoken. The attorney general must now decide. And the country, which has waited seven years for an answer, must wait a little longer.
