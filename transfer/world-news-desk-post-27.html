TITLE: Huawei Found Guilty on 10 of 12 Counts — Billions in Fines Loom After a 7-Year US Battle LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A Brooklyn jury convicted Huawei on 10 of 12 counts, including bank fraud and stealing US trade secrets, ending a seven-year criminal case. ---BODY---

Huawei founder Ren Zhengfei. His company was convicted in a US federal court on November 24, 2026. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0).

A federal jury in Brooklyn has convicted Huawei Technologies, one of China's most powerful companies, of criminal fraud and stealing American trade secrets — closing a case that has run for almost seven years, cost tens of millions of dollars in legal fees, and sat at the very centre of the technology war between the United States and China. On Tuesday, November 24, 2026, after a two-month trial and five days of deliberation, the jury found Huawei and three of its subsidiaries guilty on 10 of the 12 criminal counts they faced, including bank fraud, wire fraud, violating United States sanctions, and conspiring to steal trade secrets from American companies. The company was acquitted on the two most serious charges — racketeering conspiracy and conspiracy to obstruct justice — a result its lawyers immediately described as a partial victory. But the convictions carry the threat of penalties that could run into the billions of dollars, and sentencing is set for September 14, 2027, before US District Judge Ann Donnelly in Brooklyn.

The verdict is the most significant courtroom defeat Huawei has ever suffered in the United States, and it arrives at a moment when the company is already fighting on several fronts at once: barred from much of the American market, cut off from the most advanced computer chips, and caught in the middle of a trade and technology rivalry that has only grown sharper. For the American prosecutors who brought the case, the verdict is a vindication of an argument they have made since January 2019 — that Huawei built its global empire, at least in part, by breaking the law. For Huawei, which has always denied wrongdoing and says its success came from engineering and investment, it is a blow that it has already said it will appeal. And for everyone watching the contest between the world's two largest economies, it is a reminder that the fight over technology is now being waged in courtrooms as much as in boardrooms.

What the jury decided

The jury's decision, delivered after deliberations that began on the afternoon of November 18, was neither a total victory for the prosecution nor a clean sweep for the defence. On 10 of the 12 counts, the jury found Huawei guilty. Those counts covered bank fraud, wire fraud, conspiracy to violate United States sanctions, conspiracy to steal trade secrets, conspiracy to launder money, and violations of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, the law under which American sanctions are enforced. On the other two counts, the jury found the company not guilty: the charge of racketeering conspiracy, brought under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act — known as RICO — which alleged that Huawei had operated as a criminal enterprise, and the charge of conspiracy to obstruct justice.

The split verdict matters, and both sides know it. The RICO charge was the most serious and the most consequential in the indictment, because it framed Huawei not as a company that had committed individual offences, but as an organisation that was corrupt from top to bottom. Huawei's lawyer, Brian Heberlig, told reporters after the verdict that the acquittal on that charge was a "profound rejection" of the government's narrative that the company had a corporate culture of cheating, stealing and lying. The company said it was disappointed by the convictions and planned to appeal them. But the acquittals do not erase the convictions, and the convictions are the heart of the case: a jury of ordinary Americans, after hearing more than 60 prosecution witnesses and 15 defence witnesses over two months, concluded that Huawei had deceived banks, evaded sanctions and stolen the secrets of its competitors.

The trial itself was an enormous undertaking. The jury had been hearing the case since September 8, 2026, and the proceedings involved dozens of lawyers, hundreds of motions and filings, and millions of documents. The original indictment was filed by the United States Justice Department in January 2019, and prosecutors added new charges in 2020, including the trade secret theft allegations that stretched back, in the government's telling, over two decades. By the time the verdict arrived, the case had consumed nearly seven years of litigation, and the legal bills on both sides ran into the tens of millions of dollars. The judge has set September 14, 2027, as the date for sentencing, when the company will learn the financial penalties it faces. Motions and responses in advance of sentencing are scheduled to begin in the third week of January.

The bank fraud charges: Iran, Skycom and three global banks

At the centre of the financial charges was a single question: did Huawei lie to banks about its business in Iran? According to the 2018 indictment, Huawei misled three of the world's biggest banks — HSBC, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas — about the extent of its dealings in Iran, a country under American sanctions. The prosecutors' case rested on a company called Skycom Tech Co., a business operating in Iran that, the government said, Huawei controlled while pretending it did not. By concealing its control over Skycom, prosecutors argued, Huawei was able to move money through the international banking system in ways that would have been blocked if the banks had known the truth about who was really behind the transactions.

The scheme, as prosecutors described it, was deliberate and sustained. Huawei employees, they said, took steps to evade sanctions and lied to banks about the work the company was doing in Iran, presenting Skycom as an independent local partner when it was, in reality, a Huawei operation. The banks, relying on Huawei's assurances, processed transactions that they would otherwise have refused. The fraud charges followed from those lies: bank fraud for deceiving the banks, wire fraud for using the American financial system to do it, and violations of the sanctions laws themselves. The jury agreed, finding Huawei guilty on the fraud and sanctions counts.

The Iran allegations were also the oldest part of the case, and the part that first brought Huawei into the sights of American law enforcement. The company's chief financial officer, Meng Wanzhou — the daughter of founder Ren Zhengfei — was arrested in Canada in December 2018 at the request of the United States, on charges connected to the same alleged deception of banks over Iran. She spent almost three years under house arrest in Vancouver before reaching a deferred prosecution agreement with the Justice Department in September 2021, which allowed her to return to China. The criminal case against the company itself, however, continued, and it is that case which has now ended in conviction.

The trade secret charges: Tappy, Cisco and a stolen robot

If the bank fraud charges were about money, the trade secret charges were about how Huawei became a technology giant in the first place — and they produced some of the most striking evidence of the trial. Prosecutors accused Huawei of conspiring over two decades to steal the technology of American competitors, and they brought the jury a series of episodes, each one a small story of alleged theft, that together painted a picture of a systematic programme.

The most famous of those episodes involved a robot called Tappy. T-Mobile, the American mobile carrier, had built Tappy to test its smartphones, pressing touchscreens again and again to simulate months of use. According to prosecutors, Huawei gained access to a T-Mobile laboratory and stole technical data about the robot — and one Huawei engineer, they said, took photographs of Tappy and slipped a piece of the robot into his backpack and walked out with it. Jurors were shown surveillance footage that prosecutors said captured the moment. It was a small theft in itself, but prosecutors used it as an emblem of a much larger pattern: a company that took what it could not build.

The other episodes were bigger. Prosecutors said Huawei stole source code from Cisco Systems for its routers — the software that directs traffic across computer networks, and one of the most valuable pieces of intellectual property in the technology industry. They said the company stole antenna technology from Quintel, a wireless telecommunications company. And they said Huawei hired a professor to gain access to a circuit board developed by CNEX, a semiconductor company. In each case, the government argued, Huawei obtained technology that had cost its American rivals years of research and millions of dollars, and used it to accelerate its own rise.

There was also evidence, prosecutors said, that Huawei employees themselves treated the theft as a point of pride. One prosecutor told the jury that Huawei employees stole trade secrets from other companies and bragged about the thefts on an informal company forum — an internal message board where engineers swapped stories of what they had taken. Huawei's lawyers argued that the prosecutors were criminalising ordinary business activity, and that some of the material the government called trade secrets was publicly available, or was not secret at all. The jury, after five days of deliberation, sided with the prosecution on the trade secret conspiracy count.

Seven years in the making

The case against Huawei has been running for so long that it has outlasted presidencies, trade wars and the transformation of the technology industry itself. When the Justice Department filed its first indictment in January 2019, Huawei was the world's second-largest smartphone maker and the largest supplier of the equipment that carries mobile phone calls and internet data around the world. The United States had already begun to argue that Huawei's equipment posed a security risk, and that the company's closeness to the Chinese government made it a threat. The criminal charges added a new dimension: not just that Huawei was dangerous, but that it was dishonest.

The years since have only deepened the conflict. The United States has barred Huawei from its networks, pressed its allies to do the same, and cut the company off from the most advanced semiconductors — the chips that power modern phones, computers and artificial intelligence systems. Huawei, in response, has poured money into building its own chips and its own software, and has survived, even thrived, in the Chinese market, where it remains one of the country's premier international brands. The criminal trial, running through the autumn of 2026, was a reminder that the American case against Huawei was never only about national security. It was also, in the end, a case about fraud.

The timing of the verdict carried its own significance. The trial in New York overlapped with a summit in Shenzhen — the technology-centric Chinese city where Huawei is headquartered — between President Donald Trump, China's President Xi Jinping and Russia's President Vladimir Putin. The meeting, on November 19, came months after the last nuclear arms treaty between the United States and Russia expired, and it was billed as a discussion of the world's biggest powers. That a trial of China's flagship technology company was concluding in a Brooklyn courtroom at the same moment its leaders were meeting in Huawei's home city was a coincidence that nobody in either capital is likely to have missed.

What happens next: billions, an appeal and a sentence

The most immediate question now is money. Huawei faces potentially billions of dollars in penalties when Judge Donnelly sentences the company in September 2027, and the scale of the fine will depend on how the judge weighs the convictions, the duration of the conduct, and the company's ability to pay. The case involved no human defendants — the company itself was on trial — so there will be no prison sentences. But a corporate fine of the size prosecutors are likely to seek would be one of the largest ever imposed on a technology company, and it would land on a business that has already spent heavily on surviving American sanctions.

Beyond the fine, the company faces regulatory restrictions, and the verdict will almost certainly be used by the American government to justify further measures against Huawei and against other Chinese technology companies. The conviction gives the United States a court-tested finding that Huawei broke the law — a finding that will be cited in Washington, in allied capitals, and in the boardrooms of companies deciding whether to do business with Huawei. For a company that has spent years arguing that the campaign against it is political, the verdict is a serious setback, because it moves the argument from politics to law.

Huawei, for its part, has said it will appeal. Its lawyers have several lines of attack available: they can challenge the admissibility of evidence, the instructions given to the jury, and the legal basis of the charges themselves. The appeal process could take years, and the company will argue, as it did at trial, that its commercial growth resulted from engineering, investment and research, not from theft. The acquittal on the racketeering charge will be central to that argument. Huawei's lawyers will present it as proof that the government's most sweeping claims were rejected, and that the company was not the criminal enterprise prosecutors described. Whether an appeals court agrees is a question for the future — and, given the scale of the case, one that is likely to be argued all the way to the highest levels of the American judiciary.

Why the verdict matters for the technology war

To understand why this verdict matters so much, it helps to understand what Huawei is. Founded in 1987 by Ren Zhengfei, a former engineer in the Chinese military, Huawei grew from a small maker of telephone switches into one of the largest technology companies in the world. It builds the equipment that carries mobile networks in more than 170 countries, it was for a time the world's biggest seller of smartphones, and it is one of the few companies on earth capable of building a complete fifth-generation — 5G — mobile network. Its rise was, for China, a source of enormous national pride, and proof that a Chinese company could compete with, and beat, the best of the West.

For the United States, Huawei's rise was a problem. American officials argued that the company's equipment could be used by the Chinese government to spy on other countries, and that its dominance of 5G would give Beijing a chokehold over the communications of the future. American technology giants, meanwhile, accused Huawei of stealing their business secrets — the same accusation that was at the heart of the criminal trial. The two arguments reinforced each other: a company that stole technology, the argument went, could not be trusted with the world's networks. Huawei has always denied both charges, insisting that it is independent of the Chinese government and that its success came from its own research. The verdict does not settle the spying question, which was not part of the trial. But it does settle, in a court of law, the question of whether Huawei broke American law — and the answer, on 10 of 12 counts, was yes.

The verdict also lands at a moment of acute tension in the technology relationship between the two countries. The United States has imposed new restrictions on the export of advanced artificial intelligence chips to China, and on China's access to Nvidia processors through overseas data centres — measures that prompted a warning from Beijing ahead of the Trump-Xi summit in Shenzhen. Nvidia's own story has been intertwined with Huawei's: its chief executive, Jensen Huang, returned to lead the company in a dramatic reversal that has dominated the technology news in recent weeks. Huawei and Nvidia are, in many ways, the two poles of the artificial intelligence age — one American, one Chinese, each the champion of its own system. The conviction of one of them in an American court is a reminder of how far the rivalry has moved from the marketplace into the courtroom.

The view from China — and from Huawei's customers

In China, the verdict is likely to be received as confirmation of a long-held belief: that the American campaign against Huawei was never really about the law, but about stopping a Chinese champion from competing. Chinese officials and state media have argued for years that the United States uses its legal system as a weapon against Chinese companies, and the conviction will be presented at home as the latest example. That framing matters, because it shapes how the Chinese government responds — and a government that believes its flagship company has been unfairly targeted is more likely to retaliate against American companies operating in China.

For Huawei's customers, the calculation is more complicated. The company sells its equipment in countries across Asia, Africa, Europe and the Middle East, many of which have resisted American pressure to exclude it from their networks. Those governments will now have to weigh a court verdict against their own interests: the price of Huawei's equipment, which is often lower than its rivals, against the political cost of doing business with a company that an American jury has found guilty of fraud. Some may decide the verdict changes nothing. Others may decide that it gives them a reason to look elsewhere. Either way, the verdict adds a new factor to decisions that were already difficult.

There is also the question of what the verdict means for other Chinese technology companies. If Huawei can be convicted in an American court for conduct that stretched back two decades, other companies with similar histories — and similar dealings in sanctioned countries — will be wondering whether they are next. The Justice Department has shown, with this case, that it is willing to pursue a foreign company through years of litigation, at enormous cost, to secure a conviction. That is a message that will be heard in boardrooms from Beijing to Shenzhen.

What it means for ordinary people

For most people, the Huawei verdict will not change anything they can see. It will not make their phones cheaper or their internet faster. It will not, on its own, decide who builds the next generation of mobile networks. But it will shape the world those networks operate in, in ways that are worth understanding. The technology in your pocket — the phone, the apps, the cloud services — is built by a supply chain that runs through the United States and China, and the rules of that supply chain are being rewritten by cases like this one.

If the American government succeeds in using the courts to punish Chinese technology companies, the result is likely to be a world in which technology splits into two camps: one American, one Chinese, each with its own chips, its own software and its own networks. That is already happening. Huawei's phones no longer run Google's software. Its chips are increasingly made in China. And the artificial intelligence systems being built in the two countries are being trained on different hardware, under different rules. The verdict in Brooklyn is one more step in that direction — a step towards a world in which the technology you use depends on which side of a geopolitical divide you live on.

For the people who work at Huawei — more than 200,000 of them, most in China — the verdict is a personal blow as well as a corporate one. Many of them joined the company because they believed in its mission: to build world-class technology in China, and to prove that a Chinese company could be the best in the world at something. The jury's verdict does not erase what they built. Huawei's networks still carry calls in more than 170 countries, and its research — in 5G, in artificial intelligence, in the chips that power the future — is real and formidable. But the verdict says that some of what the company did to get there was against the law, and that is a stain that will not wash off easily.

The road ahead

The next steps in the case are procedural, but they matter. Motions and responses in advance of sentencing begin in the third week of January 2027, and the sentencing hearing itself is set for September 14, 2027. In the meantime, Huawei's lawyers will prepare their appeal, and the company's executives will decide how to respond to a verdict that has damaged its reputation in the West while, paradoxically, perhaps strengthening its standing at home, where it will be seen as a victim of American power.

The broader story, though, is not about one company or one verdict. It is about whether the world's two largest economies can find a way to compete in technology without tearing the industry apart. The United States and China are each other's largest trading partners in technology, and the companies of each country depend on the other for chips, for markets and for ideas. A criminal conviction does not end that interdependence, but it does make it harder to manage. The trial of Huawei lasted two months. The case lasted almost seven years. The rivalry it is part of will last much longer — and the verdict in Brooklyn is a reminder that, in that rivalry, the courtroom has become one of the most important battlegrounds of all.

For now, the facts are these: a jury in Brooklyn found Huawei guilty on 10 of 12 counts, including bank fraud, wire fraud, sanctions violations and conspiracy to steal trade secrets. The company was acquitted on racketeering and obstruction. Sentencing comes in September 2027, with potentially billions of dollars at stake, and an appeal is certain. Seven years after the case began, the American government has its conviction. What it does with it — and what China does in response — will shape the technology world for years to come.
