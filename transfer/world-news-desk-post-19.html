TITLE: 81.5 Million Americans Are Travelling for Thanksgiving — After Delta Delayed 1,600 Flights in One Day LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: 81.5 million Americans travel for Thanksgiving 2026 as the FAA expects a record 417,775 flights — days after Delta delayed 1,600 flights. ---BODY---

Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, the world's busiest airport and Delta's biggest hub, where more than 60 percent of flights were delayed on Sunday. Photo: Harrison Keely / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0).

Some 81.5 million Americans will travel at least 50 miles from home for Thanksgiving this year, according to AAA, and the skies above them will be the busiest in 16 years — with the Federal Aviation Administration expecting 417,775 commercial flights between Monday, November 23, and Tuesday, December 1. It is a holiday travel surge of historic scale, and it is arriving just days after the American aviation system showed, in three separate failures, how fragile it has become. On November 13, the FAA ordered the longest nationwide ground stop in roughly a quarter of a century, halting most US departures for more than four hours and disrupting more than 18,000 flights. On November 16, United Airlines suffered a widespread system outage that delayed departing flights at hubs including Chicago, Denver and San Francisco. And on Sunday, November 22 — one of the busiest travel Sundays before Thanksgiving — Delta Air Lines' automated weight-and-balance system failed, delaying more than 1,600 flights, about half of the airline's mainline schedule, and forcing ground crews to count bags by hand. Now, with the biggest travel week of the year under way, the government has assembled a special team to keep the system running, airlines are operating their most aggressive holiday schedules in years, and forecasters are warning of coastal storms on both coasts. Here is what the numbers say, what went wrong, and what travellers should expect.

The scale of the Thanksgiving rush is difficult to overstate. AAA projects that 81.5 million Americans will travel at least 50 miles from home between Tuesday, November 24, and Monday, November 30 — a figure only 0.1 percent below last year's 81.6 million, ending a three-year run of record Thanksgivings but remaining near historic highs. The Transportation Security Administration, which screens passengers at US airports, said it expects to screen nearly 17.6 million passengers nationwide over the same period, and more than 3 million travellers are expected on Sunday, November 29, alone — the single busiest day of the holiday. Airlines for America, the trade group representing US airlines, estimates that carriers will transport nearly 29 million passengers between November 20 and November 30, an average of approximately 2.7 million travellers a day. And the FAA expects roughly 417,000 commercial flights over the nine-day holiday window, a 16-year high, as airlines respond to sustained demand for family and leisure trips. Tuesday, November 24, is expected to be the busiest day for aircraft traffic, with more than 52,000 flights scheduled. The numbers are up 2.2 percent over last Thanksgiving and 7.5 percent compared with 2019, before the COVID-19 pandemic — a reminder that American air travel has not merely recovered from the pandemic. It has surpassed it.

Sunday's meltdown: 1,600 Delta flights delayed

The most alarming preview of what could go wrong came on Sunday, November 22, when Delta Air Lines suffered its own technology problem during one of the busiest Sundays before Thanksgiving. The airline's automated system for calculating aircraft weight and balance — the system called ALIS, the Automated Load Instruction System — malfunctioned, and the airline resorted to manual workarounds until the system was restored later in the day. According to flight tracking site FlightAware, more than 1,600 Delta flights were delayed on Sunday — about half of the airline's mainline schedule — and more than 40 flights were cancelled. As of 9:30 p.m. ET on Sunday, over half of Delta's total flights were delayed and 6 percent were cancelled. Delta said about 70 percent of Sunday's schedule changes were due to the automation issue, with the rest attributed to bad weather and air traffic slowdowns in New York and the Northeast.

The weight-and-balance system is one of those pieces of aviation infrastructure that passengers never think about and that airlines cannot fly without. Before a plane can take off, the airline must calculate precisely how its weight is distributed — how many passengers are aboard, how much fuel is loaded, and how much luggage is in the hold, and where it sits. Get it wrong, and the aircraft's centre of gravity shifts, its handling changes, and its safety margins shrink. The automated system does that calculation in seconds. When it failed on Sunday, Delta's ground crews had to do it by hand. A radio call between a Delta employee and a pilot in Boston on Sunday morning, captured by the air traffic control monitoring site ATC.com, laid out the problem plainly: "Right now, it's a system-wide outage for Delta Air Lines. The ramp isn't really able to load because there is no ramp worksheet in there, so that's what we're doing, we're manually counting bags and doing a report for you guys." Workers were, in other words, counting suitcases by hand and filling in paper forms, on one of the busiest travel days of the year, for one of the largest airlines in the world.

The pain was concentrated at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport, Delta's biggest hub and the busiest airport in the world. The FAA issued a ground delay for Delta flights into Atlanta, which was cancelled around 11:30 a.m. ET, according to an FAA advisory. More than 60 percent of flights out of Hartsfield-Jackson were delayed on Sunday evening, FlightAware said, and around half of inbound flights were delayed as well. At the Atlanta hub alone, there were 547 delays and seven cancellations, according to FlightAware, and four million of the travellers expected over the holiday period are expected to pass through Atlanta. The average delay was reported to be 57 minutes. Delta said on Sunday afternoon that the automation was working again, and USA Today reported that the automated systems were operational by about 3:30 p.m. ET on November 22. The FAA confirmed in a social media post that Sunday's Delta delays were not related to any FAA air traffic systems. But by then the damage was done: thousands of passengers had missed connections, spent hours on the tarmac or in terminals, and arrived at their destinations late — or not at all.

A system that keeps breaking: the ground stop and the United outage

The Delta failure did not happen in isolation. It was the third major aviation technology failure in less than two weeks, and the pattern is what has alarmed lawmakers, regulators and travellers alike. The first came on November 13, when the FAA ordered the longest nationwide ground stop in roughly a quarter of a century, halting most US departures for more than four hours while engineers traced a failure in a key network router. The outage, linked to work replacing older copper wiring with fibre-optic cable, disrupted more than 18,000 flights and stranded passengers across the country before normal operations were restored. It was, by the FAA's own account, the most serious nationwide disruption to American air travel in a generation, and it prompted members of Congress to demand answers about the reliability of the nation's ageing aviation infrastructure.

The second failure came on November 16, when United Airlines experienced a separate, widespread system outage that affected multiple applications used by flight crews and delayed departing flights at hubs including Chicago, Denver and San Francisco. The disruption lasted a little over an hour before United restored its systems and lifted a temporary pause on departures. It was shorter and less severe than the FAA ground stop, but it added to a growing sense that the systems underpinning American air travel were failing with worrying frequency — and that the failures were not connected to one another, which made them harder to explain and harder to prevent.

That is the crucial point. The three failures had three different causes: a router failure at the FAA, a crew-applications outage at United, and a weight-and-balance system failure at Delta. They were not the result of a single flaw, a single vendor or a single attack. They were, instead, a reminder of how dependent modern air travel has become on complex, interconnected automation — and how a failure in any one piece of that automation can ripple through the entire network in minutes. An airline that cannot calculate the weight of its aircraft cannot fly. An air traffic system that cannot route its planes cannot move them. A crew scheduling system that cannot reach its pilots cannot staff its flights. The system works, until it does not, and when it does not, the consequences are measured in thousands of delayed flights and hundreds of thousands of stranded passengers.

The government's answer: a special team, and new technology

The Trump administration has stepped up efforts to prevent a repeat of the recent technology failures, and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has become the public face of that effort. Duffy told Axios that the administration has assembled a specialised team to improve the reliability of air traffic control operations and deployed new technology to anticipate and manage congestion. "We've taken unprecedented action to limit flight delays and cancellations," Duffy said, according to Newsmax, and the White House said the new task force has been meeting daily over the past week. The Department of Transportation and the FAA say they have deployed special teams and newer forecasting tools ahead of the holiday to reduce knock-on disruption from weather and technology issues.

The FAA is also examining how to replace vulnerable system components and improve its ability to identify technical problems before they cause widespread flight disruptions. That is a longer-term project — the modernisation of America's air traffic control system has been discussed, promised and partially attempted for decades, and the system still runs on infrastructure that, in places, dates back generations. The router failure on November 13 was linked to work replacing older copper wiring with fibre-optic cable, a reminder that the modernisation is under way but incomplete, and that the transition itself can create new points of failure. For this Thanksgiving, the government's answer is not a rebuilt system. It is a task force, a set of forecasting tools, and a promise to watch closely.

Whether that is enough is the question that will be answered over the next week. The FAA expects 417,775 flights over the nine-day holiday period, and the system will be operating at or near its maximum capacity. Any failure — a router, a weight-and-balance system, a crew application, a storm — will have less slack to absorb it than at any other time of the year. The task force can respond faster, but it cannot create capacity that does not exist, and it cannot fix a system that breaks. What it can do is what it has promised: watch, anticipate and react. For millions of travellers, that will have to be enough.

The weather: storms on both coasts

As if the technology failures were not enough, the weather is adding its own pressure. Forecasters are warning of a coastal storm in the East and Pacific storms in the West, with heavy rain, high winds and flooding on both coasts just as millions of people try to reach holiday gatherings. AAA expects about 81.5 million people to travel this Thanksgiving week, and the weather threatens to complicate that journey at its busiest moments. A storm is forecast for the East Coast on Thursday — Thanksgiving Day itself — following rain in the Southeast on Wednesday, and Pacific storms are expected in the West. The FAA is monitoring forecasts that could disrupt flights, particularly in the eastern US and Midwest, and airlines are already warning passengers to expect delays.

Weather is the one disruption that no task force can prevent. It can be forecast, and it can be planned for, but it cannot be fixed, and when a major storm hits the Northeast on the busiest travel day of the year, the consequences are measured in cancelled flights and stranded passengers, whatever the technology does. The combination is what makes this Thanksgiving genuinely concerning: a system operating at record capacity, with a recent history of technology failures, facing severe weather on both coasts, at the exact moment when more Americans are travelling than at almost any other time in history. It is a stress test, and it is happening in real time.

The fares, the crowds, and the cost of going home

Thanksgiving travel in 2026 is not only busy. It is expensive. Airfares are higher than last year, and the combination of record demand, limited capacity and the airlines' aggressive holiday schedules has pushed prices up on the most popular routes. Low-cost and ultra-low-cost carriers are concentrating on high-yield routes rather than thinly served secondary airports, according to industry reporting, which means that travellers to smaller cities may find fewer options and higher prices. Delta and United are operating aggressive holiday schedules focused on key hubs, which means more flights through Atlanta, Chicago, Denver and San Francisco — and more congestion at precisely the airports that have already shown how badly things can go wrong.

For the 81.5 million Americans travelling this week, the journey is the point. Thanksgiving is the holiday of going home, of gathering with family, of crossing the country to sit at a table together. The vast majority of those travellers will drive — AAA's figure includes everyone travelling at least 50 miles from home, by car, plane, train or bus — but the 17.6 million who will pass through TSA checkpoints, and the 29 million who will fly on US airlines, are the ones whose journeys depend on a system that has failed three times in two weeks. They are the ones who will be watching the departure boards, refreshing their airline apps, and hoping that the special team, the new forecasting tools and the daily meetings are enough.

The advice from the TSA and the airlines is familiar, and it is worth repeating: plan ahead, arrive at airports early, and build extra time into every connection. The TSA is urging passengers to arrive earlier than usual, and airlines are advising travellers to check their flight status before leaving for the airport. It is the kind of advice that sounds obvious until you are the person sprinting through Hartsfield-Jackson because your first flight was delayed by 57 minutes and your connection leaves in 20. The system, for all its technology, still depends on people making it to the gate on time — and this week, more than ever, time is the one thing in short supply.

The drivers: most of the 81.5 million are on the road

It is easy to focus on the airports, because that is where the failures have been most visible, but most of the 81.5 million Americans travelling this week will not set foot in an airport. They will drive. AAA's projection covers everyone travelling at least 50 miles from home, by any means, and the overwhelming majority of those journeys are made by car — families loading suitcases into the boot, driving for hours on motorways that will be among the busiest of the year. For them, the risks are different: traffic, not technology. The roads will be crowded from Tuesday afternoon through Wednesday evening, and again on Sunday, when the return journey begins. The busiest driving days are expected to be Wednesday, as people head to their destinations, and Sunday, as they head home — and the same storms that threaten the flights will make the roads dangerous too, with rain in the Southeast, snow and ice possible in parts of the Midwest and Northeast, and high winds on both coasts.

For drivers, the advice is the same as for flyers, and it is just as important: leave early, check the forecast, and do not assume the journey will take as long as it usually does. A drive that takes four hours on an ordinary weekend can take six or seven on Thanksgiving week, and a storm can turn it into something worse. The difference is that a driver who is delayed can pull over, wait, or take another route. A passenger whose flight is cancelled has no such option. That is the quiet advantage of the road, and it is one reason why, even in a year of record air travel, most Americans still choose to drive home for Thanksgiving.

What to watch this week

The week will be judged by a handful of numbers. The first is the TSA's daily screening count, which will show whether the 17.6 million projection holds, and whether Sunday, November 29, really does bring more than 3 million travellers through the checkpoints in a single day. The second is the flight cancellation and delay rate, which will show whether the system held together or whether the Delta failure was a preview. The third is the weather, which will show whether the East Coast storm and the Pacific storms arrived as forecast, and how much damage they did. And the fourth is the one that matters most to the people travelling: whether they got home, and whether they got back.

There are reasons for cautious optimism. The Delta system was restored within hours. The United outage lasted a little over an hour. The FAA ground stop, for all its severity, was resolved the same day. The system, in other words, has shown that it can recover — and the task force, the forecasting tools and the daily meetings are designed to make that recovery faster. But there are also reasons for worry. Three failures in two weeks, with three different causes, suggest a system under strain, not a system with a single fixable flaw. Record demand, record flights and severe weather are a combination that leaves little margin for error. And the travellers who will bear the cost of any failure are not the airlines or the regulators. They are the families trying to get home for Thanksgiving.

By Monday, November 30, the numbers will be in. The FAA will know how many of its 417,775 flights actually flew. The TSA will know how many of its 17.6 million passengers actually passed through its checkpoints. AAA will know whether its 81.5 million travellers made it to their destinations. And the millions of Americans who travelled this week will know, from their own experience, whether the system held — or whether the Thanksgiving of 2026 will be remembered as the year the planes stopped flying. For now, the airports are full, the planes are full, and the country is on the move. The question is whether the system that carries it can keep up.
