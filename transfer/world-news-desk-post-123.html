Wall Street came back from Thanksgiving hungry. After a holiday break that followed one of the wildest weeks of the year, American stocks surged on Friday, 27 November 2026, as investors rushed back into the beaten-down artificial intelligence shares that had been at the centre of the sell-off. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 275.9 points, or 0.55 percent, at the open to 50,458.86. The S&P 500 rose 79.8 points, or 1.11 percent, at the open to 7,262.61. And the Nasdaq Composite, the index most exposed to the technology trade, jumped 372.7 points, or 1.58 percent, to 23,958.463 at the opening bell.

The gains held through the session. By the close of the morning's trading reported by Reuters, the Dow was up 361.11 points, or 0.72 percent, at 50,544.06. The S&P 500 had gained 51.78 points, or 0.74 percent, to 7,235.66. The Nasdaq Composite was up 204.05 points, or 0.87 percent, at 23,789.86. All but one of the S&P 500's sectors traded higher, with information technology stocks providing the biggest boost. It was, by any measure, a strong day — and it came after a week in which the same market had looked as if it was falling apart.

The rebound had been signalled before the opening bell. Nasdaq 100 futures surged 729 points, or 2.8 percent, to 26,829.3, while S&P 500 futures advanced 144.8 points, or 2 percent, to 7,340.5. Dow Jones Industrial Average futures gained 672 points, or 1.3 percent, to 50,900. The rally gathered momentum during Asian trading, building on a late-session recovery on Wall Street on Wednesday, the last trading day before the Thanksgiving holiday. Asian markets extended the gains: the MSCI Asia Pacific Index advanced 1.8 percent on Friday, with South Korean memory chipmaker SK Hynix among the stocks leading the regional recovery for a second consecutive session.

But Friday's rally, impressive as it was, did not erase the month. Despite the week's turbulence, the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq remained on track for their worst November since 2008 — the year of the global financial crisis. The gains did, however, put both indexes on course for their best weeks since April and August respectively. That is the shape of this market in late 2026: violent swings in both directions, with the same stocks that fall hardest on one day rising fastest on the next, and with the month's losses still dwarfing any single day's gains.

The week that shook the AI trade

To understand Friday, you have to understand the week that came before it. American shares swung sharply all week, and the volatility was concentrated exactly where the money had been made: in artificial intelligence. The trigger was Nvidia, the chipmaker at the heart of the AI boom, and the strangest corporate drama of the year.

Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive on Monday, 23 November, after an 11-day exile, and the market responded with euphoria. The chipmaker's shares surged 31.5 percent on Monday, restoring nearly $1 trillion in market capitalisation in a single session. It was one of the great one-day rallies in stock market history. Then, just as quickly, the mood turned. Nvidia shares fell 5.9 percent on Tuesday and another 3.9 percent on Wednesday. Anthropic, the developer of the Claude chatbot, slipped below its IPO price of $83 a share less than a week after its record-breaking Nasdaq debut. By the time the market closed for Thanksgiving, the AI trade — the trade that had carried the market for two years — looked exhausted.

Friday was the counter-move. Chipmakers that had been hit hard this month recovered some ground. Micron and Marvell each gained more than 2 percent, while the broader chip gauge rose 1.3 percent. AI-linked companies including data-storage firm Sandisk, optical-products maker Lumentum and server maker Dell also advanced. Large technology companies rose as well: Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta Platforms and Oracle gained between 1 percent and 2.7 percent. Nvidia itself, however, fell 1.3 percent on Friday, leaving the stock nearly 40 percent below its October record high — a reminder that the company at the centre of the drama is still the one investors trust least.

The questions hanging over the AI trade have not gone away. Investors are weighing increasingly attractive valuations following the recent correction against persistent concerns over the massive capital expenditure undertaken by AI companies and the potential returns on those investments. The build-out is real — data centres, chips, power, cooling — and so is the spending. What is not yet proven is the return. That is the argument that has divided the market for weeks, and it is the argument that produced both the 31.5 percent Monday and the declines that followed. Friday's rally was a vote for the optimists. It was not a settlement of the argument.

There is also a political shadow over the trade. The S&P 500 and the Nasdaq remained on track for their worst November since 2008 amid concerns about tougher AI regulation following the Democrats' midterm election victory and expectations that monetary policy will remain restrictive. The Democrats won at least 258 seats in the House of Representatives, their largest total since 1992, and also control the Senate. Investors are now pricing in a Washington that will look much harder at the technology giants — their market power, their data practices, their spending — than the one that preceded it. That is a new variable in a market that had, for two years, treated politics as background noise.

Gold jumps 3 percent as the dollar stalls

While equities rallied, gold had its own strong day. Gold prices rose about 3 percent on Friday, recovering from a more than one-year low hit earlier in the week, with the market supported by a pause in the dollar's advance and calmer equity markets. Spot gold jumped 2.8 percent to $3,664.97 per ounce by 1251 GMT, after it touched its lowest level since August 2025 on Wednesday. United States gold futures for December delivery gained 3.6 percent to $3,663.80 per ounce.

The move in gold tells its own story about the week. Gold had been sold hard as the dollar surged and as investors fled to cash during the technology rout. The dollar was still close to its highest level in 22 months after the tech rout drove investors towards safe-haven assets, and the dollar index was set for a flat week. But on Friday the dollar's rally stalled, and gold — the oldest haven of all — reclaimed some of what it had lost. Gold and silver miners rallied as precious-metal prices recovered from recent losses. Newmont gained 3.6 percent, while the Global X Silver Miners ETF advanced 4 percent.

The combination on Friday — stocks up, gold up, the dollar flat — is unusual. Normally, a rising dollar and rising stocks pull in opposite directions for gold. Friday's pattern suggests something simpler: after a week of forced selling, investors were buying back what they had sold, across the board, and the dollar's pause gave them the room to do it. Whether that is the start of a recovery or a pause inside a decline is the question every trader on Wall Street is asking this weekend.

The trade deficit widens — and the Fed looms

Not all of Friday's news was good for the market's mood. The United States goods trade deficit widened sharply in October as an AI infrastructure buildout continued to draw in imports at a rapid clip, potentially putting trade on course to again subtract from economic growth in the fourth quarter. The goods trade shortfall increased 10.7 percent, or $14.4 billion, to $149.5 billion last month, the Commerce Department's Census Bureau said on Friday.

The number is a reminder of how much of the AI boom is imported. The servers, the chips, the components that fill the data centres being built across America are, in large part, made elsewhere — and the bill for them shows up in the trade figures. The deficit is not, in itself, a sign of economic weakness; it is a sign of investment. But it is also a drag on the growth figures, and it is one more reason the fourth quarter's numbers may disappoint after the third quarter's strength.

That third quarter, it is worth remembering, was revised sharply upward. The Commerce Department raised third-quarter 2026 real GDP to an annualised 4.3 percent, up from the 3.4 percent advance estimate — a revision so large that President Donald Trump, 80, posted on Truth Social on 26 November demanding an immediate investigation, labelling the change a massive, purposeful error and claiming the original numbers were fake. The Bureau of Economic Analysis is due to publish its final estimate on 23 December. The dispute over the numbers has become a political story in its own right, but the market's reading is simpler: growth was strong, and the Federal Reserve is watching it.

United States Treasury yields rose on Friday, reflecting the latest shift in investor sentiment on the AI buildout that in recent months has swamped other dynamics in global markets. The 10-year Treasury yield rose 3.8 basis points to 4.88 percent and the 2-year yield, more sensitive to expectations for Federal Reserve policy, rose 2.7 basis points to 4.66 percent. The 10-year yield is essentially flat for the month, reflecting tension between expectations that the Fed will raise rates multiple times in coming months to tamp down inflation and investor fear that the unwind of the artificial intelligence trade could tighten financial conditions enough to hit consumer spending and slow growth.

Traders are pricing in a 99 percent probability of an interest rate hike by the Fed in December. That is an extraordinary number — near certainty — and it frames everything else. The Fed looks poised to raise rates even as equities wobble, and that pressure is already visible in currency markets. The dollar is on track for its longest monthly winning streak since 2024, while the Mexican peso suffered its worst losing streak in five years as carry trades unwound. A December hike, if it comes, will be the test of whether the market's Friday optimism can survive the central bank's determination to keep inflation down.

Winners, losers and the stocks that moved

Beyond the indexes, Friday produced its own crop of individual stories. Vertex Pharmaceuticals slid nearly 10 percent after the United States Food and Drug Administration extended its review of the company's kidney-disease drug by up to three months — a sharp fall for a company whose pipeline depends on regulatory timing. Altria fell 1.8 percent after the United States Marlboro maker said it was considering an all-cash bid for Imperial Brands, a deal that would reshape the tobacco industry on both sides of the Atlantic.

On the winning side, the technology rebound was broad. The chip gauge's 1.3 percent rise was led by Micron and Marvell, each up more than 2 percent. Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta Platforms and Oracle all rose between 1 percent and 2.7 percent. Dell, Sandisk and Lumentum — companies that supply the physical infrastructure of the AI boom — all advanced. And the miners, as noted, had a strong day on the back of gold's recovery.

The breadth of the rally mattered. All but one S&P 500 sector traded higher. That is the difference between a rally and a rotation: on Friday, investors were not moving money from one part of the market to another, they were putting money back into the market as a whole. The question is whether they will keep doing it on Monday, when the market reopens for a full week, and when the Federal Reserve's December decision moves one week closer.

Why November is still the worst since 2008

It is worth pausing on the scale of what has happened this month, because Friday's gains, real as they are, do not change it. The S&P 500 and the Nasdaq were still on course for their worst November since 2008. That is a comparison that should concentrate the mind: November 2008 was the month the global financial system came closest to collapse, when Lehman Brothers had already failed and the world's banks were being kept alive by government guarantees. That this November should be mentioned in the same breath is a measure of how violent the AI correction has been.

The correction has not been a single event but a sequence. First, the valuations: after two years in which AI stocks seemed to rise without limit, investors began to ask whether the spending would ever pay. Then the politics: the Democrats' midterm victory raised the prospect of tougher regulation. Then the rates: the Fed's hawkish turn, with a December hike now priced at 99 percent, removed the cushion of cheap money. Then Nvidia's boardroom drama, which turned the market's bellwether into a source of uncertainty rather than confidence. And then the selling itself, which fed on itself as momentum funds and leveraged positions unwound.

Asian technology stocks have been particularly hard hit by the recent unwinding of the global AI trade, with semiconductor-heavy markets such as South Korea and Taiwan suffering steep losses this month. The MSCI Asia Pacific Index's 1.8 percent rise on Friday was a recovery, but it was a recovery from a deep hole. SK Hynix, the South Korean memory chipmaker, led the regional recovery for a second consecutive session — a sign that the same bargain-hunting instinct that drove Wall Street on Friday is at work in Asia too.

Energy markets, meanwhile, are juggling a supply glut and geopolitical risk. Oil is on track for its first weekly gain this month as attacks on energy infrastructure rattle traders, yet the OPEC cartel faces tough choices as a surplus builds. Brent crude rose to about $97.71 a barrel on Friday, on track for its first weekly gain in four weeks after attacks on tankers in the Strait of Hormuz and Saudi energy sites. Analysts say $100 a barrel is within reach if supplies tighten. Energy prices have risen since the ECB's September meeting, prompting higher inflation projections and record freight costs — one more pressure on a global economy that is already being squeezed from several directions at once.

Black Friday, the consumer and the real economy

Friday was not only a market day. It was Black Friday, the busiest shopping day of the year for physical stores, and the crowds were out before sunrise. Shoppers flocked from Thanksgiving meals to Black Friday deals, braving weather challenges across the United States, in a familiar ritual that has survived years of predictions that online shopping would kill it. Black Friday remains the busiest day for physical stores, despite the rise of e-commerce and early online discounts — and this year the ritual returned even as a Thanksgiving nor'easter knocked out power to nearly a million Americans and flooded coastal towns in New Jersey, where a state of emergency was declared.

The consumer is the other half of the market's story, and the signals are mixed. On the one hand, the economy grew at an annualised 4.3 percent in the third quarter, the fastest pace in three years, and consumer confidence jumped 11.4 points to 103.3 in the latest survey. On the other hand, the Federal Reserve is expected to raise rates in December, which will make borrowing more expensive for households and businesses alike, and the AI trade's unwind has already tightened financial conditions. The worry inside the Fed, and inside the market, is that the unwind could tighten conditions enough to hit consumer spending and slow growth — the very spending that Black Friday is supposed to measure.

That is why the retail numbers from this weekend will be watched as closely as the market's. If shoppers spend freely, it will be evidence that the consumer — the engine of the American economy — is still strong, and that the market's correction is a financial event rather than an economic one. If they do not, it will be evidence that the correction is spreading from the stock market into the real economy. The early signs, from the crowds at Macy's and the queues outside the big-box stores, suggest the appetite is still there. But appetite and affordability are different things, and the December rate decision will test both.

What to watch next

The week ahead will test whether Friday was a turning point or a pause. The Federal Reserve's December meeting is the central event: with a hike priced at 99 percent, the surprise would be if the Fed did not move, but the statement and the press conference will matter more than the decision itself. If the Fed signals more hikes to come, the market's Friday optimism will be tested hard. If it hints that December is the last move for now, the rally could extend.

The economic data will matter too. The final estimate of third-quarter GDP arrives on 23 December, and the dispute over the revision — the President's demand for an investigation, the claim that the original numbers were fake — will keep the figures in the headlines. The trade deficit, which widened to $149.5 billion in October, will be watched for signs that the AI import bill is still growing. And the corporate news will keep coming: the Altria-Imperial Brands talks, the Vertex regulatory delay, and the next round of earnings from the companies that supply the AI build-out.

For now, Wall Street has had its best day in a difficult month, and the traders who bought on Friday will go into the weekend with the market above 50,500 on the Dow, above 7,200 on the S&P 500, and with gold back above $3,660 an ounce. The month is still the worst November since 2008. But the week, for the first time in a while, ended on an up note — and in a market as battered as this one, that counts for something.
