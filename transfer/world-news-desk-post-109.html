TITLE: Steelers Just Released Aaron Rodgers, 42, After His "Insubordination" Ultimatum — Owner: "A Change Had to Be Made" LABELS: Sports SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The Steelers released Aaron Rodgers, 42, after he told coach Mike McCarthy to hand over play-calling or release him. Art Rooney II backed McCarthy. ---BODY---

Aaron Rodgers with the Pittsburgh Steelers in 2025. On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Steelers released the 42-year-old quarterback — hours after he went on television and accused head coach Mike McCarthy of falling asleep in meetings. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC0.

Aaron Rodgers is 42 years old. He is a four-time NFL Most Valuable Player. He has won a Super Bowl. He is one of the greatest quarterbacks ever to play the game. And on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the Pittsburgh Steelers released him — not because his arm had gone, and not because his team had given up on the season, but because, in the words of the team's owner, "insubordination can't be tolerated in any organization". The release came hours after Rodgers went on The Pat McAfee Show and launched an extraordinary public attack on his head coach, Mike McCarthy, accusing the 63-year-old of falling asleep in team meetings, missing walkthroughs, being behind on film study, and even breaking a halftime huddle by shouting "1-2-3 Packers" — the name of his old team — instead of Steelers. It was, by any measure, one of the most dramatic break-ups between a star quarterback and his team in modern NFL history, and it happened in the space of about 48 hours.

The timeline is short, and it is brutal. On Tuesday night, the Steelers announced that Rodgers would be inactive for Friday's Black Friday game against the Denver Broncos, and that 31-year-old Mason Rudolph would start at quarterback in his place. That announcement, on its own, was already major news: the Steelers had benched their starting quarterback, a four-time MVP, with the team sitting at 6-4 and in the middle of a playoff race. But the benching was only the beginning. On Wednesday, Rodgers appeared on The Pat McAfee Show and unloaded on McCarthy in terms that left the Steelers with, in their view, no choice. Later on Wednesday, the team released him. On Thursday, the owner, Art Rooney II, broke his silence — and made it clear that the coach was staying, the quarterback was gone, and the decision was final.

The numbers that define Rodgers' season with Pittsburgh are these: 220 completions on 361 pass attempts, for 2,241 yards, with 16 touchdowns and six interceptions, in 10 games, for a 6-4 team. Those are respectable numbers for a 42-year-old quarterback. They are not the numbers of a player who has lost his ability. They are the numbers of a player whose team was winning more often than it was losing, and whose coach had decided, nevertheless, that a change was needed at the most important position in the sport. The story of the release is not a story about statistics. It is a story about an ultimatum, a television interview, and an owner who decided that no player — not even Aaron Rodgers — is bigger than the organisation.

The ultimatum

The whole affair began, according to the reports that followed, with an ultimatum — and the ultimatum came from Rodgers himself. Gerry Dulac of the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette reported that Rodgers demanded, in a meeting with McCarthy on Tuesday night, that he be allowed to call all the plays on offence, or that he be released. McCarthy told reporters on Wednesday that the quarterback had given him "an ultimatum made clearly outside of his job description", which led to Rodgers being benched for the game against the Broncos. Rodgers himself, in his television appearance, described the conversation in his own words. He said he had spoken with McCarthy on Monday about his concerns, telling the coach: "If you try and bench me or don't change the playcall, then you should just release me because I feel like at this point, I'm doing what's right for the organization."

That is an extraordinary thing for a player to say to his head coach. In the NFL, the head coach decides who plays, and the head coach decides who calls the plays. A quarterback can suggest, can lobby, can argue behind closed doors. A quarterback cannot demand the play-calling, and cannot demand his own release, and expect to keep his job. McCarthy's response, as reported by the New York Post, was to tell Rodgers that he does not get to make demands — and then to hold an impromptu meeting with Rooney and the general manager, Omar Khan, that night. According to sources cited by the Post, it was decided at that meeting that Rodgers would be made inactive for the Broncos game, with the intention of releasing him. The benching announced on Tuesday night was, in other words, already a step towards the exit. The television interview on Wednesday simply made the exit immediate.

The dispute over play-calling was not a new one. Rodgers and McCarthy had a falling-out that, according to the reports, stemmed from the team's offensive play-calling structure. The veteran quarterback believed the play-calling was holding the offence back, and he believed he knew better. Rooney, in his interview with the Post-Gazette, pointed the blame for some of the team's play-calling back at Rodgers himself — specifically in the red zone, the area of the field inside the opponent's 20-yard line, where the Steelers had struggled to turn drives into touchdowns. The owner was making a point that will be familiar to anyone who has watched a team argue about play-calling: the quarterback who wants to call the plays is often the quarterback whose own execution is part of the problem. Whether that is fair to Rodgers or not, it is what the owner of the team said, on the record, the day after the release.

The interview that ended it

If the ultimatum was the cause of the benching, the television interview was the cause of the release. On Wednesday, Rodgers appeared on The Pat McAfee Show — the same show where he has made a habit of speaking his mind, often at length — and accused McCarthy of a series of failings that went far beyond play-calling. He said he believed McCarthy was "unhealthy" and claimed that the head coach had fallen asleep during team meetings. "Mike came in late, sat down and within 10 minutes, began snoring," Rodgers said, according to People magazine's account of the interview. He said McCarthy had missed a walk-through on Friday and a meeting on Saturday morning before the game against Philadelphia, that he was regularly behind on film study, and that he had expressed concern about the coach's health.

The most vivid of Rodgers' accusations concerned the halftime huddle of Sunday's game against the Philadelphia Eagles, which the Steelers lost 19-16 on November 22. Rodgers said McCarthy had broken the huddle by saying "1-2-3 Packers" — the name of the Green Bay Packers, the team that McCarthy used to coach and that Rodgers used to play for — rather than Steelers. For a coach in his first season with a new team, and for a quarterback who had played under that coach at their old team, the slip — if it happened as Rodgers described it — would be an embarrassing moment, a sign that the old habits die hard. For Rodgers to broadcast it on national television, two days before a game, was a different matter entirely. It was a public humiliation of the head coach, delivered by the team's own starting quarterback, on the eve of a nationally televised Black Friday game.

McCarthy had been reunited with Rodgers when the Steelers hired him as head coach in 2026. The two men had won Super Bowl XLV together as members of the Green Bay Packers, back when McCarthy was the Packers' coach and Rodgers was his quarterback. The reunion in Pittsburgh was supposed to be a story about old partners chasing one more championship together. Instead, it ended with the quarterback accusing the coach of sleeping on the job, on television, and being released the same day. The speed of the collapse — from a reunion, to a benching, to a release, in less than a week — is what makes this story so striking. The relationship did not fade. It detonated.

The owner's verdict

On Thursday, Art Rooney II, the Steelers' owner and president, spoke to Gerry Dulac of the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette, and his words left no room for doubt about where the organisation stood. "I just want to say that I have 100% confidence in Mike McCarthy and I expect he'll be our coach this season, next season and beyond," Rooney said. "The big thing, what happened this week, is insubordination can't be tolerated in any organization. When you start getting ultimatums, you can't live with that." It was a statement of support for the coach, a condemnation of the quarterback, and a declaration of the principle that the Steelers would rather lose a four-time MVP than accept a player dictating terms to his coach.

Rooney, who is 74, went further. He said that Rodgers had never expressed any concerns about McCarthy's health to him, and he disputed the quarterback's claims that McCarthy was unfit to coach. "Mike's as healthy as the rest of us," Rooney told Dulac. "Health was not an issue. I'm not sure where that part comes." He criticised Rodgers' decision to air his grievances publicly, and he criticised the timing. "There's maybe a time and place after the season if he wanted to kind of unload," Rooney said. "He talks about wanting to be a good teammate, but you don't do that to your teammates a couple days before the game, that's for sure." The owner was making two arguments at once: that the accusations were untrue, and that even if they had been true, the way Rodgers raised them — on television, two days before a game — was a betrayal of the team.

Rooney also made clear that he agreed with the decision to release Rodgers. "A change had to be made, and I certainly agree with the change and the way it was handled," he said. In a separate statement to ESPN, he said: "I am disappointed in where this went with Aaron Rodgers, but I have 100% confidence in Mike McCarthy. And I have tremendous confidence in Mason Rudolph and the other players in our locker room. Decisions were made on what we believe is in the best interest of the Pittsburgh Steelers. Our main focus right now is preparing for tomorrow's game." The message was consistent across every outlet: the coach stays, the team moves on, and the game on Friday matters more than the drama of the week.

Mason Rudolph and the game on Friday

While the drama around Rodgers dominated the headlines, the Steelers still have a football game to play. On Friday — Black Friday, in a nationally televised slot — the 6-4 Steelers host the Denver Broncos, and Mason Rudolph, 31, will start at quarterback. Rudolph is not a stranger to the Steelers: he has been with the organisation for years, and he has started games for Pittsburgh before. But starting a Black Friday game against Denver, in the middle of a playoff race, in the week that the team's star quarterback was released after a public meltdown, is a different kind of assignment. The eyes of the NFL will be on Rudolph, on McCarthy, and on a locker room that has just watched its most famous player walk out the door.

The Steelers' decision to start Rudolph was announced on Tuesday night, before the release, and it was already a bold call. Benched quarterbacks in the NFL are usually benched because they are playing badly, or because the team has given up on the season. Neither was true of Rodgers: his numbers were respectable, and the Steelers were 6-4. The benching was a statement about authority — about who runs the team — and the release was the same statement, made louder. Starting Rudolph is the next part of the statement: the Steelers believe they can win with their backup, and they believe the team is better off without a quarterback who gives ultimatums to his coach.

Whether they are right will be decided on the field. A win against Denver on Friday would validate everything the Steelers did this week: the benching, the release, the owner's public backing of the coach. A loss would raise the obvious question — whether a team that was 6-4 with a four-time MVP at quarterback has just made itself worse, in the name of a principle, in the middle of a playoff race. The NFL is a results business, and principles are easy to defend after a win and hard to defend after a loss. Friday's game is the first test of the Steelers' decision, and it comes less than 72 hours after the release.

A 42-year-old at the end of the road

Rodgers will turn 43 on December 3, and he has said he plans to retire after the 2026 season. That fact hangs over the whole affair. If this is the end of his career — and a release in late November, at 42, after a public falling-out with his coach, is a hard place to come back from — then the manner of the ending matters. Rodgers' career is one of the great careers in NFL history: four Most Valuable Player awards, a Super Bowl championship, a decade and a half as one of the best quarterbacks in the sport, and a move to Pittsburgh that was supposed to be a final chapter, a last run at a championship with a coach he already knew. Instead, the final chapter, if this is it, is a benching, a television interview, an accusation that his coach falls asleep in meetings, and a release.

It is worth remembering what Rodgers was when he arrived in Pittsburgh. He had led the Steelers to the playoffs the previous year, according to the reports on the release. He had signed a one-year deal to return and reunite with McCarthy, the coach with whom he had won Super Bowl XLV in Green Bay. The reunion was the story of the Steelers' season: the old partners, back together, chasing one more title. The fact that it ended in a public dispute about play-calling and a claim that the coach said "1-2-3 Packers" in a huddle is, in its own way, a fittingly strange end to a strange partnership — two men whose shared history was their greatest asset, and whose shared history, in the end, could not save them from each other.

The question of what happens next for Rodgers is, for now, unanswered. He is a free agent. A team in need of a quarterback could sign him. But a team that signs Aaron Rodgers now is signing a 42-year-old who has just been released for insubordination, who has just accused his head coach of sleeping in meetings on national television, and who has said he plans to retire after this season anyway. The market for that player is, to put it mildly, limited. The reports on Thursday noted that Rodgers' NFL options are dwindling after the ugly Steelers divorce. It is possible that the Steelers game against Philadelphia on November 22 — the 19-16 loss in which McCarthy allegedly broke the huddle with the wrong team's name — was the last game Aaron Rodgers will ever play in the NFL. If so, it is a strange and sad end to a great career: not a farewell tour, not a final playoff run, but a loss, a huddle, and a television interview.

McCarthy, Tomlin, and the Steelers' way

There is a wider story here, about the Steelers and the way they run their football team. Rooney hired McCarthy this off-season to replace Mike Tomlin, who stepped down after 19 seasons as the Steelers' head coach. Nineteen seasons is an extraordinary tenure in the NFL, where coaches are fired after two bad years, and Tomlin's departure marked the end of an era in Pittsburgh. Hiring McCarthy — a Super Bowl-winning coach, a man with a proven record, a man who had already worked with Rodgers — was supposed to be a smooth transition: a new coach, but a familiar one, with a familiar quarterback, chasing the same goal. Instead, the first season of the McCarthy era has produced the most public crisis the Steelers have faced in years.

The Steelers are one of the NFL's most stable franchises. They have had three head coaches since 1969 — Chuck Noll, Bill Cowher, and Mike Tomlin — and the Rooney family has owned the team for generations. Stability is the Steelers' identity, and it is the reason Rooney's statement on Thursday mattered so much. When the owner of the most stable franchise in the NFL says "insubordination can't be tolerated in any organization", he is not just talking about Aaron Rodgers. He is talking about the Steelers' way of doing things: the coach coaches, the players play, and no one — no matter how famous, no matter how decorated — gives the coach an ultimatum. The release of Rodgers was, in that sense, a statement about the franchise as much as about the player. The Steelers would rather be 6-4 with Mason Rudolph than 6-4 with a quarterback who tells the coach what to do.

Whether that is the right call, in football terms, is a question that will be answered on the field over the next six weeks. The Steelers are 6-4, in the middle of a playoff race, with a backup quarterback and a head coach whose authority has just been publicly tested and publicly defended. The locker room has watched its most famous player leave, and it has watched its owner back the coach without reservation. The next six weeks will show whether that backing was enough — whether the team rallies around McCarthy and Rudolph, or whether the loss of a four-time MVP proves too much to overcome. The Black Friday game against Denver is the first answer, and it comes on Friday. The rest of the season is the rest of the answer.

What the fallout means for the NFL

Beyond Pittsburgh, the Rodgers release is a story about power in the modern NFL — about who holds it, and what happens when a star player tries to take it. The NFL has always been a coaches' league, in the sense that the head coach's authority is the foundation of a team's discipline. But the modern NFL is also a players' league, in the sense that star quarterbacks have more power than ever before: they choose their teams, they influence personnel decisions, they have their own media platforms, and they can, as Rodgers did, go on television and say whatever they want about their coach. The tension between those two facts — the coach's authority and the star's power — is the central tension of the modern NFL, and the Steelers' release of Rodgers is the most dramatic example yet of a team choosing the coach over the star.

Other teams will be watching. Every general manager and every owner in the league has a star player who has, at some point, wanted more say in how the team is run. Every head coach has had a player who thought he knew better. The Steelers' answer — release the player, back the coach, and say so publicly — is a clear one, and it will be cited the next time a star player gives his coach an ultimatum. Whether other teams would do the same is a different question. Most teams do not have the stability of the Steelers, or an owner with the authority of Art Rooney II, or a coach with a Super Bowl ring. Most teams, faced with a choice between a four-time MVP and a first-year coach, would find the decision much harder. The Steelers found it easy — or at least, they made it look easy — and that, as much as anything else, is what makes this story remarkable.

For Rodgers, the story is a cautionary one. He has always been a player who speaks his mind, and he has built a public persona around saying what other players will not say. That persona served him well for many years: it made him a star beyond football, a regular on talk shows, a player whose opinions mattered. But the same impulse — to say what he thinks, when he thinks it, on television — is what ended his time in Pittsburgh. The interview on The Pat McAfee Show was not the first time Rodgers had criticised a coach in public, but it was the first time the coach, the owner and the organisation responded by releasing him the same day. The lesson, if there is one, is that there is a line between speaking your mind and insubordination, and that the line is drawn by the people who own the team. Rodgers crossed it on Wednesday. By Thursday, he was gone.
