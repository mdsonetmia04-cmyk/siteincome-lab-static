A 15-year-old boy has just played the most destructive innings in the history of limited-overs cricket, and he has done it by breaking records that had stood for years — not one record, but four, in a single afternoon, in a single innings, against a team of grown men. Vaibhav Sooryavanshi, playing for India A against the United Arab Emirates in the ACC Men's Asia Cup Rising Stars 2026 in Doha on Tuesday, November 24, scored 204 runs from just 53 balls, hitting 28 sixes and seven fours, and in the process he set the records for the fastest century in List A cricket (28 balls), the fastest 150 (40 balls), the fastest double century (52 balls), and the most sixes in a List A innings (28). He reached his double century by hitting the UAE captain, Basil Hameed, for six, and he was dismissed on the very next ball, having scored 204 of the 254 runs that India A made in the match. India A won by seven wickets, chasing down the UAE's total of 249 in just 14.2 overs, with 214 balls to spare, and securing a place in the semi-finals of the tournament. The previous record for the fastest List A double century was 103 balls. Sooryavanshi cut it almost in half.

The numbers, on their own, are hard to believe, and they are worth setting out in full, because they are the story. Sooryavanshi faced 53 balls, and he scored 204 runs from them, at a strike rate of 384.90 — the highest strike rate ever recorded for a List A century, beating the previous best of 338.63, set by South Africa's AB de Villiers against the West Indies in Johannesburg in 2015. He reached his hundred in 28 balls, beating the previous record of 29 balls, set by Australia's Jake Fraser-McGurk for South Australia against Tasmania in the 2023-24 Marsh Cup. He reached 150 in 40 balls, breaking a record he already held. He reached 200 in 52 balls, beating the previous record of 103 balls, set by New Zealand's Chad Bowes for Canterbury against Otago in the Ford Trophy in October 2024. He hit 28 sixes, the most ever hit in a List A innings, and he did it all at the age of 15, becoming the youngest player ever to score a List A double century. He scored 204 of India A's 254 runs, which means that he made more than 80 per cent of his team's total, and that the other ten batsmen, between them, made 50. The match was, in effect, a one-man performance, and the one man was a schoolboy.

The innings began, improbably, with two dot balls. Sooryavanshi watched the first two deliveries of the innings go by without scoring, and then he hit three consecutive sixes, collecting 19 runs from his first six deliveries, and the assault did not stop. He reached his century in 28 balls, and his second hundred — the runs from 100 to 200 — took just 24 balls, which means that he was, if anything, getting faster as the innings went on. He brought up his double century with a six off Hameed, the UAE captain, and he was dismissed on the next ball, caught, having hit 28 sixes and seven fours in 53 balls. The 28 sixes alone account for 168 runs, which means that more than four-fifths of his total came from balls that he hit clean out of the ground. The seven fours account for another 28 runs, which means that 196 of his 204 runs came in boundaries, and that he scored just eight runs in singles, twos and threes. It is, by any measure, the most boundary-dominated innings ever played in professional cricket, and it was played by a 15-year-old, in a tournament for emerging players, in Doha, on a Tuesday afternoon.

The records, one by one

The fastest List A century was the first record to fall, and it was the record that set the tone for the rest of the innings. Jake Fraser-McGurk's 29-ball hundred, scored for South Australia against Tasmania in the Marsh Cup in the 2023-24 season, had stood for nearly three years, and it had been regarded as one of the most unbreakable records in the game, because it required a batsman to score at a rate that seemed, for a long time, to be beyond human reach. Sooryavanshi beat it by a single ball, reaching his hundred in 28 deliveries, and he did it in a match in which his team was chasing 250, which meant that he could not afford to play himself in, or to wait for the bad balls. He had to attack from the start, and he did, and the record fell in the space of a few overs. The third-fastest List A century, for the record, is AB de Villiers' 31-ball hundred against the West Indies in Johannesburg in 2015, which gives a sense of the company that Sooryavanshi has now joined, and of the distance by which he has surpassed it.

The fastest 150 was the second record to fall, and it was a record that Sooryavanshi already held, which meant that he was, in effect, breaking his own record. He reached 150 in 40 balls, and the significance of that number is that it shows the acceleration: he took 28 balls to reach 100, and just 12 more to reach 150, which means that his second fifty took less than half the time of his first. The fastest double century was the third record, and it was the most dramatic, because the previous record — Chad Bowes' 103 balls for Canterbury against Otago in October 2024 — was more than twice as slow. Sooryavanshi reached 200 in 52 balls, which means that he scored his second hundred in 24 balls, and that he cut the previous record almost exactly in half. The list of the fastest double centuries in List A cricket now reads: Sooryavanshi, 52 balls; Bowes, 103; N. Jagadeesan of Tamil Nadu, 114; Travis Head of South Australia, 114; and Harry Brook of England, 114. The gap between first and second is 51 balls, which is, in itself, a record of a kind: no other record in the game has been broken by such a margin, in a single innings, by a single player.

The most sixes in a List A innings was the fourth record, and it was, in some ways, the most remarkable, because it is a record that measures not speed but power. Twenty-eight sixes in a single innings means that Sooryavanshi cleared the boundary 28 times in 53 balls, which means that more than half of the balls he faced went over the rope. The previous record for sixes in a List A innings was lower, and the record for sixes in any professional innings was lower too: Sooryavanshi's 28 sixes are, according to the reports that followed the match, the most hit in a single innings in all of professional cricket. The strike rate of 384.90 is the highest ever recorded for a List A century, and it beats the previous best, AB de Villiers' 338.63, by a margin of more than 46 runs per hundred balls. The numbers are so extreme that they invite disbelief, and they have been checked, and re-checked, and they are correct. The team record is worth noting, too, because it shows that the innings was not only a personal achievement but a match-winning one. India A had to win the match, having lost their previous game to Pakistan A, and the UAE had posted 249 for 8, thanks in large part to a career-best List A century of 130 from 144 balls by the former India domestic batsman Harpreet Singh Bhatia, who now plays for the UAE. The victory secured India A's place in the semi-finals of the tournament, and it did so in a manner that no one who watched it will forget.

Who is Vaibhav Sooryavanshi?

Vaibhav Sooryavanshi — also spelled Suryavanshi — was born on March 27, 2011, which makes him 15 years old, and he is, by any measure, the most precocious cricketer of his generation. He is a left-handed opening batsman from Bihar, and he has been breaking age records since he was 13, when he became the youngest player to be signed in the Indian Premier League, by the Rajasthan Royals. At 15 years and 99 days of age, he became the youngest player ever to represent the Indian senior team, and in November 2025, playing for India A against the UAE in the same tournament, he scored 144 runs from 42 balls — an innings that was, at the time, regarded as extraordinary, and that now looks like a rehearsal for what he did a year later. The 204 in Doha is not, in that sense, a bolt from the blue. It is the latest, and the most extreme, in a series of innings that have, for two years, been redefining what a teenage batsman can do.

The question that everyone asks about a 15-year-old who plays like this is how he does it, and the answer, according to Sooryavanshi himself, is partly about who he talks to. In an interview with ESPNcricinfo after the innings, he said that a conversation with Yuvraj Singh — the former India all-rounder who hit six sixes in an over at the 2007 World Twenty20, and who knows more about hitting a cricket ball than almost anyone alive — had helped him a lot, and that it had helped him to deal with a poor run of scores that had preceded the innings. The detail matters, because it shows that the innings was not only a matter of talent but of temperament: that Sooryavanshi had been struggling, that he had sought advice, and that he had used it. The poor run, and the conversation, and the record that followed, are, together, a story about a young player learning how to be a professional, and about the value of the people around him.

The praise that followed the innings was immediate, and it came from the highest levels of Indian cricket. Rajeev Shukla, the vice-president of the Board of Control for Cricket in India, described Sooryavanshi as a remarkable talent, and said that he had done "something extraordinary" and created a world record, and that he had every confidence in the young batsman's future. The praise is not surprising, and it is not, in itself, news, but it is a measure of how seriously the Indian cricket establishment takes this player, and of how carefully it is managing him.
