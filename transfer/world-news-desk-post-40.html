The Claude maker has now lost nearly $200 billion in value. ---BODY---

Anthropic, the artificial intelligence company behind the Claude chatbot, has fallen below its initial public offering price — just five days after the most hotly anticipated stock market debut in history. Shares in the company, which trade on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT, tumbled below the $83 price at which they were sold to the public in premarket trading on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, and kept falling through the session, trading as low as $75.61 before closing at $81.91, down 6.5 per cent on the day. At the low point of the day, the company's market value briefly slipped below $1 trillion, to $999.5 billion, according to Dow Jones Market Data — a striking reversal for a company whose fully diluted valuation had briefly topped $2 trillion on its first day of trading. The fall means Anthropic has now surrendered all of its debut gains, and then some, and it has put the company on course to lose nearly $200 billion in market value in less than a week. For the investors who queued to buy into the biggest IPO in history, and for the Wall Street banks that priced it, the message is blunt: the market's appetite for artificial intelligence, which looked bottomless six days ago, has limits after all.

The speed of the reversal is what has stunned the market. Anthropic went public on Thursday, November 19, in a listing that broke every record in the book. The company raised $81.3 billion in its initial offering, surpassing the record set earlier in the year by Elon Musk's SpaceX for the largest IPO in history, and the offering later grew to $93.5 billion after the underwriters exercised their option to buy an additional 147 million shares. The shares opened at $111.50, 34 per cent above the $83 offer price, and the company's fully diluted valuation briefly topped $2 trillion — a figure that put a five-year-old AI company, founded by a group of researchers who had left OpenAI, in the same league as the largest companies on earth. The debut was supposed to be the coronation of the AI boom, the moment the industry's most admired research lab became a public company and the market put a price on the future of artificial intelligence. Five days later, that price has fallen below the level at which the shares were first sold, and the coronation has turned into a cautionary tale.

The fall: $111.50 to $75.61 in four trading days

The numbers tell the story with a clarity that needs little embellishment. Anthropic's shares opened at $111.50 on November 19, closed their first day at $97.17, and then fell in each of the sessions that followed. By Monday, November 23, the shares were already under pressure, closing at $87.64 after missing out on a broad rally led by AI-linked stocks on Wall Street — a rally driven by Nvidia, whose shares surged after the chipmaker reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive. On Tuesday, the selling accelerated. The shares dropped below the $83 IPO price in premarket trading, fell as much as 12.6 per cent during the session to $76.60, according to one report, and traded as low as $75.61 before recovering slightly to close at $81.91, down 6.5 per cent on the day. The decline left the shares roughly 22 per cent below their first-day closing price of $97.17, and nearly 30 per cent below the $111.50 opening price at which trading had begun five days earlier. For a company that had been valued at more than $2 trillion on its debut, the fall to a market value below $1 trillion — even briefly — is a loss of more than half its peak worth in less than a week.

The fall was not, it should be said, an Anthropic-specific event. The shares were swept up in a broader technology selloff, and the wider market was falling too: the Nasdaq dropped 1.48 per cent on Tuesday, and the S&P 500 fell 0.50 per cent. But Anthropic fell further and faster than the market, and the reasons for that are specific to the company and to the structure of its listing. The first is supply. Anthropic's final prospectus disclosed that up to 315 million shares would be immediately eligible for sale after the listing, a flood of stock that put downward pressure on the price from the first day of trading. The second is the sheer scale of the company's spending commitments: the prospectus also disclosed $900.9 billion in compute obligations — the cost of the computing power Anthropic has committed to buying in order to train and run its AI models — a figure that has made investors question whether the company's growth can ever generate enough cash to cover its bills. And the third is the signal sent by the company's own leadership, who sold shares in the days after the debut — a move that the market read, fairly or not, as a lack of confidence in the price.

The $280 million sales: Amodei and his sister cashed out

The detail that has drawn the most attention is the selling by Anthropic's own founders. Regulatory filings released late on Monday, November 23, showed that chief executive Dario Amodei sold 3,378,378 shares at the IPO price of $83 on November 20, the day after the debut, in a transaction worth more than $280 million. A separate filing showed that Anthropic's president, Daniela Amodei — Dario's sister and co-founder — sold the same number of shares, 3,378,378, at the same price, also worth roughly $280 million. Together, the two founders sold more than $560 million worth of stock in the first days after the listing, at the $83 offer price, even as the shares were trading in the market at well above that level. The sales were made to the IPO underwriters as part of the listing process, and they are a normal feature of a large IPO — founders and early investors routinely sell a portion of their holdings when a company goes public. But the timing, and the size, have been difficult for the market to ignore, and they have become a central part of the story of the share price's fall.

The filings also revealed something else about the listing: that more than half of the institutions that bid for Anthropic stock received no shares at all, according to Bloomberg, because demand for the offering so far outstripped the supply of shares on offer. That detail cuts both ways. On the one hand, it is a measure of how extraordinary the demand was — the largest IPO in history, oversubscribed many times over, with the world's biggest investors turned away. On the other hand, it is a reminder of how the IPO was structured: the company sold a relatively small number of shares to the public, at a price that the market immediately judged to be too low, and the shares that were sold went to a narrow group of investors. The result was a debut that looked spectacular — a 34 per cent first-day pop, a $2 trillion valuation — but that was built on a thin foundation, and that foundation gave way as soon as the wider market turned. The institutions that were shut out of the IPO, as one market commentary put it, may turn out to be the lucky ones.

The record IPO: $93.5 billion, and a warning about humanity

To understand the scale of what has happened, it is worth remembering what the IPO was. Anthropic was founded in 2021 by Dario Amodei and a group of researchers who had left OpenAI, and it built its reputation on Claude, a chatbot that became one of the most widely used AI systems in the world and one of the most admired by researchers for its safety work. The company's listing was the most anticipated IPO of the year, and the numbers it produced were unlike anything the market had seen. The initial raise of $81.3 billion surpassed the record set by SpaceX earlier in 2026, and the final figure of $93.5 billion, after the underwriters' option, made it the largest stock market listing in history by a wide margin. The $83 offer price valued the company at a level that already assumed years of extraordinary growth, and the $111.50 opening price — and the $2 trillion fully diluted valuation that came with it — assumed more growth still. The market, in other words, was not pricing Anthropic on what it is today. It was pricing it on what it might become, in a future in which AI transforms the economy and the companies that build it capture a share of that transformation that is almost unimaginable.

The IPO also carried a feature that no other listing in history has had: a formal warning, in the prospectus, about the risks that artificial intelligence poses to humanity. Anthropic's filing included an unusual disclosure about the dangers of advanced AI — the possibility that the technology could be misused, that it could cause harm at scale, and that the company's own work could contribute to risks it could not fully foresee or control. The warning was, in part, a reflection of the company's founding philosophy, which has always emphasised safety, and of the Amodei siblings' longstanding concern about the trajectory of the technology they are building. But it was also, in the eyes of some investors, a reminder of the paradox at the heart of the company: a business whose product is also, by its own admission, a source of risk to the species, and whose value depends on a future that its own founders say may be dangerous. That paradox has not gone away. If anything, the share price's fall has made it sharper, because it has forced the market to ask whether the price it paid for Anthropic was a price for a business, or a price for a bet on a future that no one can predict.

The AI valuation question: is the boom a bubble?

Anthropic's fall has added to a question that has been hanging over the market for months: whether the valuations being placed on artificial intelligence companies have run ahead of the revenues those companies can generate. The concern is not new, but Anthropic's debut and its rapid reversal have made it concrete in a way that abstract warnings never could. The company raised $93.5 billion, was valued at more than $2 trillion, and has committed to spending $900.9 billion on computing power — and yet its shares have fallen below their IPO price within five days, because the market has begun to doubt whether the arithmetic works. The doubt is not about whether AI is important. No one seriously disputes that it is. It is about whether the companies building it can earn enough, quickly enough, to justify the sums being invested in them, and whether the infrastructure being built to train them — the data centres, the chips, the power plants — will generate returns that cover their cost.

The context for that doubt is a market that has been, by any measure, extraordinary. The AI boom has driven the largest companies in the world to valuations that would have been unimaginable a decade ago, and it has drawn trillions of dollars into computing infrastructure, much of it financed by debt. Rising interest rates threaten to push those costs even higher, and the vast sums being poured into data centres and chips have made investors nervous about whether the spending will ever pay off. Anthropic's fall is, in that sense, not an isolated event but a symptom: a company that raised more money than any other in history, at a valuation that assumed the AI boom would continue indefinitely, has discovered that the market's patience is not infinite, and that the gap between promise and profit is where share prices go to die. The fall does not prove that the AI boom is a bubble. But it is the clearest evidence yet that the market is no longer willing to take the boom on trust, and that the companies at its centre will have to prove, with revenues and profits, that they are worth what they have been valued at.

That proof will take time, and it is time that Anthropic may not have in the form the market wants. The company's compute obligations — $900.9 billion, as disclosed in its prospectus — are a commitment to spend, over the coming years, on the computing power it needs to train its models and serve its customers. Those obligations are not optional. They are contracts, and they must be paid whether the revenue arrives or not. The question the market is asking is whether the revenue will arrive, and in what form, and the answer is not yet clear. Claude is a widely used product, and Anthropic's business has grown rapidly, but the company's costs have grown faster, and the gap between the two is the gap that the share price has fallen into. The fall below the IPO price is, in that sense, a verdict on the gap: the market looked at the $83 price, looked at the $900.9 billion in obligations, looked at the revenue, and decided that the price was too high.

History: Facebook fell on day two, SpaceX took a month

Anthropic is not the first blockbuster IPO to fall below its offer price, and it will not be the last — but the speed of its fall puts it in rare company. Among the biggest companies to go public, those with a market value of $75 billion or more on their first trading day, Anthropic is the second-fastest to fall back to its IPO price. The only company to do it faster was Facebook, now Meta, which fell below its offer price on only its second day of trading in 2012, in a debut that was, at the time, the most anticipated IPO in history and that became, for years afterwards, the cautionary tale of the IPO market. SpaceX, whose listing earlier in 2026 was the largest in history before Anthropic surpassed it, took about a month to dip below its IPO price. Anthropic did it in four trading days. The comparison is not flattering, and it is not one the company will want to be remembered by — but it is also, in a sense, misleading, because Facebook went on to become one of the most valuable companies in the world, and its early stumble is now a footnote in a story of extraordinary success. The question for Anthropic is whether its fall is the beginning of a similar story, or the beginning of a different one.

The history of hot IPOs suggests that the answer is not predetermined. Shares in newly listed companies often rise sharply on their first day and then give back some or all of those gains in the weeks that follow, as the excitement of the debut fades and the market begins to value the company on its fundamentals rather than on its narrative. That pattern is well known, and it is one reason experienced investors treat first-day pops with scepticism: the price on the first day reflects the scarcity of the shares on offer and the enthusiasm of the buyers, not the long-term value of the business. Anthropic's debut was the most extreme example of that pattern in history — the largest IPO ever, the highest valuation ever, the most oversubscribed ever — and its fall is, in that sense, the most extreme example of the correction that usually follows. What happens next will depend on the company, not on the pattern: on whether it can grow its revenue, control its costs, and convince the market that the $900.9 billion it has committed to spending will generate returns. Facebook did that, over years, and its share price recovered. Whether Anthropic can do the same is the question the market will be asking for a long time.

What it means for AI startups — and for the IPO market

Anthropic's fall matters beyond the company itself, because it matters for every other AI company that is thinking about going public, and for every investor who is thinking about buying into them. The IPO was supposed to open the floodgates — to show that the public markets were ready to pay extraordinary prices for AI companies, and to encourage a wave of listings by the startups that have been built on the back of the boom. Instead, it has shown that the public markets will pay extraordinary prices for about four days, and then change their minds. That is a message that will be heard in every boardroom in Silicon Valley, and it is a message that will make the next AI IPO — whenever it comes — a much harder sell. The companies that were waiting for Anthropic to prove the market's appetite will now have to prove it themselves, and the investors who were waiting to buy into the next big AI listing will now ask harder questions about price, about revenue, and about the gap between the two.

The fall also matters for the people who build AI, and for the researchers who have made the field what it is. Anthropic was founded by researchers, and its reputation was built on research — on a commitment to safety, to careful science, and to a vision of AI that was about more than profit. The company's listing was, for many of those researchers, a moment of validation: proof that the work they had done mattered, and that the market recognised it. The share price's fall does not change the quality of that work, but it changes the context in which it is done, and it raises a question that the field has been avoiding: whether the commercial pressures of being a public company — the quarterly earnings, the share price, the demand for growth — are compatible with the careful, safety-first approach that Anthropic has always claimed to embody. Dario Amodei has said, repeatedly, that the company's mission is to build safe AI, and that its commercial success is a means to that end. The market has now given its verdict on the commercial part. The safety part is still to be judged, and it will be judged over a much longer timescale than four trading days.

What's next: the lock-up, the earnings, and the long road back

The immediate future for Anthropic's shares will be shaped by the mechanics of the listing as much as by the company's performance. The 315 million shares that became eligible for sale immediately after the IPO will continue to weigh on the price, and the market will be watching for further sales by insiders — by early investors, by employees, and by the founders themselves — that could add to the supply. The company's first earnings report as a public company will be scrutinised for evidence that the revenue is growing fast enough to justify the valuation, and for any sign that the compute obligations are being managed rather than simply accumulated. And the wider market will matter too: if the technology selloff continues, Anthropic's shares will fall with it, whatever the company does; if the market stabilises, the shares may find a floor, and the long process of rebuilding confidence can begin.

There is also a longer question, and it is the one that will define the company's future: whether Anthropic can become a business that earns its valuation, rather than a story that justifies it. The company has the product, the talent, and the reputation. What it needs now is the proof — proof that Claude can generate the revenue to cover the $900.9 billion in compute obligations, proof that the AI boom is a durable transformation rather than a speculative mania, and proof that a company founded by researchers can thrive as a public company without losing the qualities that made it worth listing in the first place. That proof will take years, not days, and it will be measured in earnings reports, in customer growth, and in the slow accumulation of evidence that the bet the market made on November 19 was, in the end, the right one. The shares closed on Tuesday at $81.91, below the $83 at which they were sold to the public. The question is whether they will ever see $111.50 again.
