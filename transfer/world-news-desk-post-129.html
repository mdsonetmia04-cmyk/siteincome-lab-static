TITLE: Google's AI Just Solved 6 Math Proofs in a Single Day — Including a 34-Year-Old Problem That Guards Your Passwords LABELS: Science SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Google's Cogentic AI posted six math proofs on 25 November, closing a 34-year factoring gap — but experts say RSA encryption is safe for now. ---BODY---

Photo: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

On Tuesday, 25 November 2026, Google Research posted six mathematics papers to a public GitHub repository. Nothing about that sentence sounds dramatic — until you read what the papers claim. According to Google, every one of them was produced by an artificial intelligence system called Cogentic, built on Gemini 4, from a single prompt and with, in Google's own words, "no human interaction other than the initial prompt." The lead paper, 54 pages long, claims to close a 34-year-old theoretical gap in integer factorisation — the mathematical problem that underpins RSA encryption, the system that guards almost everything you do online, from bank logins to private messages.

The papers have not been peer reviewed. No outside mathematician has verified them yet. And the most respected computer scientist to have examined them says they pose no practical threat to your passwords. But the same week, that same scientist reported hearing from multiple reliable sources that a leading AI laboratory may be sitting on something far more dangerous: a method for factoring large integers efficiently — the breakthrough cryptographers have feared for decades. The two stories arrived on the same day, from the same corner of the internet, and the world spent the week trying to untangle which one matters.

This is the story of what Google's AI actually did, what it did not do, and why the rumour mill around encryption went into overdrive on 25 November — and why, for now at least, your bank account is safe.

What Google's AI system actually did

Cogentic is Google Research's multi-agent system for mathematical discovery. It was built by a team of seven researchers split across Google Research and Google DeepMind — Yang Cai, Vineet Gupta, Yanchen Jiang, Christopher Liaw, Aranyak Mehta, Grigoris Velegkas and Di Wang — and introduced in a preprint posted to arXiv on 30 September 2026, paper number 2609.40324, as a "prove-verify" loop: one part of the system proposes mathematical proofs, another part checks them. In its early runs, Cogentic used Gemini 3.1 Pro and an early version of Gemini 4, known internally as Argon, and its results were independently verified by domain experts before publication.

Between 30 October and 23 November, four Cogentic papers in game theory and learning theory followed. Then, on 25 November, came the big batch: six new papers landed in the repository's cogentic_research_papers directory in a single commit, numbered 4a7bc8ab0bc7, bringing the folder's total to ten Cogentic-authored papers. The commit message said something remarkable: each paper had been produced by the AI system alone, from one initial prompt, with no human being touching the mathematics afterwards.

The six papers cover discrete logarithms, approximate Nash equilibria in two-player games, communication complexity and graph reconstruction — and, most strikingly, two of them attack integer factorisation itself. That is the problem of breaking a large number into its prime factors: given a number like 15, finding that it equals 3 times 5. For small numbers it is trivial. For the 600-digit numbers used in RSA encryption, it is so hard that the entire security of the internet rests on the assumption that no one can do it in any reasonable amount of time.

The 34-year-old gap — and what closing it means

The headline paper is titled "Unconditional Integer Factorization in Time L_N[1/3, O(1)]." Its claim is precise and, if it holds up, genuinely historic. It proves that a randomised algorithm can completely factor any integer N in expected subexponential time, with an explicit constant of 105 in the exponent's leading term — and it does so without relying on any unproven mathematical assumptions.

To understand why that matters, you need a little history. The fastest factoring method actually used against real RSA keys today is the general number field sieve, usually shortened to GNFS. It works well in practice, but its speed has only ever been established heuristically — mathematicians believe it is fast, but they cannot prove it. The last fully proven bound on factoring, one that comes with a mathematical guarantee rather than a hope, dates back to 1992, when Hendrik Lenstra and Carl Pomerance proved an algorithm running in time with an exponent of 1/2. For 34 years, nobody could prove anything better. Google's paper claims to have matched the number field sieve's growth rate — an exponent of 1/3 — with a proof, not a heuristic. In the words of one report on the release, it closes a 34-year-old theoretical gap.

There is, however, a very large catch, and it sits inside the notation. The new algorithm's constant is 105. The number field sieve's comparable constant, under its heuristic analysis, is about 1.923. Both numbers sit inside an exponential, which means the difference between them is not a rounding error — it is astronomical. In practice, Cogentic's algorithm would be dramatically slower than the sieve at factoring any real RSA key. The paper also hands every number up to 210,000 to plain trial division — checking divisors one by one — a cutoff far above the 2,048- to 4,096-bit keys that RSA commonly uses. In other words, this is a theoretical milestone, not a practical weapon. As Charles Guillemet, the chief technology officer of Ledger, the crypto transaction infrastructure firm, put it: it is a theoretical milestone, not the arrival of a faster algorithm, and there is no need to revise RSA key lengths.

The second factoring paper in the batch drops randomness altogether. It targets deterministic factoring — algorithms that never use random coin flips and never return a wrong answer. Every deterministic factoring algorithm proved before it, down to a 2021 bound from University of New South Wales mathematician David Harvey at roughly the fifth root of N, scales exponentially with the number of digits. Cogentic's second paper claims a subexponential worst-case bound of L_N[3/4], growing slower than any fixed root of N, without random coin flips or unproven assumptions. Harvey himself, who flagged the earlier work in an email, said Google's new results "appear to be a better complexity bound."

The other four papers in the batch

The two factoring papers were not alone. The same 25 November commit carried four more, and together they show the breadth of what Cogentic was asked to do. One paper tackles discrete logarithms in finite fields of small characteristic — the problem that underpins Diffie-Hellman key exchange, another pillar of internet encryption, though the result is limited to a special domain and does not threaten the versions used in practice. Another addresses approximate Nash equilibria in two-player games, a central problem in game theory with applications from economics to artificial intelligence itself. The remaining two cover communication complexity — how much information two parties must exchange to solve a problem together — and graph reconstruction, the question of whether a network can be rebuilt from its pieces.

None of the six has been peer reviewed. Each paper's README says Cogentic completed a proof in Lean 4, the software that checks mathematical steps line by line, using only Mathlib, the community-built Lean mathematics library. But by midday on 25 November, as the release passed 300 points on Hacker News, the repository held only PDFs and READMEs. The commit says Google will release all six Lean formalisations in a companion submission, allowing outside researchers to run the proof checker and compare the formal statements with the papers' theorems. Until those formalisations appear, the proofs exist as claims in PDF form, not as machine-checked facts. Later on 25 November, Google revised the batch's Nash equilibrium paper to cite earlier work, writing that no algorithmic or proof content had changed. That afternoon, the repository still held only PDFs and READMEs.

The rumour that set the internet alight

If Google's papers were the only story, this would be a quiet triumph for theoretical computer science. But on the same day, 25 November, Scott Aaronson — professor of computer science at the University of Texas at Austin, a quantum computing theorist who worked at OpenAI from 2022 to 2024 — wrote on his blog that he had heard from multiple reliable sources that OpenAI or Anthropic, or both, had discovered a way to efficiently factor large integers. In the jargon of the field, the rumour is "Factoring in P": a polynomial-time algorithm for the problem RSA depends on being hard.

Aaronson was careful, and his caution matters as much as the rumour itself. He said he had heard the claim from multiple reliable sources, but he also said clearly that he could not be confident the information was substantiated, and he took a cautious stance toward spreading unverified rumours. He distinguished Google's published research from the rumour entirely, saying the new factoring results offered nothing of immediate practical relevance to cryptographic security, and that Google's papers were not the source of the story he had heard. The two threads, as one report put it, are related only by timing. Google's papers are real and checkable; the bigger rumour is not, and the researcher who reviewed Google's work says it is not the source of it.

Even so, the combination was electric. Integer factorisation is the mathematical operation of breaking an integer into a product of primes, and RSA, the leading form of public-key encryption, bases its security on the difficulty of factoring huge integers in realistic time. A genuine polynomial-time factoring algorithm would not merely be a mathematical curiosity. It would undermine the encryption protecting bank transactions, government communications, medical records and the digital signatures that secure cryptocurrencies. Aaronson himself said it could not yet be judged whether such a method, if it existed, could break Bitcoin signatures. The crypto industry and information security professionals spent the week debating a claim that nobody has seen, about a result nobody has verified, from sources nobody has named.

Why your passwords are safe — for now

Here is the part that got lost in the excitement: nothing published on 25 November can break your encryption. Google's factoring algorithm, with its constant of 105, is vastly less efficient in practice than the number field sieve that attackers already have. The recommended lengths for RSA keys are calculated on the premise of the number field sieve's computational complexity, and the new proof does not overturn that premise. The discrete logarithm paper is limited to finite fields of small characteristic, a special case that does not touch the versions of the problem used in real systems. And the rumoured breakthrough at another lab remains exactly that — a rumour, unconfirmed, unverified, and explicitly flagged as uncertain by the person who reported it.

Aaronson's own verdict on Google's work was blunt: the papers carry "nothing of practical relevance for cryptography." FinanceFeeds, reviewing the papers, reached the same conclusion: Google's published algorithms remain computationally impractical for attacking modern RSA keys, and no publicly verified breakthrough has demonstrated that existing encryption can be defeated using these techniques. The papers are preprints, not peer-reviewed publications, and expert verification is only now getting under way.

That does not mean the week was a false alarm. It means the alarm, if there is one, is about the future, not the present. Aaronson said he was confident that AI laboratories were approaching important cryptographic discoveries. The question is not whether AI will eventually transform mathematics — it already has — but whether the next breakthrough arrives in a peer-reviewed paper, a GitHub repository, or a rumour on a blog, and whether the world will have time to react when it does.

The verification problem: when AI writes the proofs

There is a second story here, quieter but arguably more important than the factoring result itself. Cogentic's papers were written by an AI system, from a single prompt, with no human interaction afterwards. That is a new way of doing mathematics, and it comes with a new failure mode. Releasing batches of AI-generated proofs ahead of outside review has already led to quick retractions. After OpenAI released 722 AI-generated mathematics manuscripts on 6 October 2026, it withdrew three the next day, when a sign error broke one proof and two papers built on it. The error was small. The cascade was not.

Google's batch is not the first attempt at AI-generated factoring proofs, either. In late October, Isaac Rudich and Louis-Martin Rousseau of Polytechnique Montréal posted a note and Lean code claiming that a deterministic machine finds the smallest prime factor of N in subexponential time. The proof came from Anthropic's Claude, and the two wrote that they audited the formal statement and left the proof to Lean's checker. Harvey, who flagged the earlier work, said Google's new results "appear to be a better complexity bound" — but the comparison itself shows how fast this field is moving, and how much of it now happens in public repositories rather than journals.

The safeguard Google offers is Lean 4: formal verification, where a computer checks every step of a proof against the rules of logic. It is a powerful tool, and it is the reason Cogentic is built as a "prove-verify" loop in the first place. But formal verification only works once the formalisation exists and outsiders can run it. Until Google releases the Lean code it has promised, the six papers rest on the reputation of the system that wrote them — and on the willingness of mathematicians to read 54 pages of AI-generated argument and check it by hand.

That reading is under way now. The factoring paper's abstract, posted to GitHub under a Creative Commons licence, is already circulating. Mathematicians on Hacker News, on social media and in university departments are picking through the argument, looking for the sign error, the hidden assumption, the step that does not follow. If the proof holds, it will be remembered as the day AI closed a 34-year gap in number theory. If it falls, it will be remembered as another cautionary tale about publishing before verifying. Either way, the verdict will come from humans, not from the machine that wrote the proof.

What this means for mathematics — and for everyone else

Step back from the cryptography, and the week looks different. An AI system produced six research-level mathematics papers in a single day, on subjects ranging from number theory to game theory, each claiming a result that human mathematicians had not achieved. Even if one or two of them turn out to be wrong, the scale of the attempt is new. Mathematics has always been a human activity, slow, solitary and verified by argument between people. Cogentic represents a different model: a machine that proposes, a machine that checks, and humans who arrive afterwards to audit the result.

For the rest of us, the practical lesson is narrower but worth stating plainly. RSA encryption, the system that protects your online banking, your email and your messages, rests on a mathematical assumption: that factoring very large numbers is extremely hard. That assumption survived 25 November. The proven bound got better, the practical attack did not, and the rumoured breakthrough remains a rumour. But the week also showed how quickly the ground can shift. A single blog post from a respected theorist, on the same day as a real AI achievement, was enough to send the crypto world into a spin. The next time the rumour arrives, it may come with a paper attached — and the world will have to decide, in hours rather than years, whether its encryption still holds.

Google's six papers are public now, on GitHub, for anyone to read. The Lean formalisations are promised. The peer review has not happened. And somewhere, according to a rumour that its own author would not vouch for, another lab may already hold the result that would change everything. For now, the only honest summary is the one Aaronson gave: Google's papers are a genuine theoretical advance with no practical bearing on cryptography — and the bigger question is still open.

A short history of trying to break RSA

RSA was invented in 1977 by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman at MIT, and it has been the backbone of internet security ever since. Its security rests on a single asymmetry: multiplying two large prime numbers together is easy, but working backwards — taking the product and finding the two primes — is extraordinarily hard. Every time you visit a secure website, your browser and the server use that asymmetry to agree on a secret key without ever sending it across the internet in the open.

Mathematicians and codebreakers have been chipping away at the problem for nearly half a century. In 1994, a team led by Arjen Lenstra factored RSA-129, a 129-digit number that the inventors of RSA had challenged the world to crack, using hundreds of computers working together over eight months. In 2009, an international team factored RSA-768, a 232-digit number, after more than two years of computation on a cluster of machines. Each success pushed the recommended key size upwards — from 512 bits, to 1,024, to today's standard of 2,048 bits and beyond — but the fundamental wall never moved. The number field sieve, the best practical method, remained a heuristic: fast in practice, unproven in theory.

That is why the Lenstra–Pomerance result of 1992 mattered so much, and why its 34-year reign matters now. It was the last time anyone proved, with full mathematical rigour, that factoring could be done within a particular time bound. Everything since has been faster in practice but weaker in proof. Google's paper, if verified, finally gives the proven bound the same growth rate as the practical method — a result number theorists have wanted since the early 1990s. It does not make factoring faster. It makes the theory honest.

What to watch next

Three things will decide how this story ends. First, the Lean 4 formalisations: when Google releases them, independent researchers will be able to run the proof checker themselves and confirm, line by line, whether the argument holds. That is the moment the papers stop being claims and start being results. Second, the peer review: mathematicians will read the 54-page factoring paper the old-fashioned way, looking for the gap that a machine might have missed. And third, the rumour: whether OpenAI or Anthropic, or any other lab, has anything to say about the reports Aaronson relayed — and whether the method he heard about exists at all.

Until then, the sensible advice is the boring kind. Your passwords are safe. Your bank's encryption is safe. The mathematics that protects the internet survived 25 November intact. But the week also proved something else: artificial intelligence is now doing mathematics at a level, and at a speed, that no human team can match — six papers in a day, on six different subjects, from a single prompt. The next breakthrough may not take 34 years. It may take a weekend. And when it comes, the world will find out about it the same way it found out about this one: on GitHub, on a blog, and all at once.
