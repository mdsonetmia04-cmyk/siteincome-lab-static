The two most powerful companies in the artificial intelligence race are now suing each other. OpenAI filed counterclaims against Apple on Friday, 27 November 2026, accusing the iPhone maker of stealing its trade secrets, threatening the hardware suppliers working on its first consumer device, and using its size and wealth to crush a rival before it can even ship a product. The filing, made in federal court in San Jose, California, turns a one-sided lawsuit into a full-blown legal war — and it puts the future of the most anticipated gadget since the iPhone in front of a judge.

The Financial Times first reported the filing on Friday, and Reuters, Al Jazeera, Engadget, Gizmodo and others quickly confirmed the details. OpenAI's counterclaims accuse Apple of federal trade-secret theft, interference with its contracts and unfair competition under California law. The company says Apple is engaged in what the filing calls a "wide variety of unlawful, unfair, and tortious conduct" — and that the iPhone maker is trying to "compete unfairly by any means available" because it is losing the AI race.

Apple, for its part, is not backing down an inch. In a statement issued after the filing, the company said the case "is about OpenAI employees deliberately stealing, accessing, and using Apple's secret and confidential information regarding our unreleased technologies, processes, and products — not about inadvertent retention of Apple information." Apple called OpenAI's counterclaims "a transparent attempt to distract from the substance of Apple's lawsuit, in which OpenAI has already been ordered to stop using Apple's confidential information."

Both sides, in other words, are accusing the other of exactly the same crime. Both say the other stole their secrets. Both say the other is the aggressor. And both are now asking the same court to decide who is telling the truth. The trial is scheduled for 19 July 2027 — and the stakes could hardly be higher, because the fight is not really about documents. It is about who gets to build the device that replaces the smartphone.

What OpenAI filed — and what it accuses Apple of doing

OpenAI's counterclaims were filed on Friday, 27 November 2026, in the United States District Court for the Northern District of California, in San Jose. The case is Apple Inc. v. Liu, No. 5:26-cv-07078 — the same case Apple started in July, now with OpenAI firing back from the defendant's chair. According to the Financial Times, the filing makes three central accusations, and each one is more serious than the last.

First, trade-secret theft. OpenAI alleges that Apple itself stole confidential information from OpenAI — the mirror image of the accusation Apple levelled at OpenAI in July. The centrepiece of this claim is a former OpenAI employee who returned to Apple after working at OpenAI for six months. In that half-year, OpenAI says, the employee gained access to specific information about a new OpenAI device and the company's roadmap for future hardware products. Then he went back to Apple — and, OpenAI claims, took the secrets with him. "Given the timing, it now appears that stealing those documents may have been his reason for joining OpenAI in the first place," OpenAI said in its filing, according to Al Jazeera's account of the document.

OpenAI had already flagged this in October, when it told the court in an earlier filing that an employee who returned to Apple had sent more than 100 confidential files about an unreleased OpenAI device to his personal accounts. That October filing was the first sign that the case was about to flip — that OpenAI believed it had found evidence of theft running in the opposite direction. Friday's counterclaims make that accusation formal, turning an allegation whispered in a procedural filing into a full federal claim.

Second, supplier coercion. OpenAI accuses Apple of "coercing and threatening" the hardware suppliers that work with both companies — the manufacturers and component makers whose factories and parts both firms need. According to the Financial Times report, OpenAI claims Apple asked shared manufacturers to identify which personnel were assigned to OpenAI projects, and pressured at least one vendor to stop working with OpenAI altogether. The Decrypt account of the filing adds that Apple allegedly asked suppliers to name the staff working on OpenAI's devices, a demand that would let Apple map out exactly who was building its rival's product — and lean on them.

Third, a broader charge of unfair competition. OpenAI's filing argues that Apple is using its "size and wealth to forestall competition" — that a company worth trillions is throwing its weight around to strangle a rival's hardware business before it starts. The filing says Apple sued because it is losing the AI race, asserting that the company "has struggled to incorporate innovative AI capabilities into its previously popular products" and is now trying to hold back a competitor that threatens to "outcompete Apple on what Apple views as its own turf."

That last phrase matters, because it reveals what OpenAI thinks this fight is really about. It is not about a handful of stolen files. It is about turf — the turf of the device in your pocket, on your desk, and in your home. OpenAI is building a piece of hardware designed to do what the iPhone does, only without a screen and without apps. Apple, OpenAI argues, knows it, fears it, and is using the courts to stop it.

The case Apple started: July 2026 and the two engineers

To understand Friday's countersuit, you have to go back to July 2026, when Apple fired the first shot. On 10 July 2026, Apple sued OpenAI, its hardware unit io Products, and two former Apple employees — Tang Tan and Chang Liu — in the same Northern District of California court. Apple's complaint accused the two men of taking its trade secrets with them when they left Apple to join OpenAI, and accused OpenAI of using those secrets to build its competing device.

The language in Apple's original filing was blunt. According to the Engadget account, Apple's suit said the two former employees "surreptitiously accessed and downloaded dozens of Apple's confidential hardware-related files, including voluminous, detailed information about unreleased products, engineering presentations, technical specifications and proprietary project data." The accusation was not that they accidentally kept a few old files. It was that they deliberately raided Apple's systems for the blueprints of products that had not yet been announced — and carried them to a rival.

Apple's theory of the case was straightforward: OpenAI, desperate to build hardware and short of the decades of engineering knowledge Apple has accumulated, poached Apple's people to get its secrets. The two engineers, Apple said, were not hired for their talent alone. They were hired for what they knew — and what they could carry out of Cupertino on a hard drive.

OpenAI has denied those claims from the start. In October, the company swore to the court that it would not use the Apple trade secrets at the heart of the lawsuit — a pledge that Apple has repeatedly thrown back at it. Apple's statement on Friday pointedly noted that "OpenAI has already been ordered to stop using Apple's confidential information," a reminder that the court has already intervened once on Apple's side. Apple has also asked the court for a preliminary injunction, an order that would bar any further use of the secrets before a verdict is reached.

The judge overseeing the case is Edward Davila, who set the trial schedule in October. The trial is due to begin on 19 July 2027 at 9 a.m. — more than a year after Apple first sued, and more than two years after OpenAI announced the hardware project that started the whole fight. By then, OpenAI's first device is supposed to be on sale. Whether it will be — and under what name, and with whose secrets inside it — may depend on what happens in that courtroom.

The $6.5 billion device at the heart of it all

The reason two of the richest companies on earth are fighting over a handful of engineers and a stack of files is a device that does not exist yet. On 21 May 2025, OpenAI announced it was acquiring io Products, the hardware startup founded by Jony Ive — the designer behind the iPhone, the iPad and the Mac — in an all-stock deal valued at approximately $6.5 billion. It was OpenAI's largest acquisition ever, and its most ambitious bet: that the company that built ChatGPT could also build the machine ChatGPT lives in.

The public story, when the deal was announced, was a "third core device" — something that would sit in a pocket or on a desk next to a laptop and a phone, designed by the person who shaped the iPhone, with ChatGPT as the interface instead of a grid of apps. The io team would merge into OpenAI, while Ive's independent design firm, LoveFrom, would take on design work across the company. No finished device, no price, no final name and no release date were announced. What was announced was an intention: to build a new family of AI-native devices, and to do it with the most famous industrial designer alive.

That is why Apple sued. Tan and Liu were not ordinary engineers. They were part of the team Apple had assembled over decades to design the hardware that made it the most valuable company in the world — and they had gone to work for the company building Apple's replacement. Apple's lawsuit was, in that sense, a defensive move: a company watching its most precious asset, its hardware talent, walk out of the door to a rival with $6.5 billion to spend.

But the device itself has been slower to arrive than anyone expected. In a court filing in February 2026, tied to a trademark suit brought by the audio startup iyO, OpenAI told a court that its first consumer hardware device would not ship to customers before the end of February 2027. The filing, a sworn statement from Peter Welinder, OpenAI's vice president and general manager, also said the company had produced no packaging and no marketing materials for the device, and that it had decided not to use the name "io" — or any capitalisation of it — on any AI hardware it sells. The trademark fight had forced a rebrand before the product even had a box.

What the device actually is remains, officially, a secret. Bloomberg reported on 6 August 2026 that the first product is a screen-free smart speaker, roughly the size and shape of a hockey puck, priced above $300, with a camera, a battery for room-to-room portability, and small motorised components designed to give it a sense of physical aliveness. Other reporting has described it as a portable speaker with no screen, about the size of a hockey puck, relying on ChatGPT voice capabilities, with moving parts designed to express emotion. A phone may follow — supply-chain reporting points to a potential AI-focused smartphone that could debut in late 2027 or 2028, with assembly reportedly handled by FIH Mobile, a subsidiary of Foxconn. The stated aim, according to reporting, is to move 100 million units quickly once products reach the market.

None of those specifications has been confirmed by OpenAI. The company has never officially disclosed the first device's final form, name, price, operating system or complete feature set. What is confirmed is the ambition — and the reason for it. Tony Fadell, the former Apple executive who helped create the iPod and the iPhone, said recently that Sam Altman asked Jony Ive to build OpenAI a physical device because the AI company cannot get the data it needs from the iPhones its software runs on. OpenAI's hardware push, in Fadell's account, is a strategic necessity, not a vanity project: whoever owns the device owns the data, and whoever owns the data wins the AI race.

Why Apple is afraid — and why OpenAI is angry

The fight makes more sense when you look at where each company stands. Apple is the richest hardware company in human history, and the iPhone is the most successful consumer product ever made. But in artificial intelligence, Apple has spent the last two years playing catch-up. Its Apple Intelligence features arrived late, its Siri overhaul has been delayed again and again, and its own engineers have reportedly warned that the company's AI models lag a generation behind its rivals. OpenAI's filing says so outright: Apple, it argues, "has struggled to incorporate innovative AI capabilities into its previously popular products."

That is a wound Apple does not like having pressed. The company built its empire on being first — or at least on being best. Now it is watching a company founded barely a decade ago, with no factories, no stores and no supply chain, announce that it will build a device to replace the iPhone, designed by the very man who designed the iPhone. Apple's July lawsuit was, in one reading, the act of a company that felt its crown slipping and reached for the only weapon it had: the courts.

OpenAI, meanwhile, is angry for reasons of its own. It has spent $6.5 billion on a hardware bet that has already been delayed by a year, forced into a rebrand by a trademark suit, and held up by a court that has already ordered it to stop using Apple's secrets. Now, on top of all that, it says Apple is leaning on the suppliers it needs to actually build the thing — asking factories to name the workers assigned to OpenAI's projects, and pressing at least one vendor to walk away. If that is true, it is not a lawsuit. It is a blockade.

There is also a personal edge to the fight that no filing will admit. Jony Ive spent nearly three decades at Apple, where he was, by most accounts, the closest thing the company had to a soul after Steve Jobs. He left in 2019. His startup, io, was in many ways a continuation of the work he had done at Apple — designing objects that people love to touch. For Apple to watch Ive build a rival device with OpenAI's money, using engineers Apple trained, is a humiliation as well as a threat. And for Ive, whose name is on the patents behind the most profitable products in history, the accusation that his team stole Apple's secrets must sting in a different way.

The market, for now, is unbothered. Apple shares rose 0.8 per cent in Friday's shortened Black Friday session, and were little changed in after-hours trading, according to the StockAnalysis report. Investors, it seems, do not think a lawsuit will slow either company down. They may be right. But the trial date — 19 July 2027 — now hangs over both companies' product plans like a storm cloud, and the preliminary injunction Apple has requested could, if granted, freeze OpenAI's hardware work long before a jury ever hears the case.

The supplier war: the fight nobody sees

The most consequential part of OpenAI's filing may be the part that has nothing to do with stolen files. The accusation that Apple is "coercing and threatening" suppliers goes to the heart of how hardware is actually made — and how a giant can kill a rival without ever going to court.

Modern consumer electronics are not built by one company. They are built by a web of suppliers: chipmakers, screen manufacturers, battery suppliers, camera-module makers, and the giant contract manufacturers like Foxconn and Luxshare that assemble the finished product. Those suppliers work for many companies at once, and they depend on Apple's enormous orders for their survival. When Apple asks a shared supplier to identify which of its people are working on OpenAI's projects, or presses a vendor to stop work for a rival, it is using the weight of those orders as leverage. A supplier that depends on Apple for half its revenue does not say no to Apple — not if it wants to stay in business.

That is why OpenAI's claim matters, and why it may be hard to prove. Supplier conversations happen behind closed doors, in emails and phone calls that are rarely written down in the form a court needs. But if OpenAI can produce documents or witnesses showing that Apple threatened to pull orders from a vendor that worked with it, the case stops being a trade-secret dispute and becomes something bigger: an antitrust question about whether the world's most powerful hardware company is using its market power to crush a competitor before it can compete.

OpenAI's filing also accuses Apple of encouraging its own staff to keep work files in their personal iCloud accounts and to do company business over their personal iMessage accounts — a practice that, if true, would make it far easier for secrets to walk out of the building, and far harder for Apple to know what left. It is a pointed accusation, because it turns Apple's own security practices against it: a company that accuses others of stealing its secrets, OpenAI implies, was careless with those secrets itself.

The supplier angle also explains the timing. OpenAI's first device is not expected to ship before the end of February 2027 — and the trial does not start until July 2027. That means the device, if it ships on schedule, will reach customers months before a jury decides who stole what. The fight over suppliers is the fight that matters now, in the months before launch, when OpenAI needs factories, parts and manufacturing slots. If Apple can choke off that supply chain, the device may never ship at all — and the trial will be a formality.

A graveyard of AI gadgets — and a $6.5 billion gamble

There is one more reason to be cautious about the hype, and it has nothing to do with lawsuits. The history of AI hardware is, so far, a history of failure. The Humane AI Pin — a screenless, voice-controlled pin backed by former Apple designers — arrived in 2024 to enormous hype and failed on contact with real users. The Rabbit R1, a small orange box that promised to do your tasks for you, fared no better. Both products were supposed to replace the smartphone. Both ended up as cautionary tales.

OpenAI knows this. Its device has been delayed, rebranded and kept secret for a reason: the company understands that shipping a bad gadget would be worse than shipping nothing. The reported specifications — a hockey-puck speaker with a camera and moving parts, priced above $300 — sound modest compared with the $6.5 billion spent to build it. And the target of 100 million units, reported in the trade press, would require OpenAI to sell more devices in its first years than most hardware companies sell in a decade.

But OpenAI also has something Humane and Rabbit never had: ChatGPT, used by hundreds of millions of people every week, and Jony Ive, the designer who has already built the most successful hardware products in history. The bet is that the combination — the best AI model and the best industrial designer — can do what nobody else has done: build a device people actually want to carry, not because it replaces their phone, but because it does something their phone cannot.

That is the bet Apple is afraid of. And that is the bet the courts will now decide, in part, whether OpenAI is allowed to make.

What happens next

The case now moves into the long, slow grind of American litigation. The next milestone is the preliminary injunction Apple has requested — a hearing that could bar OpenAI from using the disputed secrets, or from marketing its hardware, before the trial even begins. A separate trademark case, the iyO suit that already forced OpenAI to abandon the "io" name, has a preliminary injunction hearing scheduled for January 2027, according to reporting on that case. February 2027 is when OpenAI has told a court its first device could ship. July 2027 is when the trial begins. The calendar is crowded, and every date on it is a risk for OpenAI.

For Apple, the risks are different. If OpenAI proves that Apple stole its secrets, or that Apple threatened its suppliers, the company that prides itself on privacy and fair dealing will face a very public reckoning — and the possibility of damages, injunctions and antitrust scrutiny. Apple's own statement insists the case is simple: its employees' secrets were stolen, and OpenAI must be stopped. But the countersuit has made the case anything but simple. Now there are two accusers, two accused, and a judge who must sort out which side's story is true.

The deeper question is what this fight means for the rest of the technology industry. The AI race has been, until now, a race of models and data centres — of who can train the biggest system and rent the most computing power. This lawsuit says the next phase of the race will be fought over hardware: over who builds the device that sits between people and AI, and who controls the data that device collects. Apple has the factories, the supply chain and the stores. OpenAI has the model, the users and the designer. The courtroom in San Jose will decide whether both are allowed to compete — or whether one of them gets to use the law to stop the other from trying.

One thing is certain: the device war has begun, and it will not be settled in a keynote. It will be settled in court, one filing at a time, starting with the two lawsuits now sitting on Judge Davila's desk. The trial is 19 July 2027. The first device is due in February. Between now and then, the most interesting product in technology is not a gadget. It is a lawsuit.
