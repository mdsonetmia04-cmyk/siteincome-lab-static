TITLE: Copa America Is Coming Back to the USA in 2028 — 16 Teams, 1 Month, and a Final Just Hours Before the LA Olympics LABELS: Sports SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Copa America returns to the USA in 2028 — 16 teams, June 9 to July 9, with the final just before the Los Angeles Olympics begin. ---BODY---

Hard Rock Stadium in Miami, Florida, which hosted the 2024 Copa America final. The tournament returns to the United States in 2028. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0).

The Copa America is coming back to the United States. CONMEBOL, the South American football confederation, and CONCACAF, the governing body for North America, announced on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, that the 49th edition of the world's oldest international football tournament will be played on American soil from June 9 to July 9, 2028 — the third time in five editions that the United States has hosted, and the second time in a row. Sixteen teams will take part: all ten South American nations, joined by six from North America, Central America and the Caribbean, who will qualify through the 2027 Gold Cup. The final will be played just before the Los Angeles Olympics begin, in a summer that will make the United States the centre of the sporting world. For American football — for the fans who filled stadiums in 2024, for the cities that will bid to host, and for a national team that will play a major tournament on home soil two years after the World Cup — the announcement is the beginning of a long and lucrative countdown.

The decision is, in many ways, a vote of confidence in the American market. The 2024 tournament, also hosted by the United States, drew more than 1.5 million fans to matches across the country, and the final — Argentina's 1-0 victory over Colombia at Hard Rock Stadium in Miami — was one of the most watched football matches in American television history. The 2028 edition will be the third American hosting in twelve years, after the centenary tournament of 2016 and the 2024 edition, and it will be the first time the tournament has been staged in the same country twice in succession. "The CONMEBOL Copa America is much more than a tournament: it is part of our identity, our history, and the greatness of South American football," said CONMEBOL President Alejandro Dominguez in the joint announcement. "In 2028, we will once again bring that passion to the United States, reaffirming our historic partnership with CONCACAF and our shared commitment to continuing to drive the growth of football across the continent."

CONCACAF President Victor Montagliani struck a similar note, describing the agreement as a reflection of his confederation's commitment to the growth of the game. "Our strategic partnership with CONMEBOL reflects our confederation's commitment to the growth of football," he said in a statement. "This agreement will bring six Concacaf national teams a new opportunity to compete against some of the best in the world, while further reinforcing the competitive significance of our Concacaf Gold Cup." The language is diplomatic, but the substance is commercial. The Copa America in the United States is, by a wide margin, the most lucrative version of the tournament that CONMEBOL can stage. American stadiums are among the largest in the world, American television rights are among the most valuable, and the American market — with its large South American diaspora, its growing football culture, and its appetite for major events — is the one place where a South American tournament can fill NFL stadiums and command prime-time audiences. The 2028 decision is, in that sense, a business decision as much as a sporting one. And it is a decision that both confederations, having seen the numbers from 2024, were always likely to make.

A month of football — and then the Olympics

The dates are the most striking detail of the announcement. The tournament will run from June 9 to July 9, 2028 — a full month of football, ending just days before the Los Angeles Olympics begin on July 14. The timing is deliberate, and it is tight. The first pre-Olympic group-stage men's soccer matches begin on July 10, only hours after the Copa America final, and the Olympic football tournament will then run through the Games themselves. For the players, the calendar will be brutal: a month of Copa America, followed immediately — for those selected for their Olympic teams — by a second tournament, in the same country, with barely a pause between them. For the fans, it will be a summer without a break: the Copa America in June, the Olympics in July, and, for the cities that host both, a season of sport that will dominate the American summer.

The proximity of the two events is not an accident. It is a consequence of a calendar that has, over the past decade, increasingly concentrated the world's biggest football tournaments in the United States. The 2026 World Cup — hosted by the United States, Canada and Mexico — will have been played two years earlier. The 2028 Copa America will follow, and the Olympics will follow that. For the American soccer establishment, the sequence is an opportunity: a chance to build, over four years, a football culture that can sustain itself between the great events, and to prove that the United States is not merely a venue for other people's tournaments, but a football nation in its own right. For the players, it is a test of endurance. And for the organisers, it is a logistical challenge of the first order — two major tournaments, in the same summer, in the same country, sharing stadiums, hotels, airports and audiences.

The 2028 tournament will feature 16 teams, the same format as 2024. All ten CONMEBOL nations — Argentina, Brazil, Uruguay, Colombia, Chile, Peru, Paraguay, Ecuador, Bolivia and Venezuela — will take part, joined by six CONCACAF teams. The six North American places will be decided by the 2027 Gold Cup, the North American championship, which takes place next summer. The exact mechanism of qualification will be announced at a later date, as will the host cities, the venues and the match schedule. That last detail — the host cities — is the one that will dominate the next year of speculation. In 2024, the tournament was played in 14 stadiums across the United States, from Miami to Seattle, from New York to Los Angeles. The 2028 edition is likely to follow a similar pattern, and the cities that hosted in 2024 will be the favourites to host again.

Which cities will host?

The federations said they will announce details about host cities and venues at a later date — but the speculation has already begun, and the frontrunners are clear. Looking back at the 2024 tournament, there are some obvious candidates. Miami, where the 2024 final was played at Hard Rock Stadium, is close to a certainty: the city is the heart of South American football in the United States, home to a large diaspora, to Inter Miami, and to the stadium that hosted the most memorable match of the last tournament. New York and New Jersey, whose MetLife Stadium hosted the 2026 World Cup final and has a capacity of more than 82,000, is another near-certainty. San Francisco, Dallas, Atlanta and Houston — all of which hosted matches in 2024, and all of which have the stadiums, the airports and the audiences to support a major tournament — seem like locks as well.

Beyond those, Kansas City, Philadelphia, Charlotte and Denver will all be in contention, according to NBC Sports, which noted that the cities that hosted in 2024 have a clear advantage. The competition to host will be fierce, because the rewards are substantial. A Copa America match brings tens of thousands of visitors, millions of dollars in spending, and a global television audience. For a city, hosting is not only a matter of prestige. It is a matter of money — of hotel rooms filled, of restaurants booked, of a place on the map of world football. The bidding process, when it begins, will be watched as closely as the tournament itself, and the cities that win will have earned a place in the biggest summer of sport the United States has ever staged.

There is a deeper question, too, about what the American hosting means for the tournament's identity. The Copa America is, as Dominguez said, part of the identity of South American football — a tournament that has been played since 1916, that has produced some of the greatest matches and the greatest players in the history of the game, and that belongs, in the imagination of its fans, to the continent that created it. To stage it in the United States, for the third time in five editions, is to acknowledge that the tournament's future — its money, its audience, its growth — lies, at least in part, in the north. That is a reality that some South American fans will welcome, and that others will regret. But it is a reality that the numbers support. The 2024 tournament drew more than 1.5 million fans. The television audiences were among the largest in the tournament's history. And the commercial success of the American editions has, by all accounts, been transformative for CONMEBOL's finances. The 2028 decision is, in that sense, a recognition that the Copa America is no longer only a South American tournament. It is a tournament of the Americas — and the Americas, for the moment, are most profitably served from the United States.

Argentina, Brazil and the question of who wins

On the pitch, the 2028 tournament will be shaped by the same forces that have shaped the Copa America for a century: the rivalry between Argentina and Brazil, the emergence of Colombia and Uruguay, and the question of whether the United States — as host, and as a team that has improved steadily over the past decade — can make a deep run on home soil. Argentina arrive as the defending champions, having won the 2024 tournament with a 1-0 victory over Colombia in the final at Hard Rock Stadium, and having won the 2021 edition as well. They are, by any measure, the team to beat. But the 2028 tournament will be played two years after the 2026 World Cup, and the squads that take the field in June 2028 will look very different from the ones that played in 2024. The great players of the current generation will be older, or gone. The next generation — in Argentina, in Brazil, in the United States — will be taking their place.

Brazil, the tournament's other great power, will arrive with a point to prove. The five-time world champions have not won the Copa America since 2019, and their performances in recent editions have been disappointing by their own standards. The 2028 tournament, played in the United States, in front of the large Brazilian diaspora, will be an opportunity to reassert themselves — and to remind the football world that the Copa America is, historically, a tournament that Brazil expects to win. Colombia, the beaten finalists of 2024, will also be dangerous. Their run to the final two years ago was the best performance in their history, and the core of that team will still be in its prime in 2028. Uruguay, the tournament's most successful nation alongside Argentina, with 15 titles, will be there too, and their history — of winning when least expected — makes them a threat in any edition.

And then there is the United States. The host nation's team has, in recent years, shown that it can compete with the best in the Americas — and that it can also, on a bad day, lose to anyone. The 2028 tournament will be played on home soil, in front of home crowds, in the stadiums that the American team knows best. It will be the first major tournament the United States has hosted since the 2026 World Cup, and it will be a test of whether the progress of the past decade — the investment in youth development, the growth of Major League Soccer, the increasing number of American players in Europe — has produced a team that can win a tournament, rather than merely participate in one. The 2024 Copa America, for the United States, ended in disappointment: a group-stage exit, on home soil, that was widely seen as a failure. The 2028 edition is a chance to put that right — and to do so in front of the same crowds, in the same stadiums, with the memory of 2024 still fresh.

The Gold Cup gateway

For the six CONCACAF teams that will join the South American ten, the road to 2028 runs through the 2027 Gold Cup. The Gold Cup, the North American championship, takes place next summer, and it will now carry a double significance: it is not only the championship of North America, but the qualifying tournament for the Copa America that follows. That changes the stakes of the Gold Cup considerably. In previous cycles, the Gold Cup was, for the strongest teams, a tournament to be won; for the weaker ones, a tournament to be survived. In 2027, it will be a tournament in which six places in the Copa America are at stake — and in which the teams that qualify will earn the chance to play Argentina and Brazil, in the United States, in front of the world.

The qualification mechanism — which teams, and how many, qualify from the Gold Cup — has not yet been announced, and the federations said that details will be communicated in the near future. But the principle is clear: the Gold Cup will be the gateway, and the teams that perform best in 2027 will be the ones that take their place in the Copa America a year later. For the smaller nations of CONCACAF — the Caribbean islands, the Central American teams that have rarely played in a major tournament — the 2027 Gold Cup is now the most important tournament in their history. A good run could earn them a place in the Copa America, and a place in the Copa America could change a national team's trajectory for a generation. For the United States and Mexico, the region's two giants, qualification is all but assured — but the Gold Cup will still matter, as a test of form, as a rehearsal for 2028, and as a tournament that, for the first time, leads directly to something bigger.

The arrangement also reinforces the competitive significance of the Gold Cup itself, as Montagliani noted in his statement. The Gold Cup has, at times, struggled for attention — overshadowed by the World Cup, by the Copa America, by the club football that dominates the summer. By making it the qualifying tournament for the Copa America, CONCACAF has given it a purpose that it has not had before. The 2027 edition will be watched, by fans and by coaches, with a new intensity — because what happens in the Gold Cup will determine who plays in the Copa America, and who does not. It is a clever piece of tournament design, and it is one that benefits both confederations: CONMEBOL gets a stronger, more competitive field, and CONCACAF gets a Gold Cup that matters more than it ever has.

What 2024 taught the organisers

The 2028 tournament will be built on the lessons of 2024 — and there were lessons to learn. The 2024 edition was, by most measures, a commercial success: more than 1.5 million fans, strong television ratings, and a final that captured the attention of the American public. But it was not without its problems. The final at Hard Rock Stadium was marred by chaotic scenes outside the ground, as fans without tickets attempted to enter the stadium, causing delays and raising serious questions about security and crowd management. The tournament's pitches — laid over American football fields, in stadiums designed for a different sport — were criticised by players and coaches, who said the surfaces were too small, too hard, and too uneven for the standard of football the tournament demanded. And the scheduling, which saw matches played in the heat of the American summer, in cities thousands of miles apart, tested the endurance of players and the patience of fans.

The organisers of 2028 will have to address those issues. The security arrangements for the final will be scrutinised as never before. The pitches will have to meet the standards that the world's best players expect — and that FIFA, which will have just staged a World Cup in the same country, will have shown can be met. The travel, the heat, and the scheduling will have to be managed with a care that the 2024 edition, at times, seemed to lack. None of this is insurmountable. The United States has, in the World Cup of 2026, demonstrated that it can stage a major football tournament at scale. The question is whether the lessons of that World Cup — and of the Copa America of 2024 — will be applied to 2028, or whether the same mistakes will be repeated. The federations have two years to get it right. The players, the fans, and the television audiences will be watching to see whether they do.

There is also the question of the stadiums themselves. The 2024 tournament was played in 14 stadiums, most of them built for American football, with capacities of 60,000 or more. The 2028 edition is likely to use a similar set — and the choice of stadiums will shape the tournament in ways that go beyond capacity. A match played in a 70,000-seat NFL stadium, with the stands far from the pitch, feels very different from a match played in a 25,000-seat soccer-specific stadium, where the crowd is on top of the action. The 2024 tournament, by and large, chose scale over atmosphere, and the result was, at times, a tournament that felt distant — watched by large crowds, but in stadiums that were not built for football. The 2028 organisers will face the same choice, and the same trade-off. The economics favour the big stadiums. The football, arguably, favours the smaller ones. Which they choose will say something about what the tournament is for.

The summer of 2028 — and what it means for American football

The summer of 2028 will be, for the United States, an extraordinary season of sport. The Copa America will run from June 9 to July 9. The Los Angeles Olympics will begin on July 14, and will run through July 30, with the first men's soccer matches beginning on July 10 — hours after the Copa final. In the space of seven weeks, the United States will host two of the biggest sporting events on the planet, in the same country, in the same summer, with the same stadiums and the same audiences. It is a concentration of sport that the country has never seen before, and it will be a test of the American sporting infrastructure — of its stadiums, its transport, its hotels, its security — on a scale that has no precedent.

For American football, the summer is an opportunity and a risk. The opportunity is obvious: a month of Copa America, followed by an Olympic football tournament, will put the sport in front of the American public for seven consecutive weeks, in prime time, in the biggest stadiums, with the best players in the world. It is the kind of exposure that football in the United States has always dreamed of — and that, in the past, it has only ever had in brief, isolated bursts. The risk is that the exposure will be too much, too fast — that the American public, having watched a month of Copa America, will be tired of football by the time the Olympics begin, or that the two tournaments will cannibalise each other's audiences, each diminishing the other. The organisers will have to manage that risk carefully, and the federations will have to think hard about how to make the two events feel distinct, rather than redundant.

But the larger significance of the 2028 announcement is not about a single summer. It is about the direction of football in the Americas. The decision to stage the Copa America in the United States for the third time in five editions, and for the second time in succession, is a recognition that the centre of gravity of the game in the Americas has shifted — that the money, the audience, and the future of the sport are, increasingly, in the north. That shift has been underway for a decade, and it has been driven by the growth of Major League Soccer, by the success of the American hosting of 2016 and 2024, and by the willingness of American audiences to embrace a tournament that is not their own. The 2028 Copa America is the latest, and the most emphatic, expression of that shift. It is a tournament that belongs to South America, played in North America, for an audience that spans both. And it is, for the fans who will fill the stadiums in June and July 2028, the beginning of a wait that will feel, for the next nineteen months, very long indeed.

What to watch next

The first thing to watch is the announcement of the host cities and venues, which the federations said will come at a later date. That announcement will set the geography of the tournament — which cities, which stadiums, which matches where — and it will begin the long process of preparation in the cities that are chosen. The second is the 2027 Gold Cup, which will decide the six CONCACAF teams that qualify, and which will, for the first time, be played with the Copa America at stake. The third is the 2026 World Cup, which will be played in the United States, Canada and Mexico, and which will shape the squads, the form, and the expectations of every team that will play in 2028. The fourth is the announcement of the match schedule — the dates, the kick-off times, the order of the matches — which will determine how the tournament is watched, and by whom.

And then, in June 2028, the football itself. Sixteen teams, ten from the south, six from the north, playing for the oldest international trophy in the game, in the country that has become its second home. Argentina will arrive as champions. Brazil will arrive as challengers. Colombia and Uruguay will arrive as threats. The United States will arrive as hosts, with a point to prove and a crowd behind them. And the final, on July 9, will be played in a stadium that has yet to be named, in front of a crowd that has yet to be counted, hours before the Olympic flame is lit in Los Angeles. It is, by any measure, a tournament worth waiting for. The wait, as of today, is nineteen months. The countdown has begun.
