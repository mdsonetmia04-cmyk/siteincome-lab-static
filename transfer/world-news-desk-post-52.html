TITLE: US Economy Just Grew 4.3% — Fastest in 3 Years as AI Spending Drives the Biggest Investment Surge Since Covid LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: US GDP grew 4.3% in the third quarter, revised up from 3.4%, as business investment surged 16.1% and consumer spending rose 4.2% ---BODY---

The New York Stock Exchange. The US economy grew at an annual rate of 4.3% in the third quarter of 2026, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis — the fastest pace in three years. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (freely licensed).

The American economy is growing much faster than anyone thought. The US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) reported on Wednesday that real gross domestic product (GDP) increased at an annual rate of 4.3 per cent in the third quarter of 2026 — the three months of July, August and September — in its second estimate of the quarter's growth. The figure is a sharp upward revision from the 3.4 per cent reported in the advance estimate last month, an increase of 0.9 percentage points that caught Wall Street by surprise: economists had expected a token revision to 3.5 per cent, and the 4.3 per cent print was almost a full point above even that. It is the fastest pace of growth in three years, since the third quarter of 2023, and nearly double the 2.2 per cent rate recorded in the second quarter of this year. The economy, in short, did not merely grow in the third quarter. It accelerated — and it did so on the back of a business investment boom, driven by spending on artificial intelligence, that is the biggest since the reopening of the economy after Covid.

The revision is remarkable not only for its size but for what drove it. According to the BEA, the 0.9 percentage point upgrade was primarily driven by upward revisions to investment and consumer spending — the two engines of the American economy — and the details, released alongside the headline number, tell a story of a business sector spending at a pace not seen in years. Gross private domestic investment surged 22.4 per cent in the quarter and contributed 3.74 percentage points to GDP growth, up from 3.27 points in the advance estimate. Business investment — nonresidential fixed investment, the spending by companies on equipment, buildings and intellectual property — rose at a robust 16.1 per cent annualised rate, up from the 12.7 per cent first reported, and the most since the reopening quarter of the third quarter of 2020. Fixed investment as a whole grew at a 12.0 per cent annualised rate, the fastest since the fourth quarter of 2020, when the economy was still bouncing off the lockdown lows. And the gains were widespread: spending on equipment jumped 21.8 per cent, investment in intellectual property products rose 13.6 per cent, and spending on structures increased 9.5 per cent — all three components pulling in the same direction, for the first time in a long while.

Consumer spending, which accounts for more than two-thirds of the American economy, was also stronger than first reported. Personal consumption expenditures rose 4.2 per cent in the quarter, up from the 3.8 per cent reported in the advance estimate, and contributed 2.78 percentage points to GDP growth. The improvement came from both sides of the consumer's wallet: spending on goods rose 5.0 per cent, up from 3.8 per cent, driven by pharmaceuticals, recreational goods and vehicles, and gasoline, while spending on services rose 3.9 per cent, up from 3.4 per cent. The combination — strong consumers and booming business investment — produced a measure of underlying demand that economists watch more closely than the headline number itself. Final sales to private domestic purchasers, which strips out inventories, trade and government spending to capture the core strength of household and business demand, rose 5.8 per cent — its strongest quarterly gain, outside the pandemic period, since 2003, according to TD Economics, and the fastest since the second quarter of 2021 by another measure. It was initially estimated at 4.9 per cent. The revision, in other words, did not just make the quarter look better. It made the underlying economy look stronger than it has in a generation.

The AI investment boom — equipment up 21.8%, software driving two-thirds of the gain

Behind the investment numbers is a story that has come to dominate the American economy in 2026: artificial intelligence, and the extraordinary sums of money being poured into the infrastructure that powers it. Business investment has now posted three straight quarters of double-digit growth, and the third quarter's surge was, by the account of analysts who have studied the BEA release, an AI capital expenditure boom in all but name. Equipment spending — which includes servers, networking gear and everything else that goes inside a data centre — contributed 8.2 percentage points to the growth in nonresidential investment, or about half the total. Investment in intellectual property products contributed 5.2 points, and structures — the buildings themselves, including the data centres that have finally become large enough to offset the slump in every other kind of commercial building — contributed 1.8 points, its first meaningful positive contribution in more than a year.

The details of the intellectual property numbers are particularly striking. Investment in intellectual property products grew at a 13.6 per cent annualised rate, up from about 9 per cent in the second quarter, and the third time in the past six quarters that the series has printed above 13 per cent. Of that 13.6 per cent, software alone accounted for 8.9 points — 65 per cent of the total — with research and development adding 3.7 points. The upward revision to business investment was driven, in large part, by the government's incorporation of revised software price data, which showed companies spending more on software than had previously been counted. It is a reminder of how the modern economy works: the most important capital spending in America today is not on factories or trucks, but on code — on the software, the models and the computing infrastructure that power artificial intelligence, and on the data centres that house them.

There are, however, warnings attached to the boom, and economists have been quick to make them. Business investment has posted three straight quarters of double-digit growth, but economists warned that the trend could slow as investors scrutinise AI valuations — the question, now asked openly on Wall Street, of whether the hundreds of billions of dollars being spent on AI infrastructure will ever generate the returns to justify it. The third quarter's numbers do not answer that question. They show only that the spending is happening, at a scale and a pace that has not been seen since the reopening of the economy after Covid, and that it is, for now, the single most powerful force in the American economy. Business investment alone was worth a little over two percentage points of the 4.3 per cent GDP print — roughly half of the entire quarter's growth. Take away the investment boom, and the economy grew at barely half the reported rate. That is the measure of how much the American economy now depends on AI spending — and of how much is riding on the bet that it will pay off.

Consumers, profits and the other side of the ledger — 4.2% spending, 21.3% profit growth, and a 3.21-point trade drag

The consumer, the other half of the story, is spending with a confidence that the headline numbers only partly capture. Personal consumption expenditures rose 4.2 per cent in the third quarter, and the upward revision from 3.8 per cent reflected stronger spending on prescription drugs, computer software and accessories, and gasoline than had previously been estimated. Households, in other words, are spending more on their health, on their technology, and on getting around — the three categories that define modern American consumption. The strength of the consumer is all the more notable given what happened in October, the first month of the fourth quarter: a separate BEA report released on the same day showed that inflation-adjusted personal spending was little changed from a month earlier in October, suggesting that the consumer's momentum may be cooling as the year ends. The BEA also said the rise in incomes in October partly reflected one-time payments associated with the Medicare Improvement Fund premium rebate, although underlying wages and salaries continued to advance. The third quarter was strong. The fourth quarter, on the early evidence, is starting more slowly.

Corporate profits, released alongside the GDP figures, tell a similarly strong story. Profits from current production rose 21.3 per cent in the third quarter from a year earlier, the fastest increase since 2021, and increased at a rate of 206.0 billion dollars in the quarter, after rising at a pace of 384.0 billion dollars in the April-to-June quarter. American companies, in other words, are not merely growing. They are making money at a pace not seen in five years — and the profits are coming alongside the investment, in a virtuous circle that has defined the strongest periods of the American economy. A separate measure of the economy, real gross domestic income (GDI) — an alternative gauge of output based on the income generated by economic activity rather than the spending — rose 4.4 per cent in the quarter, after rising 2.3 per cent in the second quarter, confirming that the growth is real on both sides of the national accounts.

Not everything in the report was strong. Net exports subtracted a whopping 3.21 percentage points from growth — the biggest trade drag since the tariff front-running of the first quarter of 2025 — with exports subtracting about 0.6 points and imports subtracting about 2.6 points. The drag reflects an American economy that is importing far more than it exports, a pattern that has been amplified by the tariff environment and by the sheer strength of domestic demand. Inventories added 1.64 percentage points to growth, as businesses rebuilt stocks, and government spending added a modest 0.97 points, with government consumption rising 5.7 per cent. Housing, meanwhile, was the weak spot: residential investment fell 3.4 per cent, reflecting weaker sales and construction activity, and a housing market that remains under pressure from high prices and the cost of borrowing. The economy grew 4.3 per cent in spite of housing, not because of it — and the contrast between the booming business sector and the struggling housing market is one of the defining features of the American economy in 2026.

The inflation picture — a 1.8% deflator, 2.5% core PCE, and room for the Fed to breathe

The growth numbers are only half the story. The other half is inflation — and here, too, the report delivered good news. The GDP deflator, the broadest measure of price changes in the economy, increased just 1.8 per cent in the third quarter, down from the 2.2 per cent reported in the advance estimate and below the 2.2 per cent consensus forecast. Core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) prices — the Federal Reserve's preferred measure of underlying inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy costs — rose 2.5 per cent, exactly in line with expectations and unchanged from the advance estimate. Headline PCE prices rose 2.1 per cent, also unchanged from the advance estimate. And the gross domestic purchases price index, which measures the prices Americans actually pay for what they buy, was revised down to 2.7 per cent from 3.0 per cent in the advance estimate. Nominal GDP — the value of the economy before adjusting for inflation — was revised up by only 0.6 points, to 6.2 per cent from 5.6 per cent, while real GDP was revised up by 0.9 points, which means the extra growth was real, not inflationary.

The combination — faster growth and cooler inflation — is the outcome every central banker dreams of, and it matters enormously for what the Federal Reserve does next. Strong growth with moderating prices gives the Fed room to keep interest rates where they are, or even to consider cutting them, without worrying that it is fuelling inflation. But it also creates a dilemma. An economy growing at 4.3 per cent, with business investment surging at 16.1 per cent and consumers spending at 4.2 per cent, is an economy that is running hot — and a central bank that cuts rates into an economy this strong risks reigniting the very inflation it has spent years trying to tame. The GDP report is backward-looking, as economists are quick to point out, but it will factor into the Fed's thinking, and into the market's expectations, in the weeks ahead. The report's own authors note that the figures will be superseded by the third estimate, due on December 23, 2026, alongside industry-level GDP data, corporate profits, state GDP and state personal income figures. Until then, the 4.3 per cent number is the number that matters — and it is a number that will be debated, dissected and argued over by everyone with a stake in the American economy.

The political dimension of the report is impossible to ignore, and it was visible within hours of the release. Supporters of the Trump administration celebrated the 4.3 per cent figure as proof of a strong economy, while critics argued that the number masks underlying weakness — pointing to the inflation that remains, to the proposed tariffs, and to a recovery that benefits the wealthy more than the working class. The debate is familiar, and it is one that will run through the rest of the year. What is not in doubt is the scale of the revision. The advance estimate of 3.4 per cent, released last month, was already a respectable number. The second estimate of 4.3 per cent is a different kind of number altogether — a number that puts the third quarter of 2026 among the strongest quarters of the past decade, and that reframes the entire conversation about the state of the American economy. The economy did not just grow in the third quarter. It grew at the fastest pace in three years, on the back of the biggest investment boom since Covid, with consumers spending, companies profiting, and inflation cooling. That is a combination that does not come around often.

Three years of context — from 2.1% to 4.3%, and the long road back

To appreciate the 4.3 per cent figure, it helps to put it in the context of the year that preceded it. The American economy grew at a rate of 2.1 per cent in the first quarter of 2026, and 2.2 per cent in the second quarter — respectable, steady, unremarkable numbers, the kind of growth that keeps an economy moving without setting anything alight. The third quarter's 4.3 per cent is nearly double the second quarter's pace, and it is the fastest since the third quarter of 2023. The jump was not gradual. It was sudden, driven by a surge in business investment that had been building for three quarters and finally broke through in the summer, and by a consumer who spent more than the first estimate had captured. The quarterly progression — 2.1 per cent, 2.2 per cent, 4.3 per cent — tells the story of an economy that was cruising, and then, in the space of three months, hit the accelerator.

The longer view is equally instructive. The third quarter of 2025 saw growth of 3.9 per cent, the fourth quarter of 2025 just 0.2 per cent, and the first quarter of 2026 2.5 per cent, by one table of the BEA's data — a reminder that the American economy has been volatile, swinging between strong quarters and weak ones, for the past two years. The 4.3 per cent print breaks that pattern, not because it is the highest number in the series, but because of what drove it. The final sales to private domestic purchasers figure — 5.8 per cent, the strongest since 2003 outside the pandemic — is the number that economists keep coming back to, because it measures the part of the economy that matters most: what households and businesses are actually buying, stripped of the noise of inventories, trade and government. When that number grows at 5.8 per cent, the economy is not merely growing. It is booming, in the places that count.

There is a caution in the data, and it is worth stating plainly. The third quarter's growth was flattered, in part, by the composition of the revision. The mix of consumer spending was less flattering than the headline: goods were revised higher on pharmaceuticals, recreational goods and vehicles, and gasoline, while services were revised lower, led by health care. The trade drag of 3.21 points is the largest since the tariff front-running of early 2025, and it is a reminder that the American economy's strength is, in part, a story of imports — of goods and services bought from abroad, at a pace that subtracts from growth even as it reflects domestic demand. And the October data, showing inflation-adjusted personal spending little changed from September, suggests that the consumer's momentum may already be fading. The third quarter was a remarkable quarter. Whether the fourth quarter will be is a question that the data, so far, has not answered.

What happens next — December 23, the Fed, and the question of whether the boom lasts

The next milestone is December 23, 2026, when the BEA will release its third estimate of third-quarter GDP, alongside industry-level GDP data, corporate profits, state GDP and state personal income figures. That release will refine the 4.3 per cent number — and revisions of this size are often revised again, in either direction, as more complete data becomes available. But the direction of the story is unlikely to change. The investment boom is real, the consumer is spending, and the economy grew at its fastest pace in three years in the third quarter. The question is not whether the third quarter was strong. It was. The question is whether the strength lasts — whether business investment can sustain a fourth straight quarter of double-digit growth, whether consumers can keep spending at a 4.2 per cent pace, and whether the AI boom that is driving the investment surge will deliver the returns to justify it.

For the Federal Reserve, the report is both a relief and a complication. A 4.3 per cent growth rate with a 1.8 per cent GDP deflator is the kind of combination that argues against urgent action in either direction — the economy is too strong to need stimulus, and inflation is too cool to demand restraint. But the Fed will be watching the fourth quarter closely, and the October spending data, showing a consumer who has pressed pause, will be part of that assessment. If the fourth quarter slows, the third quarter's boom will look like a peak. If it does not, the Fed will face the question of whether an economy growing at 4 per cent needs interest rates where they are, or whether it can afford to bring them down.

For American households, the numbers mean something simpler, and something more tangible. An economy growing at 4.3 per cent, with corporate profits rising 21.3 per cent and wages and salaries continuing to advance, is an economy in which jobs are plentiful, pay is rising, and businesses are investing in the future. It is also an economy in which the benefits are not evenly shared — in which the AI investment boom enriches the companies that build data centres and write software, while the housing market falls 3.4 per cent and the families trying to buy a home find the door still closed. The 4.3 per cent number is real, and it is remarkable. But it is an average, and averages hide as much as they reveal. The American economy grew at its fastest pace in three years in the third quarter of 2026. The question now is who felt it — and whether the boom, built on servers and software and data centres, will reach the people who are still waiting for it to arrive.
