TITLE: Italy Just Banned Burqas in Schools and Capped Foreign Pupils at 30% — 142 MPs Voted Yes, Fines Up to €1,000 LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Italy's parliament banned face veils in schools and capped foreign pupils at 30% of first-year classes. Fines reach €1,000; the cap starts in 2027. ---BODY---

Italy has made it law: no face veils in schools, and no more than 30 per cent foreign pupils in a first-year classroom. The Chamber of Deputies, the lower house of the Italian parliament, gave final approval to the government's education decree on Thursday, 26 November 2026, by 142 votes to 112, with four abstentions. The ban on face coverings took effect immediately. The classroom cap begins with the 2027–28 school year. And the fines for breaking the veil ban run from €200 to €1,000 — payable by parents when the wearer is a minor.

The law is the most far-reaching school integration measure Italy has ever passed, and one of the most controversial education laws in Europe this year. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's right-wing government calls it integration. The opposition calls it discrimination. The country's Muslim communities call it a punishment aimed squarely at them. And the rest of Europe is watching closely, because Italy has just done what France did a generation ago — only further, and with a classroom quota attached that no other major European country has tried.

The decree does far more than ban the burqa. It obliges foreign parents whose limited Italian prevents effective communication with schools to attend language courses if social services instruct them to do so. It brings in measures to support pupils with disabilities, to combat cyberbullying, and to prevent children from dropping out of school. But nobody is talking about those parts. The argument — in parliament, in the press, and in school gates across Italy — is about two things: the veil, and the 30 per cent.

What the law actually says

The text of the law is, in one respect, careful. It does not name the burqa or the niqab. Instead, it bars pupils from taking part in lessons and other school activities if their faces are covered or concealed in any way that prevents identification. The government has presented the measure openly as targeting the burqa and the niqab — the garments that cover the face entirely — and the ban applies to everyone on the entire school grounds, not just in classrooms. But the wording matters, because it is the wording that will be tested in court.

Headscarves that leave the face uncovered are not subject to the ban. A hijab — the headscarf worn by millions of Muslim women and girls, which covers the hair but not the face — remains legal in Italian schools. The line the law draws is identification: if a teacher can see the pupil's face and know who she is, the garment is allowed. If the face is hidden, it is not. That distinction will matter enormously in practice, because it is the difference between a law about security and a law about religion — and the difference, very likely, between a law that survives a court challenge and one that does not.

The fines are real. Violations may result in administrative fines of between €200 and €1,000 — roughly $225 to $1,120. When the wearer is a minor, the fine is payable by the parents. The ban applies during school activities, and — according to the reporting of the vote — to everyone on the entire school grounds. A mother in a niqab collecting her child from the school gate could, on a strict reading, be fined. That is the kind of detail that turns a parliamentary vote into a daily confrontation, and it is the kind of detail the opposition seized on during the debate.

The 30 per cent cap works differently, and more slowly. From the 2027–28 school year, foreign pupils with limited Italian-language skills must not account for more than 30 per cent of pupils in each first-year class at primary and secondary schools. The restriction is aimed, in the first instance, at newly arrived students. In the first grades of elementary school, the quota applies to children who do not hold Italian citizenship, were not born in Italy, and have not attended an Italian preschool for at least two years. In middle and high school, students must have attended Italian schools for at least three years to fall outside the quota.

The key phrase is "limited Italian." The cap does not apply to foreign pupils who have already acquired an adequate level of Italian. A child born in Italy to foreign parents, who speaks fluent Italian and has been through Italian nursery school, is not counted. A child who arrived last year from Bangladesh or Egypt or Morocco, and is still learning the language, is. The government says the rule is about language and integration, not nationality. The opposition says that is a distinction without a difference — that in practice, the cap will fall on the children of immigrants, and only on them.

How it passed: 142 to 112, and a government that would not blink

The vote in the Chamber of Deputies on 26 November was the end of a process that began in September, when Meloni's cabinet adopted the decree-law. Italy's parliament is bicameral, and the text had to clear both houses. The Senate approved it first, on 20 November, by 86 votes to 46. The Chamber of Deputies then gave it final approval on 26 November, by 142 votes to 112, with four abstentions, in the 400-seat chamber. The text now awaits the signature of President Sergio Mattarella and publication in the Official Gazette.

The numbers tell a story of their own. In the Senate, the government won by 40 votes. In the Chamber, by 30. The opposition — the Democratic Party, the Five Star Movement, and the smaller left-wing parties — voted against it in both houses, and the debate was, by Italian standards, unusually bitter. The opposition argued that the law was discriminatory, that it would stigmatise Muslim girls, and that a quota on foreign children was a form of segregation dressed up as integration. The government argued that a school cannot function if teachers cannot see their pupils' faces, and that a classroom in which a third of the children cannot speak Italian cannot teach anyone properly.

The decree-law mechanism matters too. In Italy, the government can adopt a decree-law that takes effect immediately, but parliament must convert it into law within 60 days or it lapses. The school decree was adopted by the cabinet in September, and the conversion process — Senate first, then the Chamber — consumed the autumn. The ban on face coverings, as a result, has in a sense already been in force for weeks, under the decree. What happened on 26 November was the parliament making it permanent: converting a temporary government order into a law of the republic.

Meloni herself has not hidden behind the technical language. Her government drafted the amendment, defended it in both houses, and presented it publicly as a measure to protect Italian schools and Italian identity. The prime minister leads Brothers of Italy, a party that rose to power on promises to control immigration and defend what it calls traditional Italian values. The school law is, in many ways, the purest expression of that programme in domestic policy: a law that tells immigrant families that integration in Italy will happen on Italian terms — in the Italian language, with the face uncovered, in classrooms where Italian children are the clear majority.

The parents must learn Italian too

The part of the law that has received the least attention may turn out to be the most consequential for immigrant families. The decree requires foreign parents whose limited Italian prevents effective communication with schools to attend language courses if instructed to do so by social services. In plain language: if a school cannot talk to a child's parents because the parents do not speak Italian, the state can order the parents to learn.

It is a striking extension of the state's reach into family life, and it raises questions the text does not answer. Who decides that a parent's Italian is insufficient — the school, or social services? What happens if a parent refuses to attend, or cannot attend because of work? Are there sanctions for non-compliance, and if so, what are they? The reporting on the law so far does not answer these questions, and the answers will be worked out in the months ahead, school by school, case by case.

But the intent is clear enough. The government believes that integration fails when parents cannot speak to teachers, cannot read school letters, cannot help with homework and cannot take part in school life. The language requirement for parents is the other side of the 30 per cent cap: the cap limits how many non-Italian-speaking children can be in a classroom, and the parental requirement tries to make sure the children's homes are not cut off from the school either. Together, they amount to a philosophy — that the school is the front line of integration, and that the state has the right to demand participation in it.

Critics see it differently. They argue that ordering adults to attend language courses, under threat of state intervention, is coercive, and that it treats immigrant parents as a problem to be managed rather than as partners in their children's education. They point out that many immigrant parents work long hours in jobs that leave little time for evening classes, and that the requirement may punish precisely the families who are already struggling most. The government, in response, says that language courses are a support, not a punishment — and that a parent who can speak Italian is a parent who can help their child succeed.

Italy is not the first — but it has gone further than most

Italy is not the first European country to ban face coverings in schools, and the history of those bans is a warning about how hard they are to enforce. France banned the wearing of conspicuous religious symbols in public schools in 2004, and banned the full-face veil in all public places in 2011. Denmark banned the burqa and niqab in public in 2018. Austria banned face coverings in schools in 2019 — a law its own constitutional court later struck down. The Netherlands, Belgium, Switzerland and several German states have their own versions. In every case, the debate was the same: security and integration on one side, religious freedom and discrimination on the other.

What makes Italy's law different is the combination. No other major European country has paired a face-veil ban with a numerical cap on foreign pupils in the classroom. The 30 per cent quota is genuinely new — a piece of social engineering that treats the composition of a classroom as something the state should control, like a budget or a curriculum. Supporters say it is common sense: a class in which a third of the children do not speak the language cannot function. Critics say it is a quota on children, and that quotas on children have a history that Europe should be ashamed of, not proud of.

The comparison that will haunt the debate is France. When France banned religious symbols in schools in 2004, it did so in the name of laïcité — the strict separation of religion and the state that is written into the French constitution. Italy has no such tradition. Its constitution protects religious freedom, and the Catholic Church remains a powerful presence in Italian public life — including in schools, where crucifixes still hang in many classrooms. The opposition has already made this point: a state that keeps the crucifix on the classroom wall, they argue, has no business telling a Muslim girl she cannot cover her face. The government has not answered that point convincingly, and it is the point a court may one day have to decide.

The European Court of Human Rights has, in the past, upheld face-veil bans, ruling that states have a wide margin of appreciation in deciding how to balance religious freedom against public order and the rights of others. But the court has never ruled on a classroom quota of the kind Italy has just introduced, and the combination of the two measures — a ban plus a cap — may face a harder test than either would alone. Legal challenges are already being discussed, and the law's opponents have made no secret of their intention to fight it in the courts as well as in parliament.

What happens in a real classroom on Monday morning

Laws are voted in Rome. They are lived in classrooms in Milan, Turin, Bologna and Palermo — and it is there, on Monday morning, that Italy's new law will meet reality. Consider what the ban means in practice. A teacher arrives at school and finds a pupil in a niqab at her desk. The law says the pupil may not take part in school activities with her face covered. The teacher must ask her to remove it. If she refuses, what then? Is she sent home? Is she put in a separate room? Is she marked absent? The law does not say. The school must decide, and every school will decide differently.

Now consider the 30 per cent cap. A primary school in a neighbourhood with a large immigrant population has a first-year class in which 12 of 30 children are foreign pupils with limited Italian. That is 40 per cent — above the cap. What happens? Are two of those children moved to another class, another school, another neighbourhood? Are they held back a year? Are they put in a separate language class until their Italian improves? The law says the cap applies from the 2027–28 school year, which gives schools a year to prepare — but it does not say how a school that is already above the cap is supposed to get below it. The logistics alone are daunting: in some parts of northern Italy, foreign pupils already make up far more than 30 per cent of first-year classes, and there is no obvious place for the excess children to go.

Then there is the question of who counts. The cap applies to children who do not hold Italian citizenship, were not born in Italy, and have not attended an Italian preschool for at least two years — or, at secondary level, have not attended Italian schools for at least three years. Applying that test requires the school to know each child's citizenship, birthplace and schooling history, and to assess each child's Italian. That is a bureaucratic exercise of considerable complexity, and it will be carried out by head teachers and administrators who have not been given new staff, new funding or new guidance. The risk is not just confusion. It is that the rule will be applied unevenly — strictly in some schools, loosely in others — and that the children who suffer will be the ones whose schools are least equipped to handle it.

And then there is the human cost, which no regulation can capture. A girl who has worn the niqab to school for years is told, one morning, that she must take it off or leave. A boy who has just arrived from Egypt, who speaks no Italian and knows no one, is told that he cannot join the class down the corridor because it already has too many children like him. These are the moments in which a law stops being a paragraph in the Official Gazette and becomes a memory a child carries for the rest of her life. The government says those moments are the price of integration. The opposition says they are the opposite — the moment integration fails.

The numbers behind the argument

The debate over the law has been fought mostly in the language of principle — freedom against security, integration against discrimination. But there are numbers behind it, and they help explain why the government acted now. Italy's foreign population has grown steadily for two decades, and its schools have changed with it. In some cities in the north — Milan, Turin, Brescia, Verona — foreign pupils now make up a large share of primary school enrolments, and in some individual schools they are the majority. The government has argued, repeatedly, that classrooms in which a large proportion of children do not speak Italian cannot deliver a proper education to anyone — not to the foreign children, who fall behind, and not to the Italian children, whose lessons slow down.

The 30 per cent figure is not arbitrary. It is the threshold the government believes marks the point at which a classroom stops functioning normally: below it, a teacher can manage; above it, the class becomes, in effect, a language school. Whether that threshold is real or invented is a matter of fierce dispute among educationalists, and the reporting on the law does not cite research to support it. But the political logic is clear: 30 per cent sounds reasonable, sounds moderate, sounds like a number a voter can accept. It is high enough to look like a compromise, and low enough to change the composition of a great many classrooms.

The vote itself also tells a story. The Chamber of Deputies has 400 seats. The government's majority — Meloni's coalition of Brothers of Italy, the League and Forza Italia — delivered 142 votes for the law. The opposition delivered 112 against. Four abstained. That leaves more than 140 deputies who did not vote at all — absent, or choosing not to be recorded. In a parliament where attendance is usually high for a flagship government bill, the scale of the absences suggests something the headlines have not captured: that plenty of deputies, on both sides, would rather not have their names attached to this law. It passed, but it passed with a silence around it that may prove as significant as the votes themselves.

The fines, too, are worth pausing on. A fine of €200 to €1,000 is not trivial for a family living on an immigrant's wage. For a mother fined because her daughter wore a niqab to school, €1,000 could be a month's rent. The government says the fines are a deterrent, not a revenue measure. The opposition says they are a tax on being Muslim. Either way, the fines mean the law will not be an abstraction. It will arrive, for some families, as a letter demanding money — and that letter will do more to shape public opinion than any speech in parliament.

What Europe is watching — and what comes next

The rest of Europe is watching Italy for a simple reason: if the law works — if it survives the courts, if schools can enforce it, if it produces the integration the government promises — other governments will copy it. The politics of immigration have shifted across the continent in the last few years, and the appetite for measures that would once have been considered beyond the pale has grown. A classroom quota on foreign children, proposed five years ago, would have been dismissed as extremist. Today it is law in the European Union's third-largest country.

The first test will come in the courts. Italy's constitutional court, and ultimately the European Court of Human Rights, will be asked to decide whether a state may ban a garment in schools and cap the number of foreign children in a classroom. The government's lawyers will argue that the law is about identification and language, not religion and nationality — and that states have wide latitude to run their schools as they see fit. The challengers will argue that the law's effect, whatever its wording, falls on one religious group and one category of child, and that its effect is what matters. Both sides can cite precedent. Neither can be sure of the outcome.

The second test will come in the schools. The 2027–28 school year is less than a year away, and schools must work out how to apply a cap they have never applied before, to children whose circumstances they may not fully know, with no new resources. Head teachers' associations have already warned that the logistics will be difficult. If the implementation is chaotic — if children are moved between schools, if classes are split, if parents are fined in confusing circumstances — the law's supporters will find it hard to defend, whatever the courts say.

The third test will be political, and it will come at the next election. Meloni's government has staked a great deal on this law. It is the flagship domestic measure of a government elected on promises to control immigration and defend Italian identity. If the law is seen to work — if classrooms improve, if integration measurably advances — it will be a triumph. If it is seen to fail — if it produces court defeats, school chaos and a generation of alienated children — it will be remembered as the moment a government chose symbolism over sense.

For now, the law is the law. The veil ban is in force. The cap is coming. And in classrooms across Italy, on Monday morning, teachers will begin the difficult work of turning a parliamentary vote into a school day. The girls who wear the niqab will have to decide what to do. The boys who have just arrived and speak no Italian will have to find a classroom that has room for them. And the country will find out, one school at a time, whether a law can make children Italian — or whether it can only make them feel that they are not.
