TITLE: The FCC Just Cleared SpaceX to Launch 1 Million Satellites — Data Centres in Space Are Now Real LABELS: Science SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The FCC has authorised SpaceX to deploy up to 1 million Starmind satellites as orbital AI data centres, the first such approval in US history ---BODY---

Elon Musk in March 2024. His company SpaceX has won FCC approval to deploy up to 1 million satellites as data centres in space, the first authorisation of its kind in American history. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0).

The United States has just authorised something that sounds like science fiction: one million satellites, orbiting the Earth, working as data centres in space. On Wednesday, November 25, the Federal Communications Commission approved SpaceX's request to deploy its Starmind constellation — up to 1,000,000 solar-powered orbital compute modules designed to process massive amounts of data in orbit for use back here on Earth — in what the agency described as the first authorisation in US history for a constellation of data centres in space. The FCC's chair, Brendan Carr, announced the decision in a post on X, writing that "The Starmind & competitor constellations will use solar power to extend America's lead in Super Intelligence." The agency's own press release put it more formally: "At this pivotal time in America's development of super intelligence [artificial intelligence], the FCC is granting SpaceX authorisation to deploy 1,000,000 orbital compute modules to process massive amounts of data to be used back here on Earth." The number is so large that it is difficult to grasp. There are only about 17,000 satellites in orbit today, in total, from every country and every company on Earth. SpaceX is now authorised to launch nearly sixty times that many.

Starmind is not another Starlink. Starlink is a communications network — satellites that beam internet down to Earth — and SpaceX sought approval for 42,000 of those before it began deployment, and now has just over 11,000 of them in space. Starmind is something else entirely: a network of computing machines in orbit, designed to do the work that data centres do on the ground, but in space, where the sun never sets on a solar panel and where the cold of orbit does the cooling for free. The satellites will communicate with one another using optical links — lasers — with radio frequencies serving only as a backup for communications and emergency operations. The FCC's authorisation covers satellites in orbital shells between 500 and 1,002 kilometres in altitude, at 30-degree and sun-synchronous inclinations, and its radio spectrum licence is limited to backup and emergency telemetry, tracking and command. The idea, as SpaceX has presented it, is to shift power-hungry AI infrastructure into orbit in order to bypass the terrestrial electricity, land and cooling bottlenecks that are now constraining the growth of artificial intelligence on Earth. The data centres that train today's AI models consume electricity on the scale of small cities, and they need land, water for cooling, and grid connections that take years to build. In orbit, the power is free, the cooling is free, and the land is infinite.

The FCC's decision did not come out of nowhere. SpaceX filed its application with the FCC on January 30, 2026, for a constellation of one million satellites operating as orbital data centres, and the FCC's Space Bureau granted it in part on November 25, 2026, denying petitions to deny filed by Kuiper Systems, Viasat and the American Astronomical Society. The order itself states the commission's reasoning plainly: "We find that grant of this application will serve the public interest by permitting SpaceX to pioneer emergent technology that advances American leadership in space and artificial intelligence." The FCC said it was also approving SpaceX's requests to back up its optical network by approving additional communications, and that it is continuing to review additional orbital data centre proposals and intends to issue decisions on each expeditiously according to the agency's regular procedures. The approval is conditional, and it comes with a clock. Under the deployment milestones in the authorisation, SpaceX must launch its first satellite by November 25, 2033, 10 per cent of the constellation by 2035, half by 2038, and the full constellation by 2040 — or the authorisation becomes void. That is a long runway, and it is also a deadline: fourteen years to put a million machines in orbit, or lose the right to do so.

Why space — and why now

The reason Starmind exists is the reason the AI industry is running out of room on Earth. The demand for AI computing is growing faster than the power grid can support it. Training a frontier AI model now requires data centres that draw hundreds of megawatts — the consumption of a small city — and the largest facilities under construction are measured in gigawatts. In the United States, data centre developers are waiting years for grid connections, competing with homes and factories for electricity, and running into local opposition over land and water use. In some places, the constraint is not money but physics: there is only so much power a grid can deliver, only so much land near a fibre connection, only so much water for cooling. Space removes all three constraints at once. A satellite in sun-synchronous orbit is in sunlight almost continuously, so its solar panels generate power around the clock. The vacuum of space is cold, so the heat a computer generates can be radiated away without water or air conditioning. And there is no land to buy, no planning permission to seek, no neighbour to object. The FCC's own announcement reached for the grandest possible framing for this logic, referencing a Kardashev Type II civilisation — a hypothetical stage of technological advancement capable of harnessing all the energy emitted by the sun, roughly ten billion times more power than what humanity currently uses. The reference was in a graphic posted to X by the FCC and Chairman Carr, and it was not subtle: the commission sees Starmind as a step towards capturing the sun's energy at a scale that no terrestrial power station can match.

Whether that vision is realistic is another question, and it is one that even the FCC's announcement did not answer. It was unclear whether SpaceX would put one million satellites in space, where only 17,000 satellites exist currently, and the FCC did not specify a deployment timeline or an estimated cost for the Starmind constellation. The economics of the project depend heavily on Starship, SpaceX's next-generation reusable rocket, which is still under development and which the company is counting on to bring the cost of launching a satellite down far enough to make a million-satellite constellation affordable. Musk has said the company could begin demonstrating its AI satellite technology by late 2027, with broader deployment potentially beginning as early as 2028, and during an investment conference earlier this month he estimated that the first Starmind computers could launch in the third quarter of 2027. Those are ambitious dates for a system that has never been built, and the history of mega-constellations suggests that the gap between authorisation and reality can be wide. SpaceX sought approval for 42,000 Starlink satellites and has deployed just over 11,000. A million satellites would be a deployment on a scale that no one has ever attempted, and the milestones in the FCC's authorisation — first satellite by 2033, 10 per cent by 2035 — suggest that even the commission expects the build-out to take the better part of two decades.

But the direction of travel is clear, and it is not only SpaceX that is moving in it. The FCC said it is reviewing additional orbital data centre proposals, which means other companies have filed their own plans to put computing in orbit, and the commission intends to decide them quickly. The race to build data centres in space is now a real, regulated competition, with the United States as its first mover. The prize is not only the computing power itself, but the position it confers: the country that builds the orbital infrastructure for superintelligence will set the terms on which that intelligence is built, and the FCC's order frames the decision explicitly in those terms — as a matter of American leadership in space and artificial intelligence. The reference to "super intelligence" in the commission's press release, and Carr's post about extending "America's lead in Super Intelligence", makes the strategic logic plain. This is not only a commercial decision. It is an industrial policy decision, made by a regulator, about who will own the infrastructure of the next era of computing.

The objections — and why the FCC overruled them

Not everyone wanted this approved. Three parties filed petitions to deny SpaceX's application: Kuiper Systems, Amazon's satellite venture and a direct competitor to Starlink; Viasat, an established satellite operator; and the American Astronomical Society, the professional body for astronomers in the United States. The FCC denied all three petitions. The competitors' objections were, in essence, commercial: a million-satellite constellation would crowd the orbital environment, complicate coordination, and raise the risk of collisions and debris for everyone else operating in the same shells. The astronomers' objections were scientific: a million bright satellites would flood the night sky with reflected sunlight, interfering with ground-based telescopes and with the observation of the universe itself. Both objections have a history. Astronomers have been warning about the impact of mega-constellations since Starlink began deploying, and the American Astronomical Society's petition was the formal expression of a concern that has been building for years: that the night sky, which belongs to everyone, is being filled with machines that belong to a small number of companies, and that the science that depends on dark skies is being sacrificed to it.

The FCC's decision to deny the petitions does not mean those concerns were found to be baseless. It means the commission weighed them against the public interest in allowing SpaceX to pioneer the technology, and found that the balance favoured granting the application. The order's language — that the grant "will serve the public interest by permitting SpaceX to pioneer emergent technology that advances American leadership in space and artificial intelligence" — is the language of a regulator that has decided the strategic value of the project outweighs the costs, and that the costs can be managed through the conditions of the authorisation rather than by refusing it. The conditions are real: the orbital shells are defined, the inclinations are specified, the radio licence is limited to backup and emergency use, and the deployment milestones give the commission a mechanism to revoke the authorisation if SpaceX does not build what it promised. But the conditions do not answer the astronomers' concern, which is not about whether SpaceX meets its milestones but about what a million satellites will do to the sky while it is meeting them. That argument is not over. It has simply moved from the FCC's docket to the wider public debate, where it will be fought again as the first Starmind satellites are built, launched and seen.

There is also the question of debris. A million satellites is a million objects that can collide, break apart and generate fragments, and the orbital shells SpaceX has been authorised to use — 500 to 1,002 kilometres — are among the most crowded in low Earth orbit. The history of spaceflight includes collisions that have already created clouds of debris, and the addition of a million new objects, even with deorbit plans and collision avoidance, changes the arithmetic of risk for every operator in those shells. SpaceX's answer, in its filings and in its public statements, has been that its satellites are designed to deorbit at the end of their lives and that its autonomous collision avoidance systems have already performed hundreds of thousands of manoeuvres for Starlink. Whether that answer scales from 11,000 satellites to a million is a question that no one can answer today, because no one has ever operated a constellation of that size. The FCC has, in effect, authorised an experiment at a scale that has no precedent, and the experiment will be conducted in an environment — low Earth orbit — that is shared by every spacefaring nation on Earth.

What a million computers in orbit would actually do

The technical case for Starmind rests on a simple observation: the most expensive part of running an AI data centre is not the computers, but the power and the cooling, and both are free in space. A data centre on Earth needs electricity delivered through a grid, and it needs to get rid of the heat its chips produce, which it does with water and air conditioning. A satellite in orbit generates its own electricity from sunlight, and it gets rid of heat by radiating it into the vacuum, which is cold enough to do the job without any machinery at all. The Starmind satellites are designed as solar-powered orbital compute modules, and they will communicate with one another using optical links — lasers that carry data between satellites at the speed of light, without the need for ground stations or fibre cables. Radio frequencies are reserved for backup and for emergency telemetry, tracking and command. The result, if it works as designed, is a data centre that is not a building but a constellation — a distributed computer the size of a planet's orbit, powered by the sun, cooled by the void, and connected by light.

The applications are, for now, a matter of speculation, because the system does not exist yet. The FCC's announcement says the constellation will process massive amounts of data for use back on Earth, and the obvious uses are the ones that already strain terrestrial data centres: training large AI models, running inference for millions of users, processing satellite imagery, and handling the data generated by the growing number of sensors, cameras and devices on the ground. There is also a strategic application that the FCC's framing makes plain: a computing infrastructure in orbit is harder to attack, harder to cut off, and harder to regulate than a building on the ground, and for a country that is competing with China for leadership in artificial intelligence, that resilience has value of its own. The reference to a Kardashev Type II civilisation in the FCC's announcement was not a technical claim. It was a statement of ambition — a signal that the commission sees the capture of solar energy in orbit as the long-term answer to the energy demands of superintelligence, and that it is willing to authorise the first step towards it now.

Whether the first step leads anywhere depends on Starship. SpaceX's request bets heavily on reduced costs from Starship, the company's next-generation reusable rocket, to help deploy the satellites, and the arithmetic is unforgiving: a million satellites cannot be launched on the rockets that exist today at the prices that exist today. Starship is designed to carry far more mass to orbit, far more cheaply, than any rocket before it, and it is the enabling technology for Starmind in the same way that the Falcon 9 was the enabling technology for Starlink. If Starship works as intended, the cost of putting a satellite in orbit falls far enough to make a million-satellite constellation conceivable. If it does not, Starmind remains an authorisation on paper, with milestones that will expire in 2033, 2035, 2038 and 2040. Musk's own timeline — first Starmind computers in the third quarter of 2027, a demonstration by late 2027, broader deployment as early as 2028 — suggests that SpaceX intends to move quickly, and that the company believes Starship will be ready. The FCC has given it fourteen years to prove it.

What happens next

The immediate next step is the first hardware. SpaceX has said it could begin demonstrating its AI satellite technology by late 2027, and Musk has estimated that the first Starmind computers could launch in the third quarter of that year. Those dates are close — less than a year away — and they will be the first real test of whether the constellation is a plan or a product. The demonstration satellites will need to prove that a computer can operate in orbit, that it can be powered by sunlight, that it can shed its heat, that it can communicate with other satellites by laser, and that it can do useful work — and they will need to prove it in a radiation environment that is hostile to electronics, in a thermal environment that swings between extremes, and in an orbital environment that is already crowded. If the demonstration works, the next step is deployment, and the next step after that is scale: from a handful of satellites to hundreds, to thousands, to the tens of thousands that would mark the first per cent of the authorised constellation.

The regulatory next step is the FCC's review of the other orbital data centre proposals it has received. The commission said it is continuing to review additional proposals and intends to issue decisions on each expeditiously, which means Starmind will not be alone for long. Competitors will file, and the commission will have to decide whether the orbital environment can support more than one million-satellite constellation, and if so, how many, and under what conditions. The astronomers, whose petition was denied, will be watching the night sky, and the first Starmind satellites will be visible — bright points of light, moving in formation, in shells that pass over every observatory on Earth. The debate about whether the sky belongs to science or to industry will be fought again, with real satellites in the sky this time, and the FCC's decision will be judged not by its reasoning but by its consequences.

And the strategic next step is the one the FCC named: the race for superintelligence. The commission's press release and Carr's post both frame Starmind as a matter of American leadership, and the reference to a Kardashev Type II civilisation — a civilisation that harnesses all the energy of its star — is a measure of how seriously the commission takes the stakes. The demand for AI computing is growing faster than the Earth's power grid can support it, and the country that solves that problem first will have an advantage that is difficult to overstate. SpaceX has now been given the legal right to try to solve it in orbit, with a million satellites, powered by the sun. Whether it can build them, launch them and make them work is a question for the next fourteen years. The authorisation is real. The satellites are not — yet. But for the first time in history, a government has said yes to data centres in space, and the sky will never look quite the same again.
