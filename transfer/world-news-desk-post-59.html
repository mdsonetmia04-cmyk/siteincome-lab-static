TITLE: KOSPI Just Crashed 6.16% in a Single Day — Samsung Lost 9.87% and SK Hynix 11.05% as the AI Rally Unravelled LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: South Korea's KOSPI plunged 6.16% to 4,697.51 as Samsung fell 9.87% and SK hynix 11.05%, wiping out the Nvidia-fuelled rally in one session ---BODY---

The Korea Exchange building in Busan, South Korea. The country's benchmark KOSPI index lost 6.16% in a single session on Wednesday, wiping out the entire rally sparked by Nvidia's Jensen Huang returning as CEO. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).

South Korea's stock market lost 6.16% in a single day on Wednesday, in a sell-off so violent that it erased the entire rally of the previous session, dragged the country's two most valuable companies down by double digits, and sent a shiver through every market in Asia. The benchmark KOSPI index closed at 4,697.51, down 308.52 points, after falling as low as 4,672.55 during the session, and the damage was concentrated exactly where it hurts most: Samsung Electronics, the market bellwether, shed 9.87% to close at 170,700 won, and its chipmaking rival SK hynix went down 11.05% to 1.03 million won. The two companies together account for nearly half of the index's market capitalisation, which means that when they fall, the whole market falls — and on Wednesday, they fell together, in the same session, by amounts that would be remarkable for a month, let alone a day. The KOSPI, which had reclaimed the 5,000 level on news that Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang was returning, fell back below 4,700 in a single session, and closed at its lowest level since January 13.

The sell-off was not a mystery. It was a reaction, in Seoul, to a sell-off that had already happened overnight in New York, where the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index tumbled 7.88%, the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.48% and the S&P 500 fell 0.50%, and where Nvidia — which had surged 31.5% the previous day on Huang's return — fell 5.86%, and Micron fell 11%. The overnight losses revived concerns over the sustainability of the artificial intelligence trade, the rally in chip stocks that has driven markets around the world for the past two years, and the fear spread from Wall Street to Seoul before the Korean market even opened. The KOSPI opened at 4,809, already down 3.94% from the previous close, according to the Korea Exchange, and it kept falling. A sell-sidecar was triggered at 9:08 a.m., shortly after the market opened, temporarily suspending program sell orders for five minutes amid heightened volatility — a circuit breaker designed to slow a rout, which did not stop it. Foreign investors, who had led the previous day's rebound, sold a net 1.95 trillion won on the main board, including net sales of 1.01 trillion won in Samsung Electronics and 559.6 billion won in SK hynix. They were not the only sellers, but they were the ones who mattered, and their selling drove the index down through the day to its close at 4,697.51.

The immediate trigger, beyond the overnight losses on Wall Street, was a series of price target cuts by leading global investment banks, which cited risks to the chip market's longer-term profitability. In a Sunday report, Goldman Sachs lowered its target for SK hynix from 3.5 million won to 2 million won, and for Samsung Electronics from 500,000 won to 350,000 won — cuts of more than 40% and 30% respectively, which sent a message to the market that the world's most influential investment bank no longer believed the chipmakers were worth what investors had been paying for them. The movement came amid a series of price target cuts by leading global investment banks, which cited risks to the chip market's longer-term profitability, and the cuts landed on a market that was already nervous about the sustainability of the AI boom. The two chipmakers had surged on Tuesday on the news of Huang's return to Nvidia, and the rally had been built on the assumption that the demand for the memory chips that power AI data centres would keep growing, and that the prices of those chips would keep rising. The price target cuts challenged that assumption directly, and the market's answer was to sell — first the chipmakers, and then everything else.

The damage was not limited to Samsung and SK hynix. SK Square, the parent of SK hynix, backtracked 11.19% to 635,000 won, while power equipment manufacturer Samsung Electro-Mechanics shed 12% to 755,000 won, and Samsung Electronics preferred shares dropped 10.19%. By industry, semiconductors and semiconductor equipment fell 10.31%, and electronic equipment fell 10.10% — the two sectors that define the Korean market, and the two sectors most exposed to the AI trade. The tech-heavy Kosdaq opened 1.75% lower at 654.21 before closing at 650.64, down 2.29%, and the selling was broad enough that even the stocks that rose — software, up 9.56%, cosmetics, up 7.12%, and pharmaceuticals, up 4.72% — could not offset the losses in the sectors that matter. Samsung Electronics' common stock market capitalisation shrank to 997.96 trillion won, slipping below 1,000 trillion won for the first time in the current rally, and SK hynix tumbled to 1.03 million won, or about $767, a level that would have seemed unthinkable a week earlier. The market had given back, in a single session, everything it had gained the day before — and then some.

The Nvidia connection

To understand what happened in Seoul on Wednesday, you have to understand what happened in California on Tuesday, and what happened in the days before that. Nvidia is the most important company in the artificial intelligence trade, and its chief executive, Jensen Huang, is the most important person in it. Huang stepped down as Nvidia's chief executive on November 12, 2026, and the company's shares slid more than 25% in pre-market trading on the news. The market's reaction was a measure of how much of Nvidia's value — and, by extension, how much of the AI trade — rests on a single person. Then, on Monday, Nvidia brought Huang back as chief executive, and the market's reaction was equally dramatic: Nvidia's shares surged 31.5% in a single day, and the rally spread around the world, lifting chip stocks in Seoul, in Tokyo and in Taipei, and pushing the KOSPI back above the 5,000 level. The reinstatement of Huang was, for a day, the best news the market had heard in weeks — a sign that the AI trade was back, that the demand for chips was real, and that the future was bright.

Wednesday was the day the market changed its mind. Overnight on Wall Street, Nvidia fell 5.86%, giving back a significant part of its one-day gain, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 7.88%, its worst day in months. Micron, the American memory-chip maker and the closest analogue to Samsung and SK hynix, fell 11%. The message from Wall Street was that the Huang rally had been a one-day phenomenon, driven by relief rather than by fundamentals, and that the underlying questions about the AI trade — about how much money the data-centre build-out will actually make, about whether chip prices can stay as high as they are, about whether the demand for AI hardware will keep growing at the pace investors expect — had not been answered by Huang's return. The Korean market, which had risen on the same news, fell on the same logic, and it fell harder, because Samsung and SK hynix are more exposed to the memory-chip market than Nvidia is, and because the Korean market is more concentrated: when two companies account for nearly half of the index, a sell-off in those two companies is a sell-off in the whole market.

The Goldman Sachs price target cuts gave the market a reason to sell, and the Wall Street losses gave it the excuse. Goldman lowered its target for SK hynix from 3.5 million won to 2 million won, and for Samsung Electronics from 500,000 won to 350,000 won, in a report published on Sunday, and the cuts were based on the bank's view of the risks to the chip market's longer-term profitability. The logic is straightforward: the memory-chip market is cyclical, and the prices of memory chips — the DRAM and the high-bandwidth memory that power AI data centres — have risen sharply in the past two years, driven by demand from the companies building AI infrastructure. But high prices attract new supply, and new supply eventually brings prices down, and the investment banks are now asking whether the current prices can be sustained, and whether the profits that Samsung and SK hynix are making today will still be there in a year or two. The market's answer, on Wednesday, was that it is not sure — and when a market is not sure, it sells first and asks questions later.

There was also a second factor weighing on the market, and it was a political one. Investor sentiment was dampened by speculation that the Trump administration is preparing a new wave of tariffs targeting trading partners over industrial subsidies and overcapacity, with new import duties on a wide range of goods expected, and with 14 major trading partners in the frame. "Foreign investors turned net sellers, while Trump's remarks and uncertainty over tariffs also weighed on the market," said Lee Kyung-min, an analyst at Daishin Securities. The tariff threat is not new — the Trump administration has been preparing the new duties for weeks, and the probe into excess industrial capacity in 16 major trading partners has been public knowledge — but the timing of the reports, arriving on the same day as the Wall Street sell-off and the Goldman cuts, added to the sense that the market was facing risks from every direction at once. South Korea is one of the countries most exposed to American tariffs, because it exports so much to the United States, and because its largest companies — Samsung, Hyundai, SK — depend on access to the American market. A new wave of tariffs would hit them directly, and the market priced that risk in on Wednesday, along with everything else.

A rout across Asia

South Korea was not the only Asian market to fall on Wednesday. In Tokyo, Japan's Nikkei fell 2.78%, with SoftBank down 14.8%, and in Taiwan, the Taiex fell around 5%. The pattern was the same in every market: the technology stocks that had risen on the AI trade fell, the chipmakers fell hardest, and the selling was driven by the same combination of Wall Street losses, price target cuts and tariff fears. The Asian sell-off was, in effect, a single event, transmitted from New York to Seoul to Tokyo to Taipei in the space of a few hours, and it showed how connected the world's markets have become — and how dependent they all are on the same story. The AI trade is a global trade, and when it falters in one market, it falters in all of them. The KOSPI's 6.16% fall was the worst in Asia, but it was not an outlier; it was the extreme end of a pattern that ran across the whole region.

The scale of the foreign selling in Seoul is worth pausing on. Foreign investors sold a net 1.95 trillion won on the main board, or nearly 2 trillion won, according to different reports, and they had been the buyers the day before, leading the rebound that followed Huang's return. The speed of the reversal — from net buyers on Tuesday to heavy net sellers on Wednesday — is a measure of how fast sentiment can change in a market dominated by foreign capital, and of how little loyalty foreign investors have to any single market. They buy when the story is good, and they sell when it turns, and on Wednesday the story turned. The 1.01 trillion won of net sales in Samsung Electronics and the 559.6 billion won in SK hynix were the largest single-day foreign sales in those stocks in recent memory, and they were enough, on their own, to drive the index down sharply. The domestic investors who bought what the foreigners sold will be hoping that the foreigners were wrong — but the history of the Korean market suggests that when foreign investors sell in size, they are usually selling for a reason.

The sell-sidecar, triggered at 9:08 a.m., is a small detail that tells a larger story. A sidecar is a circuit breaker: when the market falls too fast, the exchange temporarily suspends program trading — the automated buying and selling by computers — for five minutes, to give human traders a chance to catch up. The fact that it was triggered less than ten minutes after the market opened, at 9:08 a.m., shows how fast the selling was, and how little time the market had to absorb it. The sidecar did not stop the fall; it only paused it, for five minutes, and when trading resumed, the selling continued. The market opened down 3.94%, fell to a low of 4,672.55, and closed at 4,697.51 — down 6.16% on the day, and down from the 5,000 level it had reclaimed only the day before. The KOSPI's lowest close since January 13 is a reminder of how much ground has been lost, and how quickly it was lost: in a single session, the index gave back weeks of gains, and returned to a level it had not seen in ten months.

Why two companies can move a whole country

The most striking thing about Wednesday's fall is not the size of it, but the concentration of it. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix together account for nearly half of the KOSPI's market capitalisation, according to the Korea Times, which means that the fate of the entire Korean stock market rests, to an extraordinary degree, on the fate of two companies that make the same thing: memory chips. When those two companies fall by 10% in a day, the index cannot help but follow, and the diversification that investors in other markets take for granted — the idea that a fall in one sector will be offset by a rise in another — does not work in Korea, because there is no other sector large enough to offset the chipmakers. On Wednesday, software rose 9.56%, cosmetics rose 7.12% and pharmaceuticals rose 4.72%, and it made no difference at all, because those sectors are small, and the chipmakers are enormous. The market is, in effect, a bet on memory chips, and on Wednesday that bet lost.

That concentration is a product of Korea's economic history. Samsung is not just a company; it is the largest chaebol, the family-controlled conglomerate that has dominated the Korean economy for decades, and its electronics division is the most valuable part of it. SK hynix is the memory-chip arm of the SK group, another of the great chaebols, and together the two companies dominate the global market for the DRAM and high-bandwidth memory that power everything from smartphones to AI data centres. Their dominance is a source of national pride, and it is also a source of national vulnerability, because when the memory-chip cycle turns — as it has, repeatedly, over the past thirty years — the whole country feels it. The Korean stock market is the world's most concentrated major market, and the 6.16% fall on Wednesday is what that concentration looks like when it goes wrong: a single day, two companies, and an entire index down by more than a twentieth of its value.

The tariff threat adds a second layer to the story, and it is a layer that has nothing to do with chips. The Trump administration is poised to release the findings of a probe aimed at countering alleged excess industrial capacity in 16 major US trading partners, according to three people with knowledge of the matter, with new import duties on a wide range of goods expected. The probe is about industrial subsidies and overcapacity — the argument, made by the administration, that other countries have built too much factory capacity with government support, and that the resulting flood of cheap goods is unfair to American manufacturers. South Korea is one of the countries that could be affected, because it is a major exporter of the very goods the probe is examining, and because its companies — Samsung, Hyundai, SK — sell billions of dollars of products into the American market every year. The new tariffs would come on top of the duties already in place, and they would arrive at a moment when the Korean economy is already dealing with a falling stock market and a nervous foreign investor base. The market's reaction on Wednesday — selling first, and asking questions later — is the rational response to a risk that is real, but whose size is still unknown.

What the fall means — and what comes next

The question now is whether Wednesday was a correction or the beginning of something worse. A correction is a fall of 10% or more from a recent peak, and the KOSPI has now fallen sharply from the 5,000 level it reached on Tuesday — but a single day does not make a trend, and the market has recovered from worse days before. The case for a recovery is that the demand for AI hardware is real, that the data-centre build-out is continuing, and that Samsung and SK hynix are the companies that make the memory chips the AI industry needs, which means that their long-term prospects are still strong. The case against a recovery is that the prices of memory chips may have risen too far, too fast, that the investment banks are now questioning the sustainability of those prices, and that the tariff threat from Washington adds a risk that has nothing to do with the fundamentals of the chip market. The market will decide, in the coming days, which of those cases is right — and the decision will be visible in the prices of Samsung and SK hynix, which are now the market's verdict on the AI trade itself.

The first thing to watch is Wall Street, and whether the sell-off in American chip stocks continues. If Nvidia stabilises, and if the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index recovers, the Korean market is likely to follow — because the Korean market follows Wall Street, and because the AI trade is a single global story. If the American sell-off deepens, the Korean market will fall further, and the 4,697.51 close may not be the bottom. The second thing to watch is the foreign investors, and whether they keep selling. The 1.95 trillion won of net sales on Wednesday was a large number, and if it is repeated on Thursday, the market will struggle to absorb it. The third thing to watch is the tariff announcement from Washington, and whether the new duties — and the list of countries they apply to — include South Korea, and how severe they are. The fourth thing to watch is the price of memory chips, and whether the investment banks' warnings about longer-term profitability prove correct, or whether the demand for AI hardware keeps prices high.

The fifth thing to watch is the KOSPI itself, and the 4,700 level. The index closed at 4,697.51, just below 4,700, and the psychological importance of round numbers in markets should not be underestimated: a close below 4,700 is a signal, and a recovery above it would be a signal in the other direction. The market's lowest close since January 13 is a fact, but it is also a warning — that the gains of the past ten months can be given back, and that they can be given back quickly. The investors who bought Samsung at 170,700 won on Wednesday, or SK hynix at 1.03 million won, will be hoping that the bottom is near. The investors who sold will be hoping that it is not. Both sides cannot be right, and the market will tell us, in the coming days, which one was.

Wednesday's fall is a reminder of a truth that every investor learns, sooner or later: that markets go up and markets go down, and that the same news can move them in opposite directions on consecutive days. On Tuesday, the news was that Jensen Huang was back at Nvidia, and the market rose. On Wednesday, the news was that Nvidia had fallen, that Goldman Sachs had cut its targets, and that tariffs were coming, and the market fell — by more than it had risen, in a single day, wiping out the rally and then some. The AI trade is not over. The demand for chips is not gone. Samsung and SK hynix are still the companies that make the memory the world needs. But the market has decided, for now, that it is not sure — and when a market is not sure, it sells. The KOSPI lost 6.16% on Wednesday. The question is what it will do on Thursday.
