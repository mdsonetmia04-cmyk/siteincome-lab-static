TITLE: CrowdStrike Just Bought Zscaler for $43.9 Billion — the Biggest Cybersecurity Deal in History LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: CrowdStrike will buy Zscaler in a $43.9 billion all-stock deal, the biggest cybersecurity acquisition ever, creating a $10 billion revenue giant. ---BODY---

George Kurtz, chief executive of CrowdStrike, will lead the combined company. Photo: CrowdStrike, Inc. / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).

CrowdStrike has agreed to buy Zscaler in a $43.9 billion all-stock deal, the companies announced on Monday, November 23 — the biggest cybersecurity acquisition ever recorded, and one of the largest deals of a blockbuster year for mergers and acquisitions. The agreement unites two of the biggest names in digital defence at a moment when high-profile hacks, ransomware gangs and fast-moving artificial intelligence threats have made cybersecurity a boardroom priority for almost every large company on earth. Under the terms, Zscaler shareholders will receive 1.135 shares of CrowdStrike common stock for each Zscaler share they own, valuing Zscaler at roughly $260.41 a share — a 34 percent premium over its closing price on Friday, November 20. When the deal closes, existing CrowdStrike shareholders will own about 84.1 percent of the combined company, while Zscaler investors will hold roughly 15.9 percent. The combined business is expected to generate more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue, the companies said, and CrowdStrike says the deal will expand its total addressable market by more than $185 billion. It is a bet, in short, that the future of cybersecurity belongs to one giant platform rather than dozens of separate tools — and it is a bet worth nearly $44 billion.

The announcement landed on a Monday, and the market's first reaction was not applause. CrowdStrike's own shares fell by almost 10 percent on the day the deal was announced, wiping out about $22 billion in market value — roughly half the price CrowdStrike had agreed to pay. That is the paradox at the heart of this deal: the biggest cybersecurity acquisition in history was greeted, at least at first, by investors selling the buyer. The reason is not hard to understand. CrowdStrike is paying entirely in its own stock, so every dollar of the price comes directly out of its existing shareholders' pockets, and the deal will not close until the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028 — which runs from August 2027 to January 2028 — leaving more than a year for regulators, rivals and the market to pick it apart. But the companies, and their chief executives, insist the logic is overwhelming, and the prize is a cybersecurity giant built for the era of AI agents, when the threats move faster than any human team can follow. Here is what the deal is, why it happened, who wins and who worries, and what comes next.

The deal, in numbers

The headline figure is $43.9 billion, and it is worth pausing on what that means. According to the Wall Street Journal, the all-stock transaction marks one of the bigger deals in what has been a blockbuster year for mergers and acquisitions, and analysts at Wells Fargo called it the largest security deal on record, beating the acquisition of the artificial intelligence cloud security platform Wiz by Alphabet in March. The structure is simple: no cash changes hands. Zscaler shareholders receive 1.135 shares of CrowdStrike Class A common stock for each Zscaler share they own. Based on CrowdStrike's closing share price on Friday, November 20 — $229.44 — that exchange ratio implies a value of roughly $260.41 per Zscaler share, a premium of about 34 percent over Zscaler's closing price that day.

But that $260.41 is not a fixed promise. Because the payment is entirely in CrowdStrike stock, its real value rises and falls with CrowdStrike's share price, and on Monday it was already falling. As the Motley Fool reported, CrowdStrike's slide on Monday cut the value of the offer to about $235 per Zscaler share, and Zscaler itself traded about 3.5 percent below the announced deal value. That gap is the market's way of saying the deal is far from certain, and that the price may move a long way before it closes. The companies expect the transaction to close in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028, subject to approval from Zscaler shareholders and the relevant regulatory authorities. Either side can walk away if the deal has not closed by November 23, 2027, a date that moves to February 23, 2028, if only antitrust or foreign-investment reviews remain. And if Zscaler walks away for a better offer, it would owe CrowdStrike a termination fee of $1.4 billion, according to filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission.

The ownership split tells its own story. When the deal closes, existing CrowdStrike shareholders will own about 84.1 percent of the combined company, while Zscaler investors will hold roughly 15.9 percent. CrowdStrike is, in other words, swallowing a company worth nearly a fifth of the combined business — and doing it with paper, not cash. CrowdStrike says the combination will expand its total addressable market by more than $185 billion, and that the merged entity will have more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue at the time of closing. It also reckons it can boost its annualised revenue by around $1 billion within 30 months of closing by selling more products to the two companies' existing customers, and it is targeting $500 million in annual cost savings within 18 to 24 months of completing the acquisition, identifying datacentre infrastructure, supplier spending and wider operations as areas where it can trim the fat.

There is one more number that matters, and it is the one that explains why Zscaler's board said yes. Zscaler's founder and chief executive, Jay Chaudhry, and his affiliates hold about 34 percent of Zscaler's shares, and they have agreed to vote in favour of the deal. With more than a third of the votes already committed, and a 34 percent premium on the table, the shareholder vote looks like a formality. The real obstacle is not shareholders. It is regulators.

Who is Zscaler, and what does it bring?

Zscaler was founded in 2007 by Jay Chaudhry, and it operates a unified cybersecurity platform based on a zero-trust architecture — a framework that follows the principle of "never trust, always verify." In plain language, that means no user and no device is trusted simply because it is inside the corporate network. Every time an employee tries to access an internal system or resource, they must authenticate again. It is a model built for a world in which people work from home, from coffee shops, from airports and from phones, and in which the old idea of a secure corporate perimeter — a castle wall around the office — no longer makes sense.

The company, based in San Jose, California, provides cloud cybersecurity products to about 8,000 enterprise companies, according to TechCrunch, and it reported $3.9 billion in annual recurring revenue in the first quarter of its fiscal year 2027. Its technology secures corporate networks and cloud connections through that zero-trust architecture, and it has become one of the standard tools that large companies use to control who can reach their systems and data. Zscaler's founder, Jay Chaudhry, will continue to lead the Zscaler business after the deal closes, and he will join CrowdStrike's board of directors as a Class I director. That matters, because it signals that this is not a deal in which the acquired company's leadership is shown the door. Chaudhry built Zscaler from nothing into a $44 billion company, and CrowdStrike wants him — and his team — to stay.

CrowdStrike, based in Austin, Texas, specialises in protecting endpoints — the laptops, servers and phones connected to a company's network — and cloud workloads. Its Falcon platform is the product that made it famous: software that watches every device on a network for signs of attack and stops threats before they spread. The plan, the companies said, is to combine Falcon's endpoint protection capabilities with Zscaler's zero-trust network security technology, giving customers a single platform for securing devices and the connections between them. Under CrowdStrike ownership, Zscaler's solutions will be folded directly into the Falcon platform and sold through CrowdStrike's commercial Falcon Flex model, which lets customers buy cybersecurity as a flexible subscription rather than a fixed bundle.

George Kurtz, the chief executive of CrowdStrike, will lead the combined company. Kurtz founded CrowdStrike in 2011 and built it into one of the most valuable cybersecurity companies in the world. He is also a racing driver — he won his class at the 24 Hours of Le Mans in 2023 — and he has a reputation for blunt, confident leadership. The deal is, in many ways, his boldest move yet: CrowdStrike's previously largest acquisition cost about $630 million, according to RBC analyst Matthew Hedberg, quoted by MarketWatch. This deal is roughly 70 times bigger. As Hedberg put it, the market will likely need time to digest a deal of this scale, given CrowdStrike's history of using a single integrated cloud platform. Buying a company 70 times larger than anything you have bought before is not a routine transaction. It is a transformation.

Why now? The AI threat, and the year of the mega-deal

The timing of the deal is no accident. Cybersecurity has become one of the hottest sectors in technology, and 2026 has been a blockbuster year for mergers and acquisitions across the industry. In March, Alphabet — the parent company of Google — acquired the AI cloud security platform Wiz, a deal that was itself described at the time as one of the largest in the sector. Now CrowdStrike and Zscaler have topped it. The reason is the same in both cases: artificial intelligence is changing the threat faster than any single company can keep up with, and the companies that defend the world's networks believe they need to be bigger, broader and more integrated to survive.

The threats are real and they are accelerating. High-profile hacks have become a weekly event, ransomware gangs have grown into industrial-scale operations, and AI tools have given attackers the ability to generate convincing phishing emails, deepfake voices and malicious code at a speed and scale that was impossible a few years ago. At the same time, the defences are changing too: AI agents — software programs that can act on their own, browse systems, run code and make decisions — are becoming a normal part of corporate life, and they create entirely new attack surfaces. CrowdStrike and Zscaler said the deal will create the "largest single addressable market" for cybersecurity, which will enable the companies to better position themselves for the AI era. The argument, in short, is that the company that can see everything — every device, every connection, every user, every AI agent — will be the company that can stop the next attack before it happens.

There is also a simpler, commercial logic. CrowdStrike and Zscaler sell to many of the same customers, but they sell different things. CrowdStrike protects the device; Zscaler protects the connection. A customer that buys one is a natural buyer of the other, and CrowdStrike has calculated that it can sell its Falcon products to about 8,000 Zscaler customers that do not use CrowdStrike today. That cross-selling opportunity — $1 billion in additional annual revenue within 30 months of closing, by the company's own estimate — is the financial engine of the deal. It is also the reason some customers are nervous. When two suppliers become one, the customer loses a choice, and the supplier gains pricing power.

The market's verdict: a $22 billion shrug

If the logic of the deal is so compelling, why did CrowdStrike's shares fall by almost 10 percent on the day it was announced? The answer, according to analysts and investors quoted in the coverage, is that the market sees the risks as clearly as the rewards. First, there is the price. A 34 percent premium is generous, and paying it entirely in stock means CrowdStrike's existing shareholders are giving up a large slice of their company — they will own 84.1 percent of the combined business, down from 100 percent of a company that was already one of the most valuable in cybersecurity. Second, there is the execution risk. Integrating two companies of this size, with different technologies, different sales teams and different cultures, is one of the hardest things in business, and CrowdStrike has never attempted anything on this scale. Third, there is the timing: the deal will not close for more than a year, and in that time the market, the technology and the regulatory environment can all change.

The numbers tell the story of that scepticism. CrowdStrike's stock fell on Monday, losing about $22 billion in market value — which, as the Motley Fool pointed out, is half the price CrowdStrike agreed to pay for Zscaler. Zscaler's own shares rose on the news, but they traded about 3.5 percent below the announced deal value, a discount that reflects the market's doubt about whether the deal will close at the announced terms, or close at all. The gap is a measure of uncertainty, and it will narrow or widen in the months ahead as regulators, analysts and investors make up their minds.

It is worth remembering, though, that the market's first reaction is not always its last. Large acquisitions are routinely greeted with a falling share price for the buyer, and many of them turn out well. The question is whether CrowdStrike can do what it promises: integrate Zscaler's technology into Falcon, sell more products to the combined customer base, cut $500 million in costs, and emerge as the dominant cybersecurity platform of the AI era. If it can, the $22 billion lost on Monday will look like a footnote. If it cannot, it will look like a warning.

What it means for customers — and the price question

For the thousands of companies that buy cybersecurity from CrowdStrike and Zscaler, the deal raises an immediate and practical question: will it cost more? The Register, reporting on the announcement, put it bluntly: customers are already bracing for pricier renewals. That is a familiar pattern in technology mergers. When two suppliers become one, the combined company gains pricing power, and the customer loses the ability to play one off against the other. CrowdStrike says the deal will create a better, more integrated product, and that the savings from combining the two companies' infrastructure will be passed on in the form of better technology rather than lower prices. Customers, reasonably enough, will believe that when they see it.

There is a counter-argument, and it is worth taking seriously. Cybersecurity is a market in which the buyer is not buying a luxury. They are buying protection against an existential threat. A company that suffers a major breach can lose its customers, its reputation and, in the worst cases, its existence. In that market, what matters most is not the price of the software but whether it works — whether it stops the attack. If the combined CrowdStrike and Zscaler can genuinely see more, detect faster and respond better than either could alone, customers will pay for it, because the alternative is worse. The test will come at renewal time, when customers sit down with the new, combined company and discover what the new price is, and what the new product does. Until then, the worry is understandable, and the promise is unproven.

There is also a question about choice. The cybersecurity market has long been fragmented, with dozens of specialist companies each doing one thing well. That fragmentation frustrates buyers, who must stitch together a dozen different tools, but it also protects them, because no single supplier can dictate terms. The CrowdStrike–Zscaler deal is part of a broader consolidation — Alphabet buying Wiz, CrowdStrike buying Zscaler — that is pushing the industry towards a small number of giant platforms. That may be more convenient, and it may be more effective. But it also concentrates power, and concentrated power in a market as important as cybersecurity deserves scrutiny.

The regulatory road ahead

That scrutiny will come, and it will come from regulators in the United States and abroad. The deal is subject to approval from Zscaler shareholders and the relevant regulatory authorities, and the companies expect it to close in the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal 2028. The antitrust question is the obvious one: does combining the largest endpoint protection company with one of the largest zero-trust network security companies reduce competition in a way that harms customers? The companies will argue that the market is large, fragmented and fiercely competitive, with rivals including Palo Alto Networks, Microsoft, Alphabet and dozens of others, and that the combined company will face intense competition on every front. That argument is plausible. Cybersecurity is one of the most competitive markets in technology, and no single company, however large, can dominate it.

But regulators in 2026 are not the regulators of a decade ago. They have grown more sceptical of large technology mergers, more willing to challenge deals, and more attentive to the argument that size itself can be a problem, even in a competitive market. The deal will be reviewed by antitrust authorities in the United States, and it may also be reviewed in Europe and elsewhere. The companies have built that into their timetable: either side can walk away if the deal has not closed by November 23, 2027, and that date extends to February 23, 2028, if only antitrust or foreign-investment reviews remain. That is a long runway, and it suggests the companies expect a thorough review.

There is also a political dimension. Cybersecurity is not just a commercial market. It is a matter of national security. The companies that protect America's networks, its hospitals, its power grids and its government agencies are, in a real sense, part of the country's defence infrastructure. A deal that creates the largest cybersecurity company in the world will attract attention from policymakers who care about that infrastructure, and from rivals who would prefer it not to exist. The deal's fate will be decided, in part, in hearing rooms and regulatory offices, not just in boardrooms.

CrowdStrike's own numbers: a company in rude health

It is worth noting that CrowdStrike announced the deal alongside its third-quarter results, and those results were strong. According to the Motley Fool, CrowdStrike's revenue rose 28 percent to $1.58 billion in the quarter. Net new annual recurring revenue reached a record $419 million, and total annual recurring revenue grew 27 percent to $6.26 billion. Those are the numbers of a company that is growing fast, winning customers and generating cash — and they are the reason CrowdStrike can contemplate a $43.9 billion acquisition at all. A company that is struggling does not buy its rival for $44 billion. A company that is confident does.

Zscaler, for its part, reported $3.9 billion in annual recurring revenue in the first quarter of its fiscal year 2027, and it serves about 8,000 enterprise customers. Together, the two companies will have more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue at closing, according to their own projections, and a customer base that spans almost every large company in the world. That scale is the point. In a market where the threats are growing faster than any single company, scale is not a luxury. It is a survival strategy.

The contrast with CrowdStrike's history is striking. The company's previously largest deal cost about $630 million. This one costs $43.9 billion — roughly 70 times more. It is a leap, not a step, and it will define George Kurtz's leadership and CrowdStrike's future. If it works, CrowdStrike will be the company that built the cybersecurity platform of the AI era, and Kurtz will be remembered as the chief executive who saw the future and bought it. If it fails, it will be remembered as the deal that was too big, too expensive and too early.

What happens next

In the months ahead, the deal will move through its stages: the shareholder vote, which looks like a formality given Chaudhry's committed 34 percent; the regulatory reviews, which will be thorough and may be contentious; and the long, slow work of integration, which will not even begin in earnest until the deal closes in the second half of fiscal 2028. Along the way, the market will render its verdict in the share price, customers will render theirs at renewal time, and competitors will render theirs by trying to win the customers that the merger unsettles.

For the wider technology industry, the deal is a signal. The age of the specialist is giving way to the age of the platform, and the companies that survive will be the ones that can do the most, for the most customers, in one place. Alphabet bought Wiz in March. CrowdStrike has now bought Zscaler. The next deal, whoever it involves, will be measured against this one — and it will have to be very large indeed to top a $43.9 billion acquisition that the market greeted by wiping $22 billion off the buyer's value in a single day. That is the strange, contradictory reality of the biggest cybersecurity deal in history: it is both a triumph of ambition and a reminder that ambition, in the market, is always priced in real time.

The story is not over. It will not be over until the deal closes, the companies are integrated, and the customers have decided whether the new giant is worth its price. But one thing is already clear: the cybersecurity industry will never be the same. Two of its biggest names are now one, and the company they have created — more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue, 8,000 enterprise customers, and a platform that watches both the device and the connection — will be the company to beat. Whether it can be beaten is the question that will define the next decade of digital defence.
