For more than a decade, the Central Intelligence Agency has told the same story about waterboarding. The agency, it has said, used the torture technique on exactly three men in the years after the attacks of September 11, 2001. Three names, three cases, and nothing more. That account survived congressional investigations, court cases and years of public argument. On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, it cracked.

Newly declassified records released by the United States government show that the CIA appears to have waterboarded at least one more detainee than it has ever admitted — a man a former CIA contract translator saw strapped to a board, his head tilted downward into water, at one of the agency's secret overseas prisons. The disclosure, reported by The New York Times, lends new weight to a suspicion that human rights groups and investigators have held for years: that the agency's use of the technique was wider than its official record shows.

The files were released in response to a lawsuit filed by The New York Times under the Freedom of Information Act, seeking material gathered by a special prosecutor who investigated the CIA's use of torture and its destruction of videotapes showing waterboarding. The prosecutor, John Durham, was appointed in 2008. His investigation ran until 2012 and ended without criminal charges against any agency personnel. The government continues to keep secret his reports on his findings. But the newly released records — including a summary of a 2012 interview with the translator — open a new window into what his investigators found.

The account is specific, and it is chilling in its plainness. In 2012, the translator told an agent of the Federal Bureau of Investigation that he had seen a man he called "Bahir" strapped to a board tilted downward into water. The interview summary does not say where the translator worked. But the newspaper determined that the location was most likely a black site outside Bagram Air Force Base, on the outskirts of Kabul, Afghanistan — the facility sometimes called the Salt Pit, or Detention Site Cobalt. The translator also described the death of a detainee by freezing, an event that matches what happened at that facility.

The CIA's press office did not respond to a request for comment from The New York Times.

The fourth man on the board

The witness was a former contract translator for the agency who worked at one of the CIA's secret prisons, known as black sites. These were the hidden facilities where, under President George W. Bush, the CIA held terrorism suspects outside the reach of American courts and used a variety of torture techniques to interrogate them. The translator's account adds to the publicly available history of that programme — and it directly contradicts the agency's official count.

According to the account, the translator saw the man he called "Bahir" lying on the board with the head of the board tilted downward into the water. The description matches the mechanics of waterboarding precisely: a person is restrained on an inclined board, a cloth is placed over the face, and water is poured on, creating the sensation of drowning. The CIA itself called the practice an "enhanced interrogation technique" — a phrase that became one of the most contested euphemisms in the history of American intelligence.

What makes the account so significant is not only what it describes, but where it fits. The CIA has told Congress and the public that its records showed it waterboarded only three men: Abu Zubaydah, the first detainee the agency held in its black-site prisons; Khalid Sheikh Mohammed, accused of being the architect of the September 11 attacks; and Abd al-Rahim al-Nashiri, who is accused of helping plan Al Qaeda's 2000 bombing of the Navy destroyer Cole. All three are now detainees at the Guantanamo Bay military prison in Cuba.

The newly released files describe a fourth. The translator's account does not name the man beyond "Bahir", and the files do not say whether he survived, was released, or was transferred elsewhere. But the description of the technique, the place and the witness is detailed enough that the newspaper concluded the location was almost certainly Detention Site Cobalt — a facility whose name has become shorthand for the darkest chapter of the CIA's detention programme.

The Salt Pit

Detention Site Cobalt was one of the first black sites the CIA opened after September 11, 2001, and one of the most notorious. It was located north of Kabul, Afghanistan, and it was sometimes called the Salt Pit — a nickname that captured the cold, the damp and the brutality of the place. Detainees there were held in darkness, shackled, and subjected to sleep deprivation, stress positions, beatings and cold. The facility became infamous for its abusive conditions, and for one death in particular.

In November 2002, a detainee named Gul Rahman died at the Salt Pit after being shackled, stripped to his waist, and left overnight in a freezing cell. His death by hypothermia was one of the most documented abuses of the entire programme, and it is the detail that helped identify the translator's location: the translator discussed a detainee death by freezing that matched what happened at the Afghanistan facility. The files do not say where the translator worked, but the death he described points to the Salt Pit as plainly as a street address.

The Salt Pit was eventually closed, and the CIA's black-site programme was shut down by executive order in 2009. But the facility's history has never stopped mattering, because it sits at the centre of the argument over how far the programme really went. The Senate Intelligence Committee's investigation found that the CIA's detention and interrogation programme was far more brutal — and far less effective — than the agency had told policymakers. The new files suggest the committee's account, damning as it was, may still have been incomplete.

What the CIA admitted — and what it didn't

The number three has anchored the CIA's public account of waterboarding for years. The agency told the Senate Intelligence Committee that its records showed it had waterboarded only Abu Zubaydah, Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and Abd al-Rahim al-Nashiri. The figure was repeated so often that it became a fact of the public debate: the technique, however indefensible, was presented as limited to a handful of the most senior Al Qaeda figures, in the most urgent circumstances, in the months after September 11.

The Senate committee's own investigation complicated that picture. Its report, released in summary form in 2014, found that the CIA had held at least 119 detainees in its black sites, and that at least 39 of them had been subjected to so-called enhanced interrogation techniques. The report documented waterboarding in graphic detail: Abu Zubaydah was waterboarded 83 times, according to the committee's count, and Khalid Sheikh Mohammed 183 times. The numbers shocked even readers who thought they knew the programme's history — the technique had not been used once or twice, but again and again, on the same men, over days and weeks.

But the committee's investigators faced a structural problem. Because the Durham investigation had raised the risk of criminal prosecution, CIA witnesses were unwilling to be interviewed by the committee. The investigators instead relied on millions of pages of internal CIA documents — a mountain of paper that was extraordinary in its scope, but still, by definition, only what the agency had chosen to preserve. If a waterboarding was not recorded, or was recorded in a document the committee never saw, it would not appear in the report.

That is what makes the translator's account so important. It comes from a different source entirely: not from the CIA's own records, but from a witness who was there, speaking to an FBI agent in 2012, as part of a criminal investigation. The witness had no reason to protect the agency's official count. He described what he saw — a man on a board, water, a tilted head — and in doing so, he added a name to a list the CIA has insisted contained only three.

The question now is whether "Bahir" was an isolated case or the visible edge of something larger. Human rights groups have long suspected that waterboarding was used more widely than the CIA admitted. The newly released files do not prove how many more times the technique was used, or on how many more men. They prove only that the official number was wrong — and once an official number is shown to be wrong, the question of how wrong it was becomes very difficult to close.

The Durham investigation

The files at the centre of this story come from one of the longest-running and least understood investigations in modern American history. John Durham was appointed in 2008 as a special prosecutor to investigate the CIA's use of torture and, separately, its destruction of videotapes showing waterboarding. The tapes — 92 of them, recorded at a black site in Thailand in 2002 — had been destroyed in 2005 by a senior CIA official, Jose Rodriguez Jr., who later said he acted to protect the identities of the interrogators and the agency's methods.

Durham's investigation ran from 2008 to 2012. It examined whether any CIA personnel had committed crimes in the interrogation programme, and whether the destruction of the tapes had broken the law. In the end, Durham brought no criminal charges against any agency personnel. The government continues to keep secret his reports on his findings — the full account of what he concluded, and why he decided not to prosecute, has never been made public.

What the newly released material shows is that Durham's investigators did gather evidence that has never been seen outside the government. The summary of the translator's 2012 FBI interview is one piece of it. The New York Times obtained the material through a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit — a reminder that the paper's reporting on this story is inseparable from its legal fight to obtain the underlying documents. Charlie Savage, the Times reporter who wrote the article, is himself the plaintiff in the lawsuit that produced the documents.

The fact that it took a lawsuit, and more than a decade after the investigation closed, for this single account to reach the public says something about how the government has handled the history of the programme. The CIA's torture programme was one of the most investigated episodes in American intelligence history — by the Senate, by a special prosecutor, by inspectors general — and yet basic facts about it are still emerging, one declassified file at a time, eighteen years after the investigation that produced them began.

The 2014 Senate report

The most complete public account of the programme remains the Senate Intelligence Committee's report, whose executive summary — more than 500 pages long — was released in December 2014 after years of fighting between the committee and the CIA over what could be made public. The report's findings were unsparing. It concluded that the CIA's enhanced interrogation techniques were not an effective means of obtaining accurate information or gaining detainee cooperation; that the agency's justifications for the techniques rested on inaccurate claims about their effectiveness; that the interrogations were brutal and far worse than the agency had represented to policymakers; and that the agency had impeded effective oversight by Congress and the White House.

The report also found that the CIA had held detainees it had no reason to hold. Of the 119 people the committee found had been detained, at least 26 were wrongfully held — people who did not meet the standard for detention, including some who had been mistaken for someone else. The programme, the committee concluded, had damaged the United States' standing in the world, and its results were not worth the cost.

The CIA disputed the report fiercely, and the dispute has never been resolved. The agency said the committee's investigators had misunderstood the intelligence, cherry-picked documents and ignored evidence that the programme had produced valuable information. The full 6,700-page report has never been released; only the executive summary is public. And the new files suggest that even the summary — the most damning official account ever published — may have understated the programme's reach in at least one respect: the number of people waterboarded.

What the law says now

Waterboarding is no longer used by the United States government — at least, no American administration has admitted to using it since 2009. In January of that year, President Barack Obama signed an executive order barring the CIA from using the technique and ordering the closure of the black sites. Congress later passed restrictions of its own, including the McCain-Feinstein amendment in 2015, which limited all government interrogations to the techniques in the Army Field Manual — a list that does not include waterboarding.

But the legal status of the programme's past has never been settled. No CIA officer has ever been prosecuted for torture in the black-site programme. The Durham investigation closed without charges. The Senate report's findings were never followed by prosecutions. The men who were waterboarded — Abu Zubaydah, Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and Abd al-Rahim al-Nashiri — remain at Guantanamo Bay, where their cases have crawled through military commissions for years, with the evidence obtained through torture complicating every prosecution.

The new files will not change that legal landscape overnight. They do not identify a prosecutable crime, and they do not name a perpetrator who can be charged. But they do something else: they change the historical record. The official story — three men, three cases — is now, on the government's own documents, incomplete. And in a debate where the government has asked the public to trust its account of what happened in secret prisons, a proven error in the count is a serious matter.

What happens next

The immediate question is what else the Durham files contain. The New York Times obtained the material through litigation, and the lawsuit continues — which means more records may follow. The government still holds Durham's reports on his findings in secret, and the full Senate report remains classified. Each new release adds a piece to a picture that is still, after more than two decades, unfinished.

There is also the question of "Bahir" himself. The files do not say who he was, beyond the name the translator used, or what happened to him. He may have been one of the detainees whose names are already known, held at the Salt Pit and later transferred to Guantanamo or to another country. He may have been someone whose detention was never recorded at all. The translator's account is a fragment — a single interview, summarised, in a file released fourteen years after it was given. It raises more questions than it answers, and that is precisely why it matters.

For the CIA, the disclosure is a reminder that the history of the torture programme is not closed. The agency's official count of waterboarding victims has stood for years, repeated in hearings, in reports and in public debate. It has now been contradicted by the government's own records. The agency did not respond to a request for comment on the new files — the same silence it has maintained on much of the programme's history.

For the public, the story is a measure of how much remains unknown about what was done in America's name in the years after September 11, 2001. The programme ended in 2009. Its victims are still in prison, or still missing, or still unidentified. Its perpetrators were never charged. And its full history is still being assembled, one lawsuit, one declassified file and one witness at a time. The translator who spoke to the FBI in 2012 described a man on a board, his head tilted downward into the water. Fourteen years later, the public is only now learning that he was there at all.

Why the count matters

It is tempting to treat the difference between three and four as a footnote — a rounding error in a programme that held at least 119 people and subjected dozens to torture. That would be a mistake. The number three was never just a number. It was the foundation of the CIA's defence of the programme, and of the argument made by its supporters for years afterward: that waterboarding was a rare, tightly controlled measure, reserved for a tiny number of the most dangerous men, in the most desperate circumstances, and used only when nothing else would work.

Every element of that defence depends on the count being right. If the technique was used on a fourth man — a man whose name the public has never heard, at a prison whose existence was denied for years, by a translator whose account sat in a government file for fourteen years — then the claim of tight control is already weakened. And if it was used on a fourth man, the natural question is whether it was used on a fifth, or a tenth, or on men whose waterboarding was never written down at all. The translator's account cannot answer that question. But it proves that the question is a real one, and that the government's answer to it has been wrong before.

There is a second reason the count matters, and it concerns the men at Guantanamo Bay. The military commission cases against Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and Abd al-Rahim al-Nashiri have been stalled for years, in large part because of disputes over evidence obtained through torture. Prosecutors have struggled to build cases that do not depend on statements made under waterboarding and other techniques, and defence lawyers have argued that the entire prosecutions are tainted. If the programme's use of waterboarding was wider than the official record shows, those disputes become harder still — and the argument that the commissions can deliver justice, more than two decades after the attacks, becomes harder to sustain.

Finally, the count matters because history is still being written. The CIA's detention programme was conducted in secret, in prisons whose locations were classified, under legal memos that were classified, by officers whose names were classified. The public record of what happened was assembled afterward, from fragments — a Senate report here, a prosecutor's file there, a translator's interview fourteen years later. Each fragment that surfaces changes the picture. The newly released files change it in a specific and measurable way: the number of people the CIA waterboarded was not three. It was at least four. And the programme's history, after twenty-five years, is still not fully known.
