Giorgio Armani. The designer, who died in September 2025 at 91, left a will ordering the sale of about 15% of his fashion house within 18 months of his death. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).

Fourteen months after Giorgio Armani died, the fashion house he built from nothing is about to change hands — piece by piece, exactly as he planned it. L'Oréal, the French beauty giant that has made Armani's perfumes and lipsticks for nearly four decades, is in talks to buy about 10% of the Italian fashion house, according to two people familiar with the matter who spoke to Reuters on 27 November. It would be the biggest slice of the 15% stake that Armani's will orders his heirs to sell, and the first step in a slow, carefully choreographed break-up of one of the last great independent names in luxury.

The deal is not done. Negotiations are ongoing, no final agreement has been reached, and the numbers could still move. But the shape of it is now clear, and it tells a story that goes far beyond a single share sale. Eyewear maker EssilorLuxottica, Armani's other great commercial partner, would buy around 2% to 3%. The heirs themselves — the niece, the nephew and the lifelong partner who inherited the empire — are considering buying a further 2% to 3% of the stake themselves, a twist the will never mentioned. And behind all of it sits a deadline: the 15% must be sold between 12 and 18 months after Armani's death, which puts the cut-off in early March 2027. After that comes the second act — the sale of a further 30% to 54.9% of the company, or a stock market listing — and a fight over whether the house Armani spent 50 years building stays independent at all.

The deal in numbers

Start with the figures, because they are the skeleton of the whole story. Armani's will requires the sale of around 15% of Giorgio Armani SpA. Of that 15%, L'Oréal is negotiating to take about 10%, EssilorLuxottica around 2% to 3%, and the heirs themselves around 2% to 3%, according to the Reuters report. Earlier reporting by the Italian newspaper Corriere della Sera had suggested EssilorLuxottica might take as much as 5%, which shows the split is still being negotiated — but the direction is consistent: L'Oréal takes the bulk, the eyewear partner takes a smaller piece, and the family keeps a hand in.

The price is the part nobody will confirm on the record. People close to the designer have put the asking price for the 15% stake at between €5 billion and €7 billion, according to French press reporting on the negotiations. If that range is right, the arithmetic is striking: a 15% stake worth €5–7 billion implies a total value for the whole Armani group of roughly €33 billion to €47 billion. That would make Armani one of the most valuable private luxury companies on the planet — and it explains why this sale, modest as 15% sounds, is being watched so closely across the industry. For comparison, L'Oréal itself, the buyer, is a company with annual sales of €43.48 billion in 2024, the largest cosmetics group in the world. A 10% stake in Armani would be a serious cheque even for a company of that size.

The timing matters as much as the money. Armani died on 4 September 2025, at the age of 91, after 50 years at the helm of the brand he founded in Milan in 1975. His will set a window of 12 to 18 months from his death for the first sale, which means the stake must change hands between September 2026 and early March 2027. L'Oréal formally announced on 19 November that it had begun discussions to acquire 10% of the brand, in line with the founder's wishes. Eight days later, Reuters reported that the heirs were considering buying part of the stake themselves. The clock is running, and the parties are now in the final stretch.

The will that forced the sale

To understand why any of this is happening, you have to understand the will — because Giorgio Armani did not leave the future of his company to chance. He left a document that reads almost like a business plan, and his heirs are now carrying it out to the letter.

The will does two things. First, it orders the sale of about 15% of the company between 12 and 18 months after his death. Second, it sets out what happens next: the disposal of a further stake of between 30% and 54.9%, or a stock market listing. And it names the preferred buyers, in order: his long-standing licensing partners L'Oréal and EssilorLuxottica, and the French luxury group LVMH — or another player in the sector "of equivalent standing". It is, in effect, a controlled demolition of his own empire, staged over years, designed to keep the house alive while gradually opening it to the outside world.

Why would a man who spent half a century guarding his independence order his own company to be sold? The answer lies in what Armani feared most. He had no children. He had watched other great Italian fashion houses — Gucci, Valentino, Versace — pass into the hands of French conglomerates or private equity, and he had spent his life refusing to let that happen to his own. But he also knew that a company owned entirely by a foundation and a handful of heirs, with no outside capital and no stock market discipline, would struggle to survive in a luxury industry that is consolidating fast. The will is his compromise: sell a minority stake now, to partners he trusted, and let his heirs decide later — with the money and the breathing room the first sale provides — whether to sell more or to list the company on the stock market.

The will also specified, in detail, the number of shares each heir will have to contribute to form the 15% stake. That matters, because the heirs are not a single bloc. They are three people with different roles, different ages and different stakes in the business — and the will treats them as such.

Who gets what — and who the heirs are

The three heirs at the centre of the story are a study in how Armani ran his company: family, and one outsider who became family. Silvana Armani, 71, the designer's niece, is in charge of womenswear design. Andrea Camerana, 56, his nephew, sits on the company's board. And Leo dell'Orco, 74, Armani's long-time partner, leads menswear. Together with the Armani Foundation, they control the empire he left behind.

According to the Reuters report, it is these three who are considering buying a small part of the 15% stake themselves — Silvana, Andrea and Leo, the people who actually run the house day to day. The will did not mention the option of some heirs buying part of the initial 15% stake, which makes the move a notable improvisation: the family using the sale itself to tighten, rather than loosen, its grip on the company. If the heirs take 2% to 3%, they would be spending their own money to keep that much more of the house in family hands — a signal, if nothing else, that they are not in a hurry to let go.

For L'Oréal, the logic is different, and it starts with a contract. The French group has held the licence for Armani's fragrances, skincare and make-up since 1988, and that licence runs until 2050. Armani beauty — the Sì perfumes, the Luminous Silk foundation, the whole cosmetics line — is one of the most profitable beauty businesses in luxury, and L'Oréal makes it. Buying 10% of the parent company deepens a relationship that is already nearly four decades old, and it protects the licence that underpins a large slice of L'Oréal's luxury division. L'Oréal first signalled its interest within days of Armani's death: on 12 September 2025, it announced it would "seriously consider" acquiring the available shares in accordance with the designer's wishes. Fourteen months later, it is doing exactly that.

EssilorLuxottica's interest runs on the same track. The eyewear giant — the world's largest glasses maker, owner of Ray-Ban and Oakley — holds the Armani eyewear licence, an agreement that runs for more than another decade. A 2% to 3% stake is small, but it is defensive: it gives the company a seat at the table of a house whose glasses it makes, and it keeps a commercial partner close at a moment when the ownership of that house is in flux.

Why LVMH walked away

The most revealing detail in the Reuters report may be the name that is not in the deal. LVMH, the world's largest luxury group, run by Bernard Arnault, was named in Armani's will as a preferred buyer — and, according to one of the people familiar with the matter, it is currently not in the process. The French press has reported that LVMH has withdrawn from the race altogether.

There are good reasons for that. Unlike L'Oréal and EssilorLuxottica, LVMH does not hold a licence for any Armani product. The beauty licence sits with L'Oréal until 2050; the eyewear licence sits with EssilorLuxottica for more than another decade. LVMH could not integrate those highly profitable divisions into its own operations for years to come, even if it bought a stake. And the asking price — €5 billion to €7 billion for 15% — was judged too high by the designer's inner circle itself, given the investment that would be needed to revitalise the brand. For a group that has spent decades buying luxury houses outright, a minority stake at a full price, with no control and no licence to show for it, was evidently not an attractive proposition.

The withdrawal also tells you something about the state of the luxury market. LVMH is not short of money. If it wanted Armani, it could afford Armani. That it chose not to — at least not at this price, and not for this stake — suggests a market in which even the biggest buyers have become choosy, and in which the days of paying any price for a famous name are, for now, over.

The heirs' dilemma: sell, or list?

The 15% sale is only the first step, and everyone involved knows it. The second step in the will — a further sale of 30% to 54.9%, or a stock market listing — is where the real decision lies, and it is a decision the heirs would rather not make yet. Reuters reported earlier this month that the heirs favour an eventual stock market listing over a sale to a large industry player, because a listing would preserve the fashion house's independence. A listing lets the family raise money, give the company a market value and a currency for deals, and still keep control. A sale to LVMH or another conglomerate would end the independence Armani guarded for 50 years.

That preference shapes everything about the current negotiation. A 15% sale to friendly licensing partners — L'Oréal and EssilorLuxottica, companies that need Armani to stay healthy and independent because their own licences depend on it — is the gentlest possible first step. It brings in billions, it brings in allies, and it changes nothing about who runs the company. It buys time: time for the heirs to prepare a listing, time for the brand to be revitalised, time for the market to recover.

But time is not unlimited. The second step has no fixed deadline in the public reporting, but the logic of the will points in one direction: the heirs cannot hold 85% of a company worth tens of billions of euros forever without either selling more or listing it. The foundation that Armani created, which holds a large part of the company, is a charitable body, not a trading company. At some point, the economics will force a choice, and the heirs will have to decide whether the house their uncle built becomes a public company, a subsidiary of a French giant, or something else entirely.

What the 15% is really worth

Valuing a private luxury company is as much art as science, and Armani is harder than most. The group does not publish the kind of detailed accounts a listed company must. What is known is that the brand is vast: fashion, beauty, eyewear, watches, hotels, restaurants, homeware, sport — an empire built over five decades from a single Milanese label. The €5 billion to €7 billion asking price for 15% is the market's best guess at what that empire is worth, and it is a guess that has already been tested once: LVMH looked at it, and walked away.

Whether L'Oréal pays that price, or negotiates it down, will be the key detail when a deal is finally announced. A stake sale at the top of the range would value Armani at close to €47 billion — more than some listed luxury groups. A sale at the bottom would still value it at more than €33 billion. Either way, it would be one of the largest luxury transactions of the decade, and it would set the benchmark for the second, larger sale that the will demands.

For L'Oréal, the maths is not just about the stake. The group already earns a large share of its luxury profits from the Armani beauty licence. Owning 10% of the parent aligns its interests with the house's owners, gives it a voice in the company's future, and — crucially — positions it as the natural buyer if and when the second step comes. If Armani is ever sold outright or listed, the company that already owns a tenth of it, and makes its best-selling products, will be first in line.

What happens next

The immediate timetable is set by the will. The 15% sale must be completed within 18 months of Armani's death, which means by early March 2027. L'Oréal's formal announcement of discussions on 19 November, and the Reuters report of the heirs' interest on 27 November, suggest the parties are now in the final phase of negotiation. A deal could be announced within weeks, or it could slip into the new year — but the deadline is real, and it concentrates minds.

After that, the questions get bigger. Will the heirs move quickly toward a listing, or will they wait? Will L'Oréal use its 10% as a springboard to a larger stake when the second step arrives? Will LVMH return for the second sale, when a controlling stake — or at least a much larger one — is on offer, and the price might be different? And will the brand itself, under its new ownership structure, invest in the revitalisation that the asking price implies it needs?

The wider luxury industry will be watching, because Armani is the last of the great founder-owned houses to face this moment. Gucci went to Kering. Versace went to Capri, and then to Prada. Valentino went to a Qatari fund. Armani held out longer than any of them, and its founder planned his exit more carefully than any of them. The 15% sale is the first page of that plan being executed. How it goes will shape not only Armani's future, but the future of every independent luxury house that is still, for now, its own master.

The man who built it: 50 years from a single label

It is worth remembering what is actually being sold, because the numbers alone do not capture it. Giorgio Armani was born in 1934 in Piacenza, a small city in northern Italy, the son of a shipping company employee. He studied medicine for two years, left without a degree, did military service, and then — almost by accident — took a job as a window dresser at La Rinascente, Milan's great department store. From there he moved to the fashion house of Nino Cerruti, where he spent six years learning the trade. In 1975, at the age of 41, he founded his own label with his partner Sergio Galeotti, selling his Volkswagen Beetle to help fund the start-up. The first collection was menswear; womenswear followed a year later.

What Armani did next changed the way the world dressed. He took the suit — the stiff, padded, structured uniform of post-war business — and took the padding out of it. His unstructured jacket, soft-shouldered and fluid, became the look of the 1980s, and then of the decades after. Hollywood did the rest. When Richard Gere wore Armani in American Gigolo in 1980, the designer's name became a global shorthand for a certain kind of quiet, expensive elegance. The red carpet followed: for 40 years, the question at every awards ceremony was who was wearing Armani, and the answer was usually the biggest star in the room.

The business grew the same way the style did — slowly, carefully, and in every direction. Emporio Armani, the younger diffusion line, arrived in 1981. Then came beauty, eyewear, watches, homeware, hotels in Milan and Dubai, restaurants, chocolates, flowers. The Armani name ended up on everything from a €50 lipstick to a €500-a-night hotel suite, and the group stayed private through all of it, owned by the man who founded it and, after his death, by his foundation and his heirs. No stock market, no conglomerate, no outside shareholders — until now.

That is why the 15% sale matters beyond the money. For half a century, Armani was the proof that a fashion house could stay independent and still compete with the giants. Its founder's will is an admission that the next half-century will be harder — that independence, in a luxury industry dominated by three French groups, now needs outside capital and outside allies. The sale to L'Oréal and EssilorLuxottica is not a surrender. It is a negotiation with reality, conducted on Armani's own terms, in a document he wrote himself.

For L'Oréal, the deal also fits a pattern. The French group's luxury division — home to Lancôme, Yves Saint Laurent, Kiehl's and a dozen other names — is the largest luxury beauty business in the world, and the Armani beauty licence is one of its crown jewels. L'Oréal does not buy fashion houses; it buys beauty. But a 10% stake in the parent company is the closest it has ever come to owning a piece of a fashion house, and it shows how seriously it takes the relationship. If the Armani licence is worth protecting with a multi-billion-euro equity cheque, it tells you just how much money a single perfume — Sì, launched in 2013, still one of the world's best-selling fragrances — can be worth.
