TITLE: JP Morgan Says AI Could Wipe Out 24 Million Office Jobs in 5 Years — and Only 6% of Work Is AI-Assisted Today LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: JP Morgan warns AI could displace 24 million knowledge-worker jobs in five years — 16 million in the US alone. ---BODY---

Jamie Dimon, chief executive of JPMorgan Chase. His bank's wealth management arm warns that artificial intelligence could displace around 24 million knowledge-worker jobs worldwide over the next five years — with the United States facing the biggest share. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0.

Artificial intelligence could displace around 24 million knowledge-worker jobs worldwide over the next five years, according to a new report from JP Morgan Wealth Management. The estimate, published in the bank's report titled "Outlook 2027: The Price of Progress", is the starkest warning yet from a major Wall Street institution about what AI means for office work — and it comes with a twist that makes it harder to dismiss: the bank says most of the damage will not come from dramatic mass layoffs, but from jobs that simply never get filled.

The headline number is 24 million. That is the number of knowledge-worker jobs — the office jobs, the desk jobs, the jobs done on a computer — that could be displaced worldwide over five years under the report's illustrative scenario. It represents about 1.3 per cent of the global knowledge workforce every year for five years. And the United States, the country where AI is being adopted fastest, would bear the biggest share: if two-thirds of the displaced roles were located in America, around 16 million workers could be affected over five years, or roughly 3 million every year.

Those numbers are large enough to sound unreal. Three million American workers a year is roughly 2 per cent of US payrolls annually — more than four times the pace of manufacturing job losses in the 2000s, the decade when factory work collapsed across the American Midwest. JP Morgan is not saying this will definitely happen. The bank is careful to call its estimate an "illustrative scenario", not a forecast. But the fact that the world's biggest bank by market value is publishing numbers like these at all tells you how seriously the financial establishment now takes the AI jobs question.

And there is a second number in the report that is, in some ways, more surprising than the first. Only 6 per cent of US work hours are currently AI-assisted, the report notes — even though roughly two-thirds of tasks are theoretically exposed to AI. In other words, the technology that could displace 24 million jobs has barely started. The disruption JP Morgan is describing has not happened yet. It is coming.

How JP Morgan got to 24 million

The 24 million figure is not a guess. It is the result of a chain of assumptions, and understanding the chain matters, because each link can be challenged. The report's scenario starts with enterprise AI spending: the money companies will spend on AI tools, software and infrastructure over the coming years. JP Morgan assumes that half of that potential spending goes towards replacing jobs that would otherwise have been filled — that is, companies buy AI not just to make their existing workers more productive, but to do work that would otherwise have required hiring someone.

The second link is cost. The report assumes AI costs about half as much as the workers it replaces. That is the economic engine of the whole estimate: if AI can do a job for half the price of a person, companies will buy AI instead of hiring people, and the jobs disappear — not necessarily through layoffs, but through hiring that never happens. Put those two assumptions together — half of AI spending on replacement, at half the cost of a worker — and the arithmetic produces 24 million displaced knowledge-worker jobs worldwide over five years.

The third link is geography. The report assumes the displacement is concentrated in the United States, where AI adoption is furthest advanced and where knowledge work is most expensive. If two-thirds of the displaced roles are in America, that is 16 million US workers over five years, or about 3 million a year. That is the number that should stop you: 3 million workers a year, every year, for five years, in a country where the total workforce is around 160 million people.

JP Morgan is the first to say this is a scenario, not a prediction. Change the assumptions and the number changes. If companies spend less on AI than expected, or if AI turns out to cost more than half a worker, or if companies use AI mainly to boost productivity rather than replace people, the number falls. But the bank's scenario is deliberately built on moderate assumptions — half the spending, half the cost — not extreme ones. And the bank's own judgement is that the direction is clear even if the exact number is not: AI is going to displace a very large number of knowledge-worker jobs, and the process has barely begun.

The quiet way jobs disappear

The most important sentence in the reporting around JP Morgan's estimate is also the easiest to miss. "Much of the AI adjustment could come through roles left unfilled rather than layoffs." That is the mechanism the bank expects: companies will not necessarily fire their existing workers. They will simply stop hiring replacements. When someone leaves, retires or moves on, the job will not be re-advertised. The work will be done by AI instead. The headcount will shrink by attrition, not by redundancy notices.

This matters for two reasons. First, it means the displacement will be hard to see. There will be no single dramatic moment, no factory closure, no mass layoff announcement to point a camera at. The jobs will disappear one unfilled vacancy at a time, spread across thousands of companies and millions of decisions. The unemployment rate may barely move, even as the number of available jobs shrinks. A young graduate who cannot find her first office job will not show up in the layoff statistics. She will simply never be hired.

Second, it means the pain will be unevenly distributed. Workers who already have jobs — especially senior workers with experience, relationships and institutional knowledge — will mostly keep them. The people who suffer will be the ones trying to enter the workforce: recent graduates, career changers, people returning after a break. The labour market will quietly close its doors to newcomers while the people inside keep their seats. That is a slower crisis than mass layoffs, but in some ways a crueller one, because it is invisible to the statistics and to the politics that respond to statistics.

JP Morgan also warns that job losses concentrated in particular occupations or regions could create resistance to AI adoption. That is the bank's polite way of saying that if AI wipes out, say, entry-level legal work in one city or junior analyst roles in one industry, the people affected will fight back — through politics, through regulation, through unions. The displacement may be quiet, but the backlash may not be.

Only 6 per cent — and two-thirds exposed

The gap between 6 per cent and two-thirds is the most revealing number in the whole report. Only 6 per cent of US work hours are currently AI-assisted, according to JP Morgan, even though roughly two-thirds of tasks are theoretically exposed to AI. That means the technology is capable of touching two-thirds of the work Americans do, but it is actually touching only 6 per cent of it. The gap between what AI can do and what it is doing is enormous — and it is closing.

Why is the gap so large? Partly because adoption takes time. Companies need to buy the tools, train their workers, redesign their processes and work out what AI is actually good at. Partly because the technology is still improving: today's AI is good at drafting, summarising and analysing, but less reliable at the judgement calls, the relationship work and the creative leaps that make up much of knowledge work. And partly because of caution: companies are still learning where AI helps and where it hurts, and many are moving slowly on purpose.

But the gap is also the reason the 24 million number is credible. If AI were already doing two-thirds of the work, the displacement would have happened already, and the number would be history, not forecast. The fact that adoption is only at 6 per cent means the disruption is still ahead of us. Every percentage point of adoption that closes the gap represents more work done by AI and less work done by people. The report is not describing a wave that has passed. It is describing a wave that is still building, far out at sea, and the 6 per cent figure tells you how far away it still is — and how big it will be when it arrives.

There is also a warning hidden in the gap. If two-thirds of tasks are exposed to AI, then two-thirds of knowledge workers are, in some sense, competing with a technology that costs half as much as they do. Not all of them will lose. Many will find that AI makes them more productive, more valuable, more capable. But the ones whose work is mostly routine — the drafting, the summarising, the data-crunching, the first-pass analysis — are the ones most exposed. And those are, disproportionately, the jobs held by younger workers, by people early in their careers, by the people with the least power to resist.

The 3 trillion dollar question

JP Morgan's report is not only about jobs. It is also about money — the staggering amount of money being poured into AI infrastructure, and whether that spending will ever pay off. The bank estimates that the global AI industry will need about 3 trillion dollars in annual customer spending by the early 2030s to make its infrastructure investments financially viable. The AI ecosystem could generate around 3.5 trillion dollars in annual revenue during that period, the report estimates. The gap between 3 trillion in spending and 3.5 trillion in revenue is the profit that justifies the hundreds of billions being spent today on data centres, chips and power.

Those numbers are almost too big to grasp. Three trillion dollars a year is roughly the size of the entire economy of the United Kingdom. It is the amount the world would need to spend on AI, every year, for the AI industry to justify the investments already being made. If that spending does not materialise, the AI boom becomes an AI bubble, and the data centres and chips being built today become the stranded assets of tomorrow.

This is where the jobs story and the money story connect. The 3 trillion dollars of customer spending has to come from somewhere, and a large part of it will come from companies buying AI to replace labour. That is exactly the mechanism in the 24 million scenario: companies spend on AI because it is cheaper than hiring people, and the spending justifies the infrastructure, and the infrastructure makes AI cheaper, and the cycle continues. The jobs displacement is not a side effect of the AI boom. It is the business model. AI pays for itself by being cheaper than people, and the cheaper it gets, the more people it replaces.

That is why JP Morgan's report matters beyond the headline number. It is the first time a major bank has laid out the full logic of the AI economy in one place: the spending, the revenue, the jobs, and the connections between them. The bank is not anti-AI. It is one of the biggest investors in AI infrastructure in the world. It is simply doing the arithmetic, and the arithmetic says that the AI economy, as currently structured, needs to displace a very large number of jobs to pay for itself.

What JP Morgan thinks will actually happen

For all the drama of the 24 million number, JP Morgan's own judgement is more measured than the headline suggests. The bank says the more likely outcome is disruption within jobs rather than the complete elimination of roles. That is an important distinction. Disruption within a job means the job changes: the routine parts get automated, the worker spends more time on the parts that require judgement, creativity and human contact, and the job itself survives in a different form. Elimination means the job goes away entirely.

Which outcome dominates will depend on the job. A lawyer who spends half her time drafting standard contracts may find that AI drafts the contracts and she spends her time on strategy and client relationships. Her job survives, transformed. A data-entry clerk whose entire job is transcribing information may find there is no transformed version of the role — the AI simply does it, and the job is gone. The report's 24 million is the number of jobs displaced under a scenario where AI replaces tasks. How many of those are transformed and how many are eliminated is the question that will decide the human cost.

The bank also notes that the adjustment could happen through roles left unfilled rather than direct layoffs, which softens the immediate blow but sharpens the long-term one. A company that stops hiring does not create a headline. But a generation of workers that cannot find its first job creates a different kind of crisis — one that shows up in delayed careers, lower lifetime earnings and a widening gap between those who got in before the door closed and those who did not.

There is a third possibility the report does not emphasise but that history suggests: new jobs. Every technological revolution has destroyed jobs and created new ones, and the optimists argue that AI will be no different. The pessimists reply that this time the new jobs require skills the displaced workers do not have, and that the transition will be faster and more brutal than the industrial or digital revolutions. JP Morgan's report does not settle that argument. It simply puts a number on the displacement side of the ledger, and leaves the creation side blank.

SAP, IBM and the week AI got real

JP Morgan's report did not arrive in a vacuum. It landed in the same week that SAP was reported to be weighing at least 10,000 job cuts as part of an AI-driven restructuring — the second such restructuring at SAP in less than three years. The two stories are the forecast and the fact: JP Morgan says AI could displace 24 million jobs, and SAP is the first big company to put a number on its own contribution to that total. When the bank that finances the AI boom and the software giant that sells AI to the world are both talking about job displacement in the same week, the conversation has changed.

The same week brought other signals. IBM has ended US visa sponsorship for employees, according to reports, leaving some workers facing a choice between a steep pay cut and losing their jobs. The details differ from the SAP and JP Morgan stories, but the theme is the same: the technology workforce is being reshaped, and the reshaping is accelerating. Companies are rethinking who they hire, where they hire, and how many people they need at all.

What is striking about this week is that the stories are no longer about AI's capabilities. Nobody is debating whether AI can write a good email or summarise a long document. The debate has moved to the second-order question: what happens to the people whose work AI can now do? JP Morgan's answer is 24 million. SAP's answer is 10,000. IBM's answer is a visa policy. The answers differ in scale, but they point in the same direction.

Who is most at risk — and who is safest?

The report's numbers are global, but the risk is not evenly spread. Knowledge workers are the target: the analysts, the paralegals, the junior accountants, the customer service agents, the content writers, the researchers, the people whose work is mostly reading, writing, summarising and analysing on a screen. The more routine the work, the more exposed it is. The more the work depends on judgement, relationships, creativity and physical presence, the safer it is — for now.

Geography matters too. The United States faces the biggest absolute number — 16 million under the two-thirds scenario — because it has the largest knowledge workforce and the fastest AI adoption. But the report's logic applies everywhere knowledge work is done. Countries with large outsourcing industries, where routine back-office work is performed for Western companies, face a particular risk: if AI can do the work in Ohio, it can do the work in Manila or Bangalore too, and the cost advantage that built the outsourcing industry disappears.

Age matters as well. The workers most exposed are the young. Entry-level roles are the most routine, the most automatable, and the easiest to leave unfilled. A company that stops hiring junior analysts does not need to fire anyone. It just needs to not replace the ones who leave. The result is a labour market that is increasingly closed to newcomers — a slow-motion crisis that is invisible in the unemployment rate but devastating for the generation trying to enter.

And there is a final, uncomfortable point. The report says the displacement could amount to about 2 per cent of US payrolls each year, more than four times the pace of manufacturing job losses in the 2000s. The manufacturing collapse of the 2000s reshaped American politics for a generation: it fuelled populism, hollowed out communities, and left a scar that is still visible. If knowledge-work displacement proceeds four times faster, the political consequences could be larger still. JP Morgan's warning about "resistance to AI adoption" is the bank's way of saying that people whose livelihoods are threatened will push back — and that the pushback could take forms the technology industry does not expect.

What workers and governments can do

The report does not offer a policy programme, but the implications are clear enough. For workers, the defence is to do the things AI cannot do well: exercise judgement, build relationships, solve novel problems, create something original. That is easy to say and hard to do, especially for workers whose current jobs are precisely the routine tasks AI is best at. Retraining helps, but retraining into what? The new jobs AI creates — AI trainers, AI auditors, AI ethicists — are real, but they are fewer than the jobs AI displaces, and they require skills that take years to build.

For governments, the options are familiar and difficult. Education systems need to teach the skills that complement AI rather than compete with it. Social safety nets need to catch people whose jobs disappear through attrition rather than layoffs — people who may never qualify for redundancy pay because they were never hired in the first place. And regulation needs to decide where the line is: which uses of AI to replace workers are acceptable, and which are not. None of these are new ideas. All of them are hard to implement at the speed AI is moving.

There is also a question for the companies themselves. JP Morgan's scenario assumes companies will use AI to replace workers because it is cheaper. But companies also have a choice. They can use AI to make their workers more productive and keep the headcount. They can use the savings from AI to expand into new markets and hire more people. They can treat AI as a tool for augmentation rather than replacement. The report's scenario is what happens if companies choose replacement. The future is not yet decided — but the incentives point in one direction, and SAP's reported 10,000 cuts suggest that at least some companies have already chosen.

The number that will define the decade

Twenty-four million is a number that will be quoted, debated and attacked for years. Critics will say the assumptions are too aggressive, the scenario too pessimistic, the timeframe too short. Defenders will say the assumptions are moderate, the scenario is illustrative, and the real number could be higher. Both sides will be partly right. The exact number is unknowable. The direction is not.

What makes JP Morgan's report different from the many AI jobs warnings that have come before is who wrote it and when. This is not an academic paper or a think-tank report. It is the wealth management arm of the largest bank in the United States, published in its flagship annual outlook, in the same week that one of Europe's biggest companies was reported to be cutting 10,000 jobs to become AI-first. The establishment is no longer dismissing the AI jobs question. It is quantifying it.

The report's most important contribution may be the 6 per cent figure. Only 6 per cent of US work hours are AI-assisted today. The disruption has barely started. The 24 million jobs are not a description of the present. They are a description of the future — a future that is still being built, still being negotiated, and still, to some extent, still a choice. But the window for choosing is closing. Every month that adoption rises from 6 per cent towards two-thirds, the choice gets narrower. JP Morgan has done the arithmetic. The rest of us now have to decide what to do with it.

For the knowledge worker reading this on a screen — the analyst, the writer, the researcher, the junior lawyer — the report is not an abstraction. It is a description of the forces already reshaping your profession. The question is not whether AI will change your job. It is whether your job will be transformed, or whether it will simply never be filled again. JP Morgan has given us the number. The answer, for 24 million people, is still being written.
