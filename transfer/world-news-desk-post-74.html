TITLE: 76 Hostages, 17 Hours, 1 Dead Gunman: Inside the Cartagena Supermarket Siege LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Colombian forces freed all 76 hostages after a 17-hour siege at an Exito supermarket in Cartagena, killing one gunman and arresting four ---BODY---

The Clock Tower Gate (Puerta del Reloj) in Cartagena, Colombia, the Caribbean port city where 76 people were held hostage for 17 hours. Photo: Bernard Gagnon via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

It began as a robbery that went wrong, and it ended, 17 hours later, as one of the most dramatic hostage rescues in Colombia's recent history. On Tuesday morning, November 24, 2026, armed men who had just failed to hijack an armoured cash-transport vehicle at a shopping mall in Cartagena fled into a branch of the Éxito supermarket chain in the city's La Castellana neighbourhood — and took everyone inside hostage. When it was over, in the early hours of Wednesday, November 25, security forces had stormed the store in a hail of flashbang grenades, freed all 76 hostages unharmed, killed one of the gunmen and arrested four others.

The number itself was a shock. For most of the day, the authorities had said that roughly 30 people were being held inside the supermarket. It was only on Wednesday, after the siege was over, that Colombian police revealed the true figure: 76 people — customers and store employees — had been held by the gunmen, more than double the number initially reported. Ten of them, including several elderly women, had been released during hours of negotiations on Tuesday. About 20 were still inside when the special forces went in. All of them came out alive.

"I would like to inform the entire public that, through an extraordinary joint operation between the National Police and the military forces, all the hostages have been successfully rescued," President Abelardo de la Espriella said in a video message recorded in Cartagena at 1:30 a.m. local time on Wednesday, shortly after the raid. The president had rushed to the city to personally command the operation, and he approved the decision to storm the supermarket when negotiations stalled and the gunmen refused to surrender.

For Cartagena, a Caribbean port famed for its colonial walled old town, its beach resorts and its cruise-ship tourism, the siege was a day of terror in a city that sells itself to the world as a paradise. For Colombia, it was a reminder that the violence the country has lived with for decades — the armed robberies, the organised crime, the bombings — has not gone away, even if its form has changed. And for the 76 people who spent 17 hours inside that supermarket, it was a day they will never forget.

How a failed robbery became a hostage crisis

The crisis began on Tuesday morning at around 8:00 a.m. local time, according to local reports, when a group of armed men tried to hijack an armoured vehicle carrying cash from a shopping mall in Cartagena. The attempt failed. Police responded swiftly, and the gunmen — cut off from their target and their escape route — fled into the nearest large building they could find: a branch of Éxito, Colombia's largest supermarket chain, in the La Castellana neighbourhood, along a major thoroughfare outside the city's historic walled colonial district.

Inside, it was an ordinary Tuesday morning. Shoppers were buying groceries; employees were stocking shelves and working the tills. Within minutes, the gunmen had seized control of the store and taken everyone inside hostage. The first reports spoke of about 30 people held. In fact, there were 76 — customers and staff together — a number that only emerged later, when police pieced together who had been in the building. A police source told the news agency AFP, on condition of anonymity, that there were 76 hostages and three assailants involved in the standoff.

As the hours passed, images began circulating on social media that showed the reality inside the store: people kneeling against the shop windows with their hands bound, visible to the crowds and cameras gathering outside. The photographs, grainy and frightening, travelled around the world within hours, and they turned a local crime story into an international one.

The gunmen, according to local media, demanded safe passage out of the supermarket, about US$2 million in cash, and an armoured vehicle — the same kind of vehicle they had failed to hijack that morning. It was, in effect, a demand to be given the robbery they had failed to commit, plus a getaway. The authorities refused. Instead, negotiators worked through the day, and during those hours the gunmen released 10 hostages, including several elderly women, in what appeared to be a gesture of good faith — or, more likely, a way of reducing the number of people they had to control.

By nightfall, the situation had not improved. About 20 hostages were still inside, the gunmen were refusing to surrender, and the authorities were running out of patience. President de la Espriella, who had arrived in Cartagena after visiting the central city of Villavicencio — where an overnight car bombing near government offices had injured 22 people and damaged roughly 500 homes and businesses — took personal command of the operation. He approved the plan to storm the supermarket, and in the early hours of Wednesday, shortly after midnight, National Police commandos, working in coordination with the military, went in.

The raid: flashbangs, gunfire, and every hostage alive

The raid itself was over quickly. Police and soldiers entered the building shortly after midnight, using flashbang grenades — devices that produce a blinding flash of light and a deafening bang to disorientate anyone inside — to subdue the gunmen before they could harm the hostages. One of the gunmen was killed in the assault. Four others were arrested and taken into custody, according to local media reports. Every remaining hostage was freed unharmed.

"In a precision operation, our National Police commandos, in coordination with the @FuerzasMilCol, managed to rescue the people who were still being held hostage inside a chain store in Cartagena," the president said. Cartagena's mayor, Dumek Turbay, confirmed that all the freed hostages had emerged unharmed and were undergoing medical evaluations before being reunited with their families. The rescued people were checked by doctors, given time to recover from the shock, and then handed back to relatives who had spent the day waiting outside the cordon, not knowing whether the people they loved were alive or dead.

The Colombian Presidency Press Office released a photograph of President de la Espriella shaking hands with hostages rescued by the security forces — an image that was carried by newspapers around the world, and that the government clearly intended as proof that the operation had worked. For a president who had taken personal command of the rescue, the photograph was also a political statement: that the state, faced with armed criminals holding dozens of citizens, had acted decisively and brought everyone home alive.

Officials gave no immediate details about the identities of the hostage-takers or the hostages. It was not clear, in the first hours after the raid, whether the gunmen were members of an organised criminal gang, freelancers attempting a high-stakes robbery, or something else entirely. What the president did say, firmly, was that guerrilla or paramilitary factions were not involved in the supermarket siege — an important distinction in a country where the line between common crime and armed conflict has always been blurred, and where the public's first fear, when gunmen seize a building, is that it is the start of something larger.

A city that sells paradise, a country that knows violence

Cartagena is one of the most visited cities in South America. Its walled old town, a UNESCO World Heritage site, draws hundreds of thousands of tourists a year. Its beaches, its colonial architecture, its restaurants and its cruise terminal are the engine of the local economy. A 17-hour hostage siege in a supermarket, broadcast live on social media, is the opposite of the image the city wants to project — and the authorities knew it. That is one reason the response was so swift and so forceful: a siege that drags on for days in a tourist city does damage far beyond the building it happens in.

But the siege also fits into a larger and grimmer pattern. Colombia has lived with armed violence for more than half a century, and while the peace agreement with the FARC guerrillas in 2016 formally ended the longest-running conflict in the Americas, the country has never been free of it. Dissident factions of the FARC, the ELN guerrillas, paramilitary successor groups and organised crime networks all continue to operate, and the violence they generate — bombings, kidnappings, assassinations, extortion — remains a daily reality in many parts of the country.

The same day as the supermarket siege, a car bombing in Villavicencio, a city in central Colombia, injured at least 22 people and damaged roughly 500 homes and businesses. The president had been visiting Villavicencio before he flew to Cartagena, and the bombing there was attributed to FARC dissidents — the same kind of group the president was careful to say had nothing to do with the supermarket siege. The two events, on the same day, in two different cities, tell the story of a country fighting two kinds of violence at once: the organised, political violence of armed groups, and the opportunistic, criminal violence of gangs who see a cash-transport vehicle and decide to take it.

The supermarket siege belonged to the second category, but it could easily have become the first. A failed robbery that turns into a hostage crisis is a familiar pattern in Colombia, and in Latin America more broadly, where heavily armed criminal groups have learned that hostages are the most valuable thing they can seize when a robbery goes wrong. What made this one different was its scale — 76 hostages is an extraordinary number for a supermarket robbery — and its ending: every hostage freed alive, in a single coordinated raid.

The 76: who they were, and what they went through

Among the 76 hostages were ordinary people doing ordinary things. Shoppers who had gone to the supermarket on a Tuesday morning to buy food for the week. Employees who had arrived for a normal shift and found themselves, within minutes, kneeling on the floor with their hands bound, in front of the shop windows, while cameras gathered outside. Elderly women who were among the first to be released, after hours of negotiation, and who walked out of the store into the arms of relatives they had not been sure they would see again.

The details of what happened inside the store over those 17 hours are still emerging, and they will emerge slowly, as the hostages recover and begin to talk. What is known, from the images that circulated on social media and from the accounts of those who were released during the day, is that the hostages were held in the open, in the main body of the store, under the eyes of the gunmen and, through the windows, under the eyes of the world. They were not hidden in a back room. They were displayed — whether as a warning, as leverage, or simply because the gunmen had no other way to control so many people in so large a space.

Seventeen hours is a long time to be held at gunpoint. It is long enough for the initial terror to give way to exhaustion, for hunger and thirst to set in, for people to wonder whether the negotiations outside are working or whether the police are about to come through the doors shooting. It is long enough, too, for the hostages to form the strange, intense bonds that people in such situations often describe afterwards — the shared glances, the whispered reassurances, the small acts of kindness between strangers who may never meet again. Those stories will come out in the days and weeks ahead, and they will be the real story of this siege: not the tactics of the raid, but the experience of the people who lived through it.

For now, what matters is the outcome. All 76 hostages were freed unharmed. They underwent medical examinations. They were reunited with their families. No hostage was killed, and no hostage was seriously injured — an outcome that, given the numbers involved and the length of the siege, is remarkable, and a credit to the negotiators who bought time during the day and to the commandos who ended it in the night.

What happens to the gunmen — and what the siege means for Colombia

The four surviving gunmen are now in custody, and they will face charges that, in Colombia, carry long prison sentences: kidnapping, aggravated robbery, illegal possession of firearms, and attempted murder among them. The investigation will also try to answer the questions the authorities have not yet answered: who organised the attempted hijacking of the cash-transport vehicle, who supplied the weapons, whether the group had attempted similar robberies before, and whether they had help from inside the mall or the supermarket.

The killing of one gunman during the raid will also be investigated, as it should be: in any operation of this kind, the use of lethal force by the state must be examined, even when the outcome is one the public welcomes. But the early accounts suggest the raid was conducted with the hostages' safety as the priority, and the fact that every hostage survived is the strongest evidence that it was conducted well.

For Colombia, the siege raises questions that go beyond the fate of four men. The first is about the security of cash in transit. Armoured vehicles carrying cash are a tempting target, and the attempted hijacking that started this crisis was not the first of its kind. If the gangs are growing bolder, and better armed, then the companies that move cash and the police who protect them will need to change their methods.

The second question is about the supermarkets and shopping malls themselves. Éxito is Colombia's largest supermarket chain, with hundreds of stores across the country. A branch in a major city was, in effect, taken over by armed men in a matter of minutes. The chain, and the shopping-mall industry more broadly, will have to ask whether their security is adequate — and whether a store full of shoppers should be so easy to seize.

And the third question is the one Colombia has been asking itself for decades: whether the violence is getting better or worse. The peace agreement of 2016 was supposed to be the beginning of the end. A decade later, the country is still counting the dead from bombings and sieges, and a president is still flying from one crisis to another in a single day. The rescue in Cartagena was a success, and it deserves to be celebrated as one. But it was a success inside a failure — the failure to prevent armed men from walking into a supermarket and taking 76 people hostage in the first place.

How the world watched a siege unfold on social media

One of the striking features of the Cartagena siege was how much of it the world saw in real time. Within hours of the gunmen entering the store, photographs and videos taken through the supermarket's windows were circulating on social media — images of hostages kneeling with bound hands, of police vehicles massing outside, of soldiers taking up positions around the building. By Tuesday evening, the siege was front-page news in London, New York, Dubai and Sydney, and the names Cartagena and Éxito were trending far beyond Colombia.

That visibility was a double-edged sword. For the hostages' families, the images were agonising — proof that their loved ones were inside, but no proof that they were safe. For the police, the coverage was a complication: every movement outside the store was visible to the gunmen inside, and every rumour on social media risked reaching them before the negotiators did. And for the gunmen themselves, the cameras were leverage: the more people watching, the more pressure on the government to give them what they wanted. In the end, the raid came in darkness, after midnight, when the cameras could see least — and that, too, was surely no accident.

The day Cartagena will not forget

By Wednesday morning, the Éxito in La Castellana was a crime scene, cordoned off and guarded by soldiers. The hostages were home, or in hospital for checks. The president had flown on to his next engagement. And Cartagena was trying to return to normal — the tourists returning to the old town, the cruise ships docking as scheduled, the city doing what it has always done after violence: carrying on, because it has no other choice.

But for the 76 people who were inside that supermarket, and for their families, normal will take a while to return. Seventeen hours is a long time, and the memory of it — the gunmen, the bound hands, the windows, the waiting, the flashbangs in the dark — will not fade quickly. What they will also remember, and what they have already told reporters, is the moment the doors opened and the police came in, and the moment they walked out into the daylight and saw their families waiting.

That is the ending of this story, and it is a good one: 76 hostages taken, 76 hostages freed, one gunman dead, four arrested, and a city that held its breath for 17 hours finally able to breathe again. In a country that has seen so many hostage crises end in bloodshed, that is not a small thing. It is, in fact, the thing that matters most.
