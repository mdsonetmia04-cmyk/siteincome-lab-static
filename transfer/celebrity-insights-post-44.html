The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth is in cinemas today — and after seven years of waiting, the verdict is finally in. Brad Pitt is back as the stuntman-turned-fixer Cliff Booth, David Fincher is directing a script by Quentin Tarantino, and the film carries an 82 percent score on Rotten Tomatoes from 112 critics and a 69 on Metacritic from 42 critics, a "generally favorable" result for one of the strangest and most talked-about team-ups in modern Hollywood. It is also, by some distance, the most unusual release of the year: a reported $200 million movie opening on roughly 250 IMAX screens in the United States, for two weeks only, before it lands on Netflix on December 23.

That combination — a beloved character, two of the biggest names in cinema, glowing reviews from some critics and a shrug from others, and a release plan that most of America literally cannot attend — is exactly why this film has dominated film conversation for a month. It is not a normal blockbuster opening. It is an event, an experiment, and a test of whether the most famous director-writer pairing of their generation can still pull a crowd when the crowd has to find an IMAX screen first.

This is the full story: the reviews, the numbers, the plot, the strange road that brought Fincher and Tarantino together, the exhibitors who refused to play it, and what happens when the two-week IMAX window closes.

The Reviews Are In: 82 Out of 100

The headline number is the one every film fan checks first. As of Wednesday morning, The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth holds an 82 percent rating on Rotten Tomatoes, based on 112 critic reviews, according to the site's movie page. On Metacritic, the film carries a Metascore of 69 out of 100, drawn from 42 critic reviews — 30 positive, 10 mixed and just 2 negative — which the site classifies as "generally favorable."

Those two numbers tell a slightly different story, and the gap between them is the story of this film. An 82 percent on Rotten Tomatoes means more than four out of five critics recommend it. A 69 on Metacritic means the average review is warm rather than ecstatic. Put together, they describe a film that almost everyone enjoys and almost nobody calls a masterpiece — a very good hang of a movie, rather than a landmark. For a film that exists largely because its creators wanted to spend two more hours with a character they love, that may be exactly the right result.

The scores also mark a clear improvement on the secrecy that surrounded the film until now. Before this week, almost nobody outside the production had seen it. Only the opening 17 minutes were shown at AFI Fest in October 2026, and the world premiere did not take place until November 16 in Los Angeles. For a $200 million film starring Brad Pitt, that is an extraordinary level of secrecy — and it meant the first real test of the film was always going to be the review embargo lifting in release week. It lifted, and the film survived it comfortably.

The audience score, for now, does not exist yet. Metacritic lists the user score as "to be determined," with no user ratings recorded at the time of writing, because the film has only just reached the public. That will change fast over the Thanksgiving weekend, and the audience verdict — not the critics' — is the number that will decide whether this becomes a cult favourite or a curiosity.

What the Critics Loved — and What They Didn't

Read the reviews in full and a pattern emerges quickly: the film's greatest strength and its greatest weakness are the same thing. It is, above everything else, a pleasure to spend time with Cliff Booth — and for some critics, that pleasure is enough, while for others it is not quite a film.

The most enthusiastic reviews are genuinely enthusiastic. HeyUGuys awarded the film 5 out of 5, with Linda Marric calling it "a confident, funny and quietly melancholy follow-up that stands tall beside its predecessor," and singling out Pitt as "the main draw" while praising Elizabeth Debicki for making "a strong impression." Little White Lies gave it a perfect 100, arguing that "all the performers here are supremely on message, and the chemistry is as precise as one of Cliff's experimental cocktails," and crediting Fincher above all: "his silky direction and sense of screwball pacing just moves this thing along like a juggernaut." The San Francisco Chronicle also gave it 100, calling it "pure entertainment" and "the best showcase yet for Brad Pitt's charm," and noting that his ability "to sound like the voice of reason while exhibiting the craziest behavior is the movie's main joke, and it's an effective one."

The Film Stage scored it 91, calling it "an assured, mature work that doesn't forget to have fun" and "Fincher's best in fifteen-something years," adding that Tarantino and Fincher "couldn't have found each other at a better moment." The Daily Beast gave it 88, describing it as "an Elmore Leonard/Donald E. Westlake-style dime-store crime yarn that's superior to its acclaimed predecessor and the best thing the Fight Club and The Social Network filmmaker has made in years." Polygon, also at 88, struck the most emotional note: "Even if we never see Cliff Booth again, I'm glad we have this weird little movie. It might not be perfect, but getting to spend a couple more hours in the world that Tarantino created feels like a gift."

Empire awarded 4 out of 5, with Dan Jolin calling it "an indulgently long, loose-fit good hang of a movie that will satisfy lovers of Once Upon A Time In Hollywood, even if it won't resonate so strongly," and adding, in a line that has already been quoted everywhere, "while it may just be a burger, it is a tasty burger." Radio Times also gave 4 out of 5, praising the "top tier production design, a number of engaging narrative detours and no shortage of sparkling dialogue scenes," and concluding that the film "is still a class above the vast majority of American crime flicks." IGN scored it 80, noting that "Quentin Tarantino may not have directed this sequel to Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, but David Fincher deftly captures Tarantino's unique voice and the spirit and tone of his films." The Guardian, also at 80, wrote that "the film can only work with the drawlingly imperturbable good humour of Brad Pitt as Cliff. His performance is endlessly watchable and entertaining."

The dissent is real, and it is worth reporting honestly, because it defines the film's limits. The Los Angeles Times' Amy Nicholson was unimpressed, arguing that "becoming a badass makes Cliff way less interesting. The movies already have plenty of those," and that his "Teflon-slickness seems too easy." The New York Post gave it just 25, dismissing it as a film "coasting on the fumes of A-list aura" and arguing that without Tarantino directing, "the dialogue and story, such as it is, are limp." The BBC scored it 60, saying it is "definitely not the hyper-stylish, boundary-pushing, Oscar-worthy masterpiece that, not so long ago, you might have assumed the writer of Pulp Fiction and the director of Fight Club would make together." IndieWire gave it 67, calling it "pleasant enough for a movie so dark, and strangely relaxed for a movie so deeply self-conflicted."

That split — delight on one side, "pleasant enough" on the other — is the honest shape of the critical response. Nobody is calling it a disaster. Nobody is calling it the film of the year either. What almost every review agrees on, positive or negative, is that Pitt is superb, that Fincher's direction is immaculate, and that the film is, at minimum, a hugely entertaining two hours and twenty minutes in the company of a character audiences already love. For a sequel that exists because its creators wanted to revisit that character, that is a success on its own terms.

The Story: Cliff Booth in 1977

The film is set in 1977, eight years after the events of Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood, Tarantino's 2019 film, which unfolded in a parallel-history version of 1969. The official synopsis is short and deliberately pulpy: "Murder, blackmail, theft, extortion — just another summer in Hollywood. It's 1977, eight years since Cliff Booth encountered the Manson family in Quentin Tarantino's parallel history of 1969. In The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, directed by David Fincher from a new script by Tarantino, the fairytale is over."

The fairytale being over is the point. In the first film, Cliff was Rick Dalton's stunt double, best friend and loyal shadow, a man whose easygoing charm masked a violent past — and whose finest hour came when he defended Rick's home from the Manson family in a sequence that rewrote history into a dark joke. Eight years later, Rick Dalton is gone from Cliff's life. Leonardo DiCaprio does not return in this film, and the central double-act of the original is absent by design. Cliff is on his own now, and the film follows him through a Los Angeles that has changed around him.

According to the reviews, Cliff now works a variety of jobs around the city, including as a cinema projectionist and a bartender — and, most notably, as a fixer for Hollywood's assorted rogues and crooks. That last job is the engine of the plot: a man with Cliff's particular skills, his total lack of fear and his unshakeable calm, is exactly the person powerful people call when they have a problem that cannot be solved legally. The film is, in the words of several critics, a crime yarn in the tradition of Elmore Leonard and Donald E. Westlake — a dime-store thriller with a literary brain — in which Cliff is pulled into a tangle of murder, blackmail, theft and extortion over the course of one long Hollywood summer.

Elizabeth Debicki co-stars, and her performance has been singled out in multiple reviews as the film's standout supporting turn. HeyUGuys praised her for making "a strong impression," and her character — kept deliberately vague in the pre-release marketing — appears to be central to the film's emotional core. The rest of the ensemble has been kept largely under wraps, which is consistent with a production that showed only 17 minutes of footage before release and guarded its plot as closely as any film this year.

The film runs 2 hours and 20 minutes and is rated R for strong disturbing violence, nudity, drug use, bloody images, pervasive language and sexual content. That is, in other words, a full-strength Tarantino script — and a reminder that this is not a softened, streaming-friendly version of the character. Fincher, a director famous for precision and control, directing Tarantino's dialogue-heavy, digressive, analog style, is the central creative gamble of the film, and the reviews suggest the gamble mostly pays off: Spectrum Culture called the pairing "a fascinating mashup" that "manages to succeed as a reflection of its creators' anxieties, successfully expanding the world of Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood."

Fincher Directs Tarantino: How the Strangest Team-Up in Hollywood Happened

To understand why this film exists at all, you have to understand the unusual deal behind it. Quentin Tarantino has said for years that he will direct only ten films and then stop. Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood was his ninth. For a long time, the assumption in Hollywood was that his tenth and final film would be a sequel following Cliff Booth — a character Tarantino has said he loves, and a story he had already written as a novel-length continuation. Then Tarantino changed his mind about directing it himself, and instead handed the screenplay to David Fincher.

That is the strangest part of the story, and the part that has fascinated the film world for two years. Tarantino, the most famously controlling director alive — a filmmaker who writes, directs, casts and edits with total authority — gave his script to another director. And not just any director: Fincher, the director of Fight Club, Se7en, The Social Network and Gone Girl, a filmmaker whose style is the opposite of Tarantino's in almost every way. Tarantino is analog, loose, digressive, romantic about the past. Fincher is digital, precise, forensic, cold. The Film Stage called it "Fincher's best in fifteen-something years" and said the two men "couldn't have found each other at a better moment." The Daily Beast called it "the best thing the Fight Club and The Social Network filmmaker has made in years."

The result, according to the reviews, is a film that feels like both men at once: Tarantino's dialogue, characters and 1977 Los Angeles, filtered through Fincher's immaculate craft. Spectrum Culture's review put it best, describing "the pairing of Tarantino's analog, dialogue-heavy style with Fincher's digital precision" as "a fascinating mashup." The Guardian praised Pitt's "drawlingly imperturbable good humour," which is a Tarantino quality, inside a film whose production design and pacing — "top tier," according to Radio Times — are pure Fincher.

There is also a quiet melancholy running through the film that the best reviews keep returning to. HeyUGuys called it "quietly melancholy." Screen Rant noted that Fincher "is able to visually tap into the sadness of the character and the time period at the heart of the film." The Film Stage called it "an assured, mature work." This is not the triumphant, fairy-tale ending of the first film. It is a film about a man eight years older, in a Hollywood that has moved on without him, doing jobs that are smaller and darker than the ones he used to do. That sadness, the reviews suggest, is what makes the film more than a victory lap — and it is also what makes the question of whether Tarantino will ever direct his tenth film feel more open than it did a year ago. Polygon's review ended on exactly that note, calling the film "a gift — especially when the director seems so hesitant to actually deliver his 10th and final film."

The $200 Million Gamble on 250 Screens

The most remarkable number attached to this film is not a review score. It is the budget. The film was reportedly made for around $200 million, which Tarantino himself has described as the largest budget he has ever been associated with, according to trade reporting. That figure makes the release plan look, on its face, almost absurd: a $200 million film opening on roughly 250 IMAX screens in the United States, for two weeks, before moving to Netflix.

The tracking reflects that reality. Trade reports ahead of the opening put the film's Thanksgiving weekend in the single-digit millions — TheWrap reported that it is "expected to open in the single digits," and Thanksgiving-weekend previews described it as "limited to 250 IMAX locations and expected to earn single-digit millions." For a film that cost $200 million, a single-digit opening weekend would be a catastrophic result under normal theatrical economics. But this is not a normal theatrical release, and the economics are not normal either.

Netflix financed the film, and Netflix's business does not depend on box-office grosses. The two-week IMAX window is a prestige play, not a commercial one: a chance for the film to be seen the way Fincher intended, on the biggest screens available, and to build the kind of cultural conversation that drives streaming viewing when the film arrives on Netflix on December 23. Netflix has said it will report the grosses from the IMAX run, which is itself unusual for the company, and suggests it expects the numbers to be worth talking about — most likely on a per-screen basis, where a $200 million film on 250 screens can still post an impressive average.

The comparison that puts the release in perspective is the first film. Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood opened in 2019 to $41.1 million across 3,659 theatres, and finished its run at $377.4 million worldwide. This sequel has roughly a fifteenth of that theatre count and two weeks instead of an open run. As one trade analysis put it, "the only number that will mean anything is the per-screen average over Thanksgiving, before Dec. 23 makes the whole question moot." That is the honest way to judge this opening: not by the total, which was never going to be large, but by how full those 250 IMAX screens are.

There is also a straightforward commercial logic to the two-week window that is easy to miss. A film that is only in cinemas for two weeks, and only on IMAX, creates urgency. Fans who want to see it properly have a deadline. The film's final trailer, released on November 18, leaned into exactly that, selling the names — Pitt, Tarantino, Fincher — and the screen, rather than the plot. And the Thanksgiving timing is deliberate: the film opens on the Wednesday before Thanksgiving, one of the busiest moviegoing weeks of the year, when families are together and looking for something to do. It will not be the biggest film of the weekend — that will be The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping in its second weekend — but it does not need to be. It needs to be the film that film lovers talk about, and on that measure it is already winning.

The IMAX-Only Experiment — and the Cinemas That Said No

The release plan has a backstory that is almost as interesting as the film itself. The two-week IMAX window was not originally built for Cliff Booth at all. According to trade reporting, Netflix inherited the IMAX corridor from Greta Gerwig's Narnia: The Magician's Nephew when that film moved to a full February rollout, which is why a $200 million Fincher-Tarantino-Pitt film is opening in Thanksgiving week with a built-in two-week shelf life. The slot was available, the film was ready, and Netflix took it.

Not every cinema chain wanted it. TheWrap reported that the film is "getting released exclusively on approximately 250 Imax screens, most of them at AMC locations." Regal, with roughly 100 IMAX screens, and Cinemark, with roughly 20, both declined — their IMAX screens went to The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping instead. Cinemark chief executive Sean Gamble described the model as "not aligned with what's of the greatest interest in our theaters," a polite way of saying that a two-week exclusive that sends audiences to Netflix on December 23 is not a deal exhibitors love.

IMAX's own chief executive, Rich Gelfond, took the opposite view on the company's November 5 earnings call. "Frankly, I don't understand why anybody wouldn't play it," he said, and the numbers behind his confidence are real. IMAX reported record financial results for the third quarter of 2026, with total revenue of $174.6 million, up 64 percent, driven by a record-breaking quarterly global box office of $712 million — nearly double the previous record. The Odyssey alone has grossed more than $550 million globally, making it the highest-grossing IMAX release in the company's history. IMAX is having the biggest year it has ever had, and a Fincher film shot for the format is exactly the kind of title it wants.

The exhibitor split also reveals something about where the film industry stands in 2026. A decade ago, a Netflix film would not have received a theatrical release at all. Now Netflix is not only releasing films theatrically, it is fighting over IMAX screens with the biggest franchises in the business — and winning some of them, through AMC, while losing others. The two-week window is a compromise that neither side fully loves: too short for exhibitors, too expensive for a streamer, but the only model that gets a film of this scale onto the biggest screens while keeping the streaming date close. Whether it works will be judged not just by this film's grosses but by whether other prestige films follow the same path next year.

The Original: What Cliff Booth Means to Hollywood

It is worth remembering, in the middle of all the numbers, why this character matters enough to justify a $200 million sequel. Once Upon a Time... in Hollywood, released in 2019, was Tarantino's ninth film and one of his most beloved: a sun-drenched, melancholy love letter to a Los Angeles that no longer exists, built around the friendship between Rick Dalton, a fading television western star played by Leonardo DiCaprio, and Cliff Booth, his stunt double, driver, handyman and best friend, played by Brad Pitt.

Pitt won the Academy Award for Best Supporting Actor for the role, and it is easy to see why. Cliff is one of Tarantino's great creations: a man of total calm and total capability, funny without trying to be, loyal without being sentimental, and dangerous in a way he never advertises. The film's most famous sequence — Cliff's visit to the Spahn Ranch, where he confronts the Manson family — is a masterclass in slow-building menace, and the finale, in which Cliff and Rick defend Rick's home from the Manson attackers in a burst of dark, cartoonish violence, rewrote one of the darkest nights in Hollywood history into a joke. The film grossed $377.4 million worldwide and received ten Oscar nominations.

Cliff Booth also matters because of what he represents in Tarantino's work. He is the working man in a film about stars — the stuntman, not the actor; the man who does the dangerous work while someone else gets the credit. That is a theme Tarantino has returned to throughout his career, and it is the reason the character stuck with audiences long after the film left cinemas. Fans have spent seven years wondering what happened to Cliff after 1969. This film is the answer, and the answer is: not a fairytale. The title says it plainly — the further mis-adventures — and the reviews say the film delivers on that promise, with a Cliff who is older, sadder and still, somehow, the coolest person in every room he walks into.

Thanksgiving Weekend: The Box Office Around It

Cliff Booth does not open in a vacuum. Thanksgiving week is one of the biggest moviegoing periods of the year, and this year's weekend has a clear hierarchy. The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping dominates, heading into its second weekend after a $225 million worldwide opening — a debut that put it just ahead of Wicked: For Good, which opened to $223 million globally in final figures. Wicked: For Good enters its second weekend with the Thanksgiving holiday ahead of it, historically one of the strongest periods for family musicals.

Disney's animated Hexed opens this weekend, and Universal and Paramount's Focker-in-Law, the fourth film in the Ben Stiller and Robert De Niro comedy series that began with Meet the Parents in 2000, also arrives. Angel Studios' Hershey, a biopic of the chocolate founders Milton and Kitty Hershey starring Finn Wittrock and Alexandra Daddario, is tracking for a $6–8 million five-day opening, despite a critical panning that left it with an 18 percent Rotten Tomatoes score.

The overall Thanksgiving weekend is expected to come in below last year's $290 million total, let alone the $423 million record set in 2024, according to TheWrap's preview — largely because there is no single $100 million-plus opener this year. That context matters for Cliff Booth, because it means the film is not competing for the biggest weekend of the year. It is competing for attention, for conversation, and for the per-screen average that will define its theatrical story. Box-office results for the Thanksgiving holiday will be released on Monday, November 30, and that is when the first real numbers for the film will be known.

Where and When to Watch

For anyone who wants to see the film as it was intended, the window is short and the options are specific. The film is in cinemas from today, Wednesday, November 25, 2026, exclusively on IMAX screens, for a two-week run. Tickets are sold through the film's official site, cliffboothfilm.com, which routes to participating IMAX theatres. In the United States, that means roughly 250 IMAX locations, most of them AMC theatres. Internationally, the film is also opening in limited release today in markets including the United Kingdom, Germany, Italy — where it is released as Le Avventure di Cliff Booth — Spain and Brazil.

After the two-week IMAX run ends, the film moves to Netflix, where it will stream globally from December 23, 2026. That is 28 days after the theatrical opening — a short window by traditional standards, and a deliberate one. Netflix wants the film to be a Christmas-week streaming event, and the IMAX run is designed to make sure it arrives there with the maximum possible conversation behind it. For viewers without access to an IMAX screen, December 23 is the date. For everyone else, the next two weeks are the only chance to see a Fincher film the way Fincher shot it.

One practical note: because the run is limited to around 250 screens in the United States, popular showtimes over the Thanksgiving weekend are likely to sell out, particularly in major cities. The film's producers have not announced any extension beyond the two weeks, and the Netflix date is fixed. If you want the big-screen version, the time to book is now.

What Happens Next

Three questions will define the next month for this film, and all three will be answered quickly. The first is the box-office question: what does a $200 million film earn on 250 screens over Thanksgiving weekend? The numbers arrive on Monday, November 30, and the per-screen average will be the figure everyone quotes. The second is the audience question: what do regular viewers think, once the critics' scores are joined by audience scores and word of mouth? The early signs — a film that critics mostly loved, a character audiences already adore, and a release designed to create urgency — point to a warm reception, but the audience verdict is the one that lasts.

The third question is the biggest, and it has nothing to do with this film's grosses. It is whether Quentin Tarantino will ever direct his tenth film. He has said for years that he will stop at ten. This script was, by most accounts, the film that would have been number ten. He gave it to Fincher instead. Polygon's review captured the feeling that now hangs over the whole project: "getting to spend a couple more hours in the world that Tarantino created feels like a gift — especially when the director seems so hesitant to actually deliver his 10th and final film." If this film succeeds — critically, culturally, and on those 250 IMAX screens — it may be the thing that convinces Tarantino to pick up the camera one more time. If it doesn't, it may stand as the closest he ever came: his words, his character, his world, directed by the one filmmaker he trusted to do it justice.

Either way, the film is here, it is out today, and the reviews say it is worth the trip. A 62-year-old stuntman, eight years past his finest hour, walking back into the Hollywood he no longer recognises — played by the biggest movie star in the world, written by the most famous screenwriter alive, directed by the most precise filmmaker of his generation. That is a sentence that would have sounded impossible two years ago. Today, it is a film you can go and see. Just make sure you find an IMAX screen first — there are only 250 of them, and the clock is already running.
