TITLE: At 36, Jess Wilson Just Ended 12 Years of Labor Rule — Victoria's First Woman Premier and Youngest Since Federation LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Jess Wilson, 36, will become Victoria's 51st premier after the Coalition's landslide win ended 12 years of Labor rule. Ben Carroll lost his own seat. ---BODY---

Jess Wilson, leader of the Victorian Liberal Party, who will become Victoria's 51st premier after the Coalition's landslide win in the 2026 state election. At 36, she is the state's first woman premier and its youngest leader since federation. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0).

It took barely four and a half hours. Polls closed in Victoria on Saturday afternoon, and by 7:30 p.m. the analysts had seen enough: the Liberal–National Coalition had won the 2026 Victorian state election in a landslide, ending 12 years of Labor government, and Jess Wilson — 36 years old, a first-term lawmaker, the leader of her party for barely a year — would become the state's 51st premier. She is the first woman to lead a party to victory at a Victorian state election, the first Liberal woman to become premier of the state, the youngest premier since federation, and the first millennial to hold the job. The premier she replaces, Ben Carroll, did not merely lose the election. He lost his own seat — the first Victorian premier to do so since Ian MacFarlan in 1945.

The scale of the defeat is difficult to overstate. Labor went into the election holding 54 of the 88 seats in the Legislative Assembly. By the early hours of Sunday, the Australian Broadcasting Corporation was projecting that the party would win just five of them — and as of 12:20 a.m. on Sunday, 29 November, with 42.6 percent of the vote counted, the ABC had the Coalition on 50 seats, the Greens on seven, Labor on four, One Nation on one, and 26 seats still in doubt. In the Legislative Council, the ABC projected the Coalition was likely to win 11 of the 40 seats, One Nation eight, the Greens seven and Labor six, with eight seats in doubt. Labor's primary vote sat at about 15.6 percent in the early counting — behind One Nation, on about 23.5 percent, and the Greens, on about 15.9 percent — while the Coalition led on about 34 percent. It was, by any measure, the worst election result in Labor's history in Victoria, a state the party has governed for 23 of the last 27 years.

Carroll called Wilson to concede defeat a few hours after the polls closed. "Victorians have spoken, and their message is clear: they want change," he said. Wilson's own message was simpler, and it was the line she had campaigned on: "Victoria is back open for business." She will be sworn in once the result is formally confirmed, and she has promised to serve as her own Treasurer for at least the first 100 days of her government — a pledge that puts her, from the first day, at the centre of the state's finances, and of the argument about what went wrong.

A landslide called early

The speed of the result was itself a story. Media outlets projected the Coalition had won a majority of seats in the Legislative Assembly early in the evening after the polls closed, and the ABC's projection came shortly after 7:30 p.m. AEDT on Saturday, 28 November — barely four and a half hours after the polls closed. By 8 p.m., with 6.4 percent of the vote counted, the Coalition had already won 28 seats outright, the Greens two, One Nation one and Labor one, with 56 of the 88 seats still in doubt. Forty-five seats are needed for a majority. The Coalition had it, in effect, before most Victorians had finished dinner.

The early numbers told the story in miniature. Labor's collapse was not a matter of a few marginal seats falling, or of a swing that could be explained away. It was a collapse across the board, in the party's safest seats as well as its most marginal ones, and it was visible in the primary vote before a single preference had been distributed. A party that had governed Victoria for 12 years, and that had held 54 seats, was reduced in the first hours of counting to a rump — and the count was still going. The final seat tally may take days, as postal and pre-poll votes are counted, but the result itself was never in doubt after the first hour.

The result was, in the words of the Australian Broadcasting Corporation, a landslide — and it was a landslide that had been widely expected. Labor's defeat had been predicted by the polls, and by the analysts, for weeks. But the scale of it surprised even the people who had predicted it. A party that loses 49 seats in a single night does not lose because of a bad campaign, or a bad week, or a single mistake. It loses because the voters have decided, collectively and decisively, that it is time for it to go. And in Victoria, on Saturday, that is what the voters decided.

The woman who won it

It has been a swift rise for the 36-year-old Wilson, and it is one of the most remarkable in Australian politics. She was elected leader of the Victorian Liberal Party and Leader of the Opposition on 18 November 2025, replacing Brad Battin after the 2025 Victorian Liberal Party leadership spill — the party's fifth leadership change in seven years. She was the first woman to lead the Victorian Liberals. In less than four years, she has gone from making her maiden speech in state parliament as the new member for Kew to premier of Victoria.

Wilson's victory makes her the first woman to lead a party to victory at a Victorian state election, and the state's first popularly elected female premier. Victoria has had two women premiers before her — Joan Kirner, who led the state from 1990 to 1992, and Jacinta Allan, who became the second female premier in the state's history — but neither won an election as leader. Wilson is the first to do so. She is also the state's youngest premier since federation, and its first millennial premier, a fact that has not been lost on the commentators who have spent the weekend trying to explain what happened.

Her campaign was built on a single, disciplined message: that Victoria, after 12 years of Labor, was closed for business, and that she would open it again. The state, she argued, was drowning in debt — the biggest state debt in the country — and choking on the cost of the projects and the promises that Labor had made and could not pay for. Her promise to serve as her own Treasurer for at least the first 100 days was a part of that message: it told the voters that she would take personal responsibility for the state's finances, and that the finances were the first thing she would fix. "Victoria is back open for business" was not a slogan. It was a promise, and it was the promise the voters chose.

Wilson also won the campaign's televised debate, receiving 39 percent of viewers' responses, compared with 25 percent for Greens leader Ellen Sandell and 16 percent for One Nation leader Warren Pickering — a result that, in retrospect, looks like a preview of the election itself. And she did it while leading a party that had changed leaders five times in seven years, a party that had been written off as unelectable, and a party that had not won government in Victoria since 2010. The scale of what she has done — taking a divided, demoralised opposition and turning it into a landslide government in a single year — is the reason this election will be studied for years.

Twelve years, and a premier who lost his seat

The defeat belongs, above all, to Ben Carroll, the man who led Labor into it. Carroll became the 50th Premier of Victoria in July 2026, after Jacinta Allan resigned — having pulled out of a leadership contest that Carroll, then the Deputy Premier, had announced he would challenge. Carroll was elected unopposed as Labor leader, and he sought re-election for a fourth consecutive four-year term for a government that had been in power since 2014. He lost, and he lost his own seat of Niddrie in the process — becoming the first Victorian premier to lose his seat since Ian MacFarlan in 1945, more than 80 years ago.

Carroll's defeat is the end of a Labor era that began in 2014, when Daniel Andrews led the party back to government, and that continued through Allan's premiership and into Carroll's brief tenure. For 12 years, Labor governed Victoria — through the pandemic, through the lockdowns, through the infrastructure boom and the debt that came with it, through the controversies and the scandals and the elections it kept winning. The party had governed the state for 23 of the last 27 years, and it had come to regard government as its natural condition. On Saturday, the voters told it otherwise.

The reasons for the defeat are not mysterious, and they were rehearsed throughout the campaign. Victoria, the analysts said, was carrying the country's biggest state debt, the legacy of a decade of borrowing for infrastructure — the level-crossing removals, the Metro Tunnel, the Suburban Rail Loop — that the state could not afford and that the voters had come to associate with waste. The state had also presided over the world's longest lockdown of a city, Melbourne, during the COVID-19 pandemic, leaving a residual distrust among voters that never fully faded. And the government, after 12 years, had the tiredness that long governments acquire — the sense, among voters, that it had run out of ideas, and that the people running it had run out of energy.

Carroll, who had been premier for barely four months, could not distance himself from that record, because he was a part of it — he had been Allan's deputy, and a minister in the Andrews government before that. His concession, delivered a few hours after the polls closed, was gracious and brief. But it could not hide the scale of what had happened to him personally: a premier who had held his seat for years, who had been elected unopposed as leader, and who had lost both the election and his own seat on the same night.

One Nation, and the party that did not break through

The election was also a test for One Nation, the anti-immigration party led in Victoria by Warren Pickering, and the result was a study in how a party can win votes and still lose. One Nation's primary vote, at about 23.5 percent in the early counting, was ahead of Labor's and ahead of the Greens' — a remarkable figure for a party that had never been a force in Victorian politics. But the party won only one seat in the early projections, and its leader, Pickering, contested Pakenham and lost. The votes, in other words, were spread across the state, and they did not translate into seats — the fate of every minor party that wins a large share of the vote in the wrong places.

For the Coalition, that was the best possible outcome. The party had feared that One Nation would split the conservative vote, and that the split would cost it seats in the marginals. Instead, the Coalition won a majority of its own, and One Nation's votes — distributed across the state, and flowing to the Coalition on preferences in the seats that mattered — helped rather than hurt it. The result was a Coalition government, a Labor rump, and a One Nation that had proved it could win a quarter of the vote and still win almost nothing. That is the paradox of the Australian electoral system, and it is one that One Nation will be thinking about for a long time.

The Greens, led by Ellen Sandell, did better. The party was projected to win seven seats in the Legislative Assembly, up from its previous representation, and seven in the Legislative Council — a result that makes it, on the early numbers, the second-largest party in the lower house after the Coalition, and a genuine force in the upper house. The Greens' leader, Sandell, held her seat of Melbourne, and the party's vote — about 15.9 percent in the early counting — was ahead of Labor's. That, too, is a measure of how far Labor has fallen: the party of government, reduced to fourth place in the primary vote, behind a minor party it once dismissed as a fringe.

What it means for Australia

Victoria is a bellwether state, and its elections are watched across the country for what they say about the national mood. The defeat of Labor in Victoria piles further pressure on Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, who is halfway through his second term, and who leads a Labor government in Canberra that has watched its sister party in Victoria collapse. The result does not, by itself, decide the next federal election. But it tells the Prime Minister something he already knew: that the voters are in a mood to punish governments, and that a party that has been in power for a long time, in a state or in a country, cannot assume that it will be given another chance.

The result also says something about the state of Australian politics more broadly. The Coalition's win in Victoria — in a state long regarded as one of the centre-left's bastions — is the second major state election the conservatives have won in recent years, and it suggests that the political tide, which ran strongly to the left in the early 2020s, has turned. The One Nation vote, at 23.5 percent, says something too: that a large minority of Victorians are drawn to a party that promises to stop immigration and to put Australia first, even if that party cannot yet turn its votes into seats. And the Greens' result — seven seats, and a primary vote ahead of Labor's — says that the voters who have left Labor have not all gone to the right. Some of them have gone to the left, and to a party that promises to do what Labor no longer does.

For Wilson, the work begins now, and it will be harder than the campaign. She has promised to restore fiscal discipline to a deeply indebted state, to open Victoria for business, and to serve as her own Treasurer for the first 100 days. The debt will not disappear because she promises to fix it. The infrastructure projects will not finish themselves. The public service, the unions, the councils and the interest groups that Labor cultivated for 12 years will not simply accept a new government that intends to cut their funding. And the voters who gave her a landslide will expect results quickly — because landslides, in Australian politics, are followed by expectations, and expectations are followed by disappointment if they are not met.

The year that made her

Wilson's rise is all the more remarkable for how recently it began. She entered the Victorian Parliament as the member for Kew less than four years ago, and made her maiden speech as a new backbencher in a party that was, at the time, in the middle of a long period of internal turmoil. The Victorian Liberal Party had changed leaders five times in seven years by the time Wilson took over, and Brad Battin — the leader she replaced — had lasted only eleven months in the job. The party was divided, demoralised, and widely regarded as unelectable. The conventional wisdom, repeated by almost everyone who covers Victorian politics, was that Labor would win a fourth term, and that the Liberals would spend another four years in opposition arguing among themselves.

Wilson changed that in a year. She became the first woman to lead the Victorian Liberals on 18 November 2025, after ousting Battin in a leadership spill, and she spent the year that followed imposing a discipline on her party that it had not known in a decade. The leadership changes stopped. The public divisions stopped. The message — that Victoria was closed for business, and that she would open it again — was repeated, without variation, for twelve months, until the voters could repeat it back. And on Saturday, they did.

Her background helped. As the member for Kew, one of Melbourne's most affluent electorates, she understood the business community that Labor had alienated, and the professional voters who had once voted Liberal and had drifted away. As a first-term lawmaker, she carried none of the baggage of the party's previous leaders, and none of the associations with the factional fights that had defined it. And as a 36-year-old, she could speak to a generation of Victorians who had grown up under Labor, who had lived through the lockdowns as teenagers or young adults, and who had never known a Liberal government in the state. She was, in every sense, a new face — and in an election in which the voters wanted change, a new face was the most valuable thing a party could offer.

The debate, held in the final week of the campaign, showed it. Wilson was declared the winner, receiving 39 percent of viewers' responses, compared with 25 percent for the Greens' Ellen Sandell and 16 percent for One Nation's Warren Pickering. It was not a landslide in the debate, but it was a clear win, and it confirmed what the polls had been saying for weeks: that Wilson was the only leader in the race who looked like a premier, and that the voters had noticed.

What happens next

The counting continues on Sunday, including postal and pre-poll votes, and the final seat tally may take days. The result itself is settled — the Coalition has won, and Wilson will be the 51st premier — but the size of the majority, and the composition of the parliament, will not be known until the count is complete. As of the early hours of Sunday, 26 seats in the Legislative Assembly were still in doubt, and the final figure for the Coalition could be higher than the 50 seats projected, or lower. The Legislative Council, where eight seats were in doubt, will also take days to finalise.

Wilson will be sworn in once the result is formally confirmed, and her ministry — the incoming 74th ministry of the Government of Victoria — will be announced in the days that follow. As of Saturday night, the full ministry had not been announced, and the only appointment that is certain is the one Wilson has already made: herself, as Treasurer, for at least the first 100 days. That decision, unusual for a new premier, is a statement of intent. It tells the public service, the business community and the voters that the state's finances are the first priority, and that the premier herself will be responsible for them.

The first 100 days will be watched closely, and not only by Wilson's supporters. She has promised to restore fiscal discipline to a deeply indebted state, and the first test of that promise will be the budget — what she cuts, what she keeps, and how quickly she moves. She has promised to open Victoria for business, and the first test of that will be the decisions she makes on the projects Labor left behind: the Suburban Rail Loop, the Metro Tunnel, the level-crossing removals, and the debt that came with them. And she has promised change, and the first test of that will be whether the voters who gave her a landslide can see it, and feel it, in their own lives.

For the rest of the country, the result will be studied for what it says about the national mood. Victoria is a bellwether, and a landslide in Victoria is a warning to every government in the country — state or federal, Labor or Coalition — that the voters are impatient, and that twelve years is a long time to be in power. For Wilson, the warning runs the other way: that a landslide is a loan, not a gift, and that the voters who gave it to her will expect it to be repaid. On Saturday, they gave her the job. On Sunday, the work begins.

But for this weekend, at least, the story is simple. A 36-year-old woman, who became the leader of her party a year ago, who had never led a party into an election, and who was written off by almost everyone who covers Victorian politics, has won a landslide, ended 12 years of Labor rule, and become the youngest premier in the state's history since federation. The voters of Victoria have spoken, and their message is clear: they want change. On Saturday, they got it — in the most decisive way possible, and from the most unlikely winner imaginable.
