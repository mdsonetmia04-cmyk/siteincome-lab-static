TITLE: America's Measles Outbreak Just Hit a 35-Year High — 5,838 Cases, 9 Dead in Pennsylvania Alone LABELS: Health, US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The US measles outbreak hit 5,838 cases, the worst since 1991. Pennsylvania reported its ninth death as the country lost its measles-free status. ---BODY---

Robert F. Kennedy Jr., United States Secretary of Health and Human Services, official portrait 2025. U.S. Department of Health and Human Services / Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

The United States is living through its worst measles outbreak in 35 years, and the numbers released this week are the grimmest yet. Pennsylvania — the hardest-hit state in the country — has reported its ninth measles death of the year, an unvaccinated resident of Centre County who tested positive for the virus and died. Nationally, confirmed cases have reached 5,838, the highest annual total since 1991. The country has lost the measles elimination status it held for 26 years. And the outbreak is still growing, with cases now reported across dozens of states, hospitals filling with patients suffering from a disease that a vaccine has prevented for generations, and health officials warning that the worst of the winter is still ahead.

This is not a story about a single state or a single death. It is a story about how a disease the United States declared eliminated in 2000 came roaring back — about vaccination rates, about the gap between state and federal numbers, about a health department under a secretary who has spent his career questioning vaccines, and about what happens when a highly contagious virus finds the gaps in a country's immunity. Here is what the data shows, what officials are saying, and what comes next.

Pennsylvania: the ninth death

The latest death was confirmed on Monday by the Pennsylvania Department of Health. An unvaccinated resident of Centre County, in central Pennsylvania, tested positive for measles and died. The department withheld identifying details about the patient to protect the family's privacy, but the fact of the death — the ninth in the state this year — was announced in a news release that laid out the scale of the crisis in stark terms.

Pennsylvania has now recorded 1,638 confirmed measles cases across 46 counties since the outbreak began in late April. The state has logged 330 hospitalizations — nearly one in five of the people identified with measles in Pennsylvania has needed hospital care, a figure that underscores the severity of an infection that is often described, wrongly, as a routine childhood illness. The nine deaths span several counties, with three in Lancaster County and two in Jefferson County, and the rest spread across the central part of the state.

The outbreak in Pennsylvania is the largest in the nation, and it has been growing for seven months. It began in late April and spread county by county, carried by a virus that is among the most contagious known to medicine. Measles spreads through the air, and it can linger in a room for up to two hours after an infected person has left. In a state where vaccination coverage has gaps — in communities where the measles, mumps and rubella vaccine is not universal — the virus found room to move.

State health officials have also reported a sharp rise in demand for the MMR vaccine in recent months, as the outbreak has driven families to seek protection. That surge in vaccination is a hopeful sign, but it comes late. The outbreak was already entrenched by the time demand rose, and the virus continues to spread through the unvaccinated population that remains.

The national numbers: 5,838 cases and counting

The national picture is worse than Pennsylvania's alone. A total of 5,838 measles cases were confirmed across the United States this year as of November 19, according to data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. That is the highest annual total since 1991 — a 35-year high — and the second consecutive record-breaking year. The number of confirmed cases in November 2026 alone is more than double the 2,289 confirmed measles cases reported for the full year of 2025.

The United States has now recorded more than 5,000 cases in 2026, and the vast majority of infections are among unvaccinated people. The country recently lost its measles elimination status, which it had maintained for 26 years since 2000. Elimination, in the language of public health, does not mean zero cases. It means no sustained, endemic transmission — no chain of infection that continues for twelve months or more. Losing that status means the virus is once again circulating on its own inside the United States, passed from person to person in ongoing chains that health officials have been unable to break.

The loss of elimination status is a formal, international judgment. The Pan American Health Organization reported last week that Mexico, the United States, and Bolivia have all lost their measles-free status after failing to curb endemic transmission of the virus. It is a humiliation for a country that once led the world in vaccination, and it is a warning. Measles was eliminated in the United States through decades of work — through school vaccination requirements, through public health campaigns, through a vaccine that is among the safest and most effective ever developed. Losing that achievement in a single year is a measure of how far vaccination coverage has fallen, and how quickly a preventable disease can return when it does.

The death toll tells the same story in human terms. Virginia recorded its first measles-related death in more than three decades on Friday. Wisconsin has reported two deaths. New York state has recorded one child death linked to the disease. Together with Pennsylvania's nine, the total measles death toll across the states stands at thirteen — and counting.

The gap between the numbers: states say 13, the CDC says 6

One of the most troubling aspects of the outbreak is that the federal government and the states cannot agree on how many people have died. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has reported only six measles-associated deaths so far in 2026, without specifying the states where the deaths were recorded. The states, counting their own dead, report thirteen. The discrepancy is not a rounding error. It is a gap of more than two to one, and it has become a story in its own right.

The gap emerged earlier this year after the health agency, under United States Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., began using mortality data from the National Center for Health Statistics instead of state figures. The change in methodology means the CDC's count lags behind, and counts differently, than the numbers reported by state health departments that are dealing with the outbreak on the ground. State officials report deaths as they are confirmed. The federal count, built on a different data source, moves more slowly and captures fewer cases.

The discrepancy matters for reasons beyond arithmetic. Public health depends on accurate, trusted numbers. When the federal government reports six deaths and the states report thirteen, the public does not know which number to believe — and the confusion feeds the distrust that helped create the outbreak in the first place. It also matters for the response. Federal emergency funding, staffing, and technical assistance are allocated based on the scale of the crisis as the federal government understands it. If the federal count understates the crisis by half, the response may understate it too.

The Department of Health and Human Services has said it is "saddened to hear of another death in Pennsylvania," and has noted that the CDC has provided the state with nearly $4 million in one-time emergency funding for response efforts in 2027. That funding is real help, and Pennsylvania needs it. But the gap between the numbers remains, and it has become a symbol of the deeper problem: a public health system in which the federal government and the states are no longer telling the same story about the same disease.

Kennedy and the health department

At the center of the federal response stands Robert F. Kennedy Jr., the 26th United States Secretary of Health and Human Services, who has served in the role since 2025. Kennedy is a figure unlike any previous health secretary: an environmental lawyer and author who has spent decades as a prominent critic of vaccines, and who has built a public career questioning the safety and necessity of the childhood immunization schedule. His appointment to lead the nation's health department was one of the most controversial of the administration, and the measles outbreak has become the defining test of his tenure.

The change in how the CDC counts measles deaths — from state figures to National Center for Health Statistics mortality data — was made under Kennedy's leadership, and it is the direct cause of the gap between the federal count of six and the states' count of thirteen. Critics of the change argue that it obscures the true scale of the outbreak at precisely the moment when the public most needs honest numbers. Supporters of the change, if any have spoken publicly, have not made a prominent case for it.

Kennedy's department has also been the target of criticism for its messaging on the outbreak. Public health experts have argued that the response has lacked the urgency and clarity that a crisis of this scale demands — that the department has been slow to promote vaccination, slow to acknowledge the severity of the outbreak, and slow to coordinate with the states that are bearing the brunt of it. The $4 million in emergency funding for Pennsylvania is a concrete step, but it is a single step in a crisis that spans the country.

What is not in dispute is the scale of the challenge Kennedy faces. The United States has lost its measles elimination status. Cases are at a 35-year high. Thirteen people are dead, including a child in New York and nine in Pennsylvania. The virus is circulating in dozens of states. No health secretary in a generation has faced a crisis quite like this one, and the way it is handled in the coming months will define Kennedy's legacy — and the country's health — for years.

The global picture: Mexico and Bangladesh

The American outbreak is part of a larger, global resurgence of measles. Mexico has reported 12 active cases of the disease, all of them in the southern state of Chiapas, Health Minister David Kershenobich said on Tuesday. Mexico, like the United States, has lost its measles-free status, the Pan American Health Organization reported last week. Kershenobich said that 90 percent of the Mexican population is vaccinated, which makes a wider outbreak much less likely, and that 10 million doses of the measles vaccine are available. The contrast with the United States is instructive. Mexico, with higher vaccination coverage, has twelve active cases. The United States, with lower coverage in the communities where the virus is spreading, has nearly six thousand.

The crisis is far worse in Bangladesh, where the measles outbreak has claimed nine more lives in a single day, raising the total number of confirmed and suspected deaths from the disease to 1,361 in 2026. According to the country's Directorate General of Health Services, the deaths were reported in the 24 hours leading up to Tuesday morning. The number of confirmed deaths remained at 105, while the total number of suspected deaths climbed to 1,256. The directorate recorded 1,283 new suspected measles cases during the same 24 hours, bringing the overall tally of suspected cases to 259,190, with 68 new confirmed cases increasing the total to 26,300.

The Bangladesh figures are a reminder of what measles does in a country where the health system is under strain and vaccination coverage is incomplete. More than a thousand dead, a quarter of a million suspected cases, and an outbreak that continues to grow. The American outbreak, terrible as it is, is still measured in thousands of cases and a dozen deaths. The Bangladeshi outbreak is measured in hundreds of thousands of cases and more than a thousand deaths. The difference is one of scale, not of kind. The virus is the same. The vaccine is the same. The only difference is how many people have received it.

Why measles spreads — and why the vaccine matters

Measles is one of the most contagious diseases known to medicine. An infected person can spread the virus to 90 percent of the unvaccinated people around them. The virus travels through the air in droplets released when an infected person coughs or sneezes, and it can remain infectious in the air for up to two hours after the person has left the room. There is no treatment that kills the virus once a person is infected. There is only supportive care — fluids, fever management, and treatment for the complications — while the body fights the infection on its own.

The complications are what make measles dangerous. The disease can cause pneumonia, which is the most common cause of measles-related death in children. It can cause encephalitis, a swelling of the brain that can leave survivors with permanent neurological damage. It can cause severe diarrhea and dehydration. And it can suppress the immune system for months or years after the infection, leaving survivors vulnerable to other diseases they would otherwise have fought off. In Pennsylvania, nearly 20 percent of the people identified with measles have been hospitalized — a measure of how serious the disease is, even in a country with modern hospitals.

The vaccine, by contrast, is among the most effective ever developed. The measles, mumps and rubella vaccine — MMR — is about 97 percent effective at preventing measles after two doses. It has been used safely for more than half a century, and it is the reason the United States was able to declare measles eliminated in 2000. The vaccine does not merely protect the person who receives it. It protects the community. When enough people are vaccinated, the virus cannot find a chain of susceptible hosts, and outbreaks cannot take hold. That is herd immunity, and it is what the United States has lost in the communities where the virus is now spreading.

The vast majority of infections in the current outbreak are among unvaccinated people. That fact, repeated by health officials in every state with cases, is the simplest summary of the crisis. The virus is not spreading because the vaccine failed. It is spreading because too many people have not received it.

What happens next

The outbreak is entering its most dangerous season. Winter is coming, and with it the months when respiratory viruses spread most easily — when people gather indoors, when schools are in session, when holiday travel carries the virus from state to state. Thanksgiving week, the busiest travel week of the year, is already underway, with tens of millions of Americans moving across the country by road and by air. Every crowded airport, every packed terminal, every family gathering is an opportunity for a virus that spreads through the air.

Health officials are watching the numbers closely, and they are worried. The outbreak has already broken records for two consecutive years. The case count in November alone is more than double the entire previous year's total. The death toll is rising. And the virus is circulating in states that had not seen sustained measles transmission in decades. If the current trajectory continues, the final case count for 2026 will be far higher than the 5,838 already recorded — and the death toll will rise with it.

The response, for now, is a patchwork. Pennsylvania has received nearly $4 million in federal emergency funding for 2027. States are running vaccination campaigns, urging families to get the MMR vaccine, and tracking contacts of confirmed cases. Demand for the vaccine is rising sharply in the hardest-hit areas, which is the most hopeful sign in the crisis. But vaccination takes time to build immunity, and the outbreak is moving faster than the response.

What the coming months will show is whether the United States can do what it did a generation ago: vaccinate its way out of a measles crisis. The tools exist. The vaccine exists. The knowledge exists. What remains to be seen is whether the will exists — in the federal government, in the states, and in the communities where the virus is spreading — to use them before the outbreak claims more lives.

The bottom line

The numbers are stark. 5,838 confirmed cases — the most since 1991. Nine dead in Pennsylvania, thirteen across the country. A 26-year elimination status, lost. A federal count that says six dead, and states that count thirteen. A health secretary whose career was built on questioning vaccines, now presiding over the worst measles outbreak in a generation. And a virus that spreads through the air, finds the unvaccinated, and sends one in five of its victims to the hospital.

Measles is a preventable disease. That is the sentence that matters most, and it is the sentence that makes this outbreak a tragedy rather than a mystery. The vaccine exists. It works. It has worked for more than fifty years. The outbreak is happening not because the disease is unstoppable, but because the protection against it has been allowed to lapse — in communities, in states, and in a country that once led the world in eliminating it. Until that protection is restored, the cases will keep coming, and the deaths with them.
