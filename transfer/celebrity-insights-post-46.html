Disney's Hexed opens in theatres across North America today, Wednesday, November 25, 2026 — the day before Thanksgiving — and it arrives carrying more weight than almost any animated film Disney has released in years. It is Walt Disney Animation Studios' first original feature since Wish, the film that marked the studio's 100th anniversary and then stumbled badly at the box office. It cost a reported $200 million to make, which puts it among the most expensive animated films Disney has ever produced. It needs roughly $500 million worldwide just to break even, according to trade reports. And after a review embargo that lifted only on Tuesday afternoon, it holds a 68 percent score on Rotten Tomatoes from 57 reviews — fresh, but only just, and only after a climb that began in rotten territory.

That climb is the story of the week so far. When the embargo lifted at noon Eastern on Tuesday, November 24, the first reviews landed hard. Forbes logged the film at 57 percent from 23 reviews that afternoon — officially rotten. Then, as more critics filed their verdicts through the evening, the number moved: 46 percent, 56 percent, 65 percent, and by Wednesday morning it had settled at 68 percent fresh, with an average rating of 6.7 out of 10. On Metacritic, the film holds a 56 from 22 critics, which the site labels mixed or average, alongside a generally favourable user score of 6.5. The short version, as one review tracker put it, is that most critics recommend Hexed, but few of them love it.

The box office forecasts are split almost as widely as the reviews. Variety's tracking has the film opening to $40 million to $45 million over the five-day Thanksgiving weekend. Box Office Pro puts the five-day range higher, at $50 million to $70 million. Box Office Theory, the most pessimistic of the major trackers, dropped its estimate to about $23 million on November 20. Other trade coverage lands in the middle, projecting $45 million to $50 million over the five days and a likely second-place finish behind The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, which opened to $120 million last weekend and now enters its second frame. The gap between the highest and lowest forecasts — $23 million to $70 million — is unusually wide, and it tells you everything about how uncertain this opening is. Nobody, not even the people who do this for a living, agrees on what Hexed will do.

That uncertainty is the point of this film. Hexed is not a sequel, not a remake, not a live-action reworking of a beloved classic. It is an original story, built from scratch, about a teenage girl who discovers she has magic, a mother who gets pulled into a hidden world of witches, and a family secret that has been buried for a generation. Original animation has been the hardest sell in Hollywood for the last four years. Strange World opened to $18.8 million over Thanksgiving in 2022. Wish managed $31.6 million a year later. Pixar's Hoppers took in $389 million worldwide against a $150 million budget earlier in 2026, and DreamWorks' Forgotten Island earned just $79.2 million worldwide despite strong reviews. The last two Thanksgivings belonged to sequels — Moana 2 and Zootopia 2 each topped $1 billion. Hexed is Disney's attempt to prove that an original animated film can still open a holiday weekend, and the whole industry is watching to see whether it can.

This is the full story of the opening: the reviews and how the score climbed out of rotten territory, what critics praised and what they could not forgive, the story and the cast behind it, the $200 million budget and the $500 million break-even line, the forecasts that disagree by tens of millions of dollars, the Thanksgiving curse it is trying to break, the competition it faces this weekend, and what happens next if the opening lands soft — or if it surprises everyone.

The Reviews: From 57 Percent Rotten to 68 Percent Fresh

The review embargo for Hexed lifted at noon Eastern on Tuesday, November 24, one day before the film opened in theatres. That timing alone told a story. Studios that are confident in a film usually lift embargoes days ahead of release, giving positive reviews time to circulate and build momentum. A Tuesday-noon lift, hours before opening night, is the move of a studio that is not sure what the critics will say — and for the first few hours, the caution looked justified.

The first batch of reviews put the film at 57 percent on Rotten Tomatoes from 23 reviews, officially rotten. Forbes reported the number that afternoon, and for a few hours the narrative was set: Disney's big Thanksgiving original was a critical disappointment. Then the score started to move. As more reviews were posted through Tuesday evening, the percentage climbed — past 46 percent, past 56 percent, past 65 percent — and by Wednesday morning, November 25, it stood at 68 percent from 57 reviews, with an average rating of 6.7 out of 10. The critics' consensus on the site was still pending when the embargo lifted, and the audience score had not posted yet, which means the numbers will keep moving as opening-night audiences weigh in.

The Metacritic picture is cooler. There the film holds a 56 from 22 critics, labelled mixed or average, with 41 percent of the counted reviews positive, 55 percent mixed and 5 percent negative. The user score sits at a generally favourable 6.5. The gap between the two aggregators is not unusual — Rotten Tomatoes measures the share of critics who recommend a film at all, while Metacritic weights how strongly they liked it — but the combination tells a consistent story: a film that more critics than not are willing to recommend, and that very few of them are passionate about.

For context, 68 percent is twenty points better than Wish, which sits at 48 percent. It is a long way behind the films Hexed is inevitably compared with — Moana and Coco both cleared 95 percent — and it sits in the middle ground that trade coverage has already identified: better than Disney's recent original flops, behind the franchise hits that defined the studio's last decade. TheWrap, reporting on the opening, described it as a middle ground between past flops and 2010s hits like Moana and Coco, and that framing has stuck, because it is accurate.

The most interesting number in the whole review picture may be the trajectory rather than the destination. A film that starts at 57 percent rotten and climbs to 68 percent fresh over the course of an evening is a film whose reception depends heavily on which critics see it first and which see it later. The early reviewers were the harshest. The later ones were kinder. That pattern usually means the film's pleasures are real but narrow — the kind of movie that wins over the people it is made for and leaves everyone else politely unmoved. Whether that is enough for a $200 million film opening on Thanksgiving is the question the box office will answer.

What the Critics Loved: The Heart, the Look and the Cat

Strip away the score and read the actual reviews, and a clear pattern emerges. The praise, where it comes, concentrates on three things: the emotional core of the mother-daughter story, the quality of the animation, and the voice performances — above all Walton Goggins as Beef, the cat.

Collider's Taylor Gates gave the film 7 out of 10, writing that the mother-daughter story is refreshing and that the film works as a gateway for a younger audience to be exposed to this specific brand of witchy storytelling. The review praised the characters and their complicated dynamics, and the immersive world they inhabit, as enough to cast a powerful spell — even while noting the film's similarities to everything from The Wizard of Oz to Beauty and the Beast and Agatha All Along.

TheWrap's Amelia Emberwing wrote that the film's already-sturdy emotional sensibilities are bolstered by strong performances from Hailee Steinfeld, Rashida Jones and Jodie Foster as the villainous Queen Celeste — but that it is Walton Goggins who steals the show as Beef, the cat. That single observation has been repeated across much of the positive coverage: the film's most memorable character may be a supporting animal sidekick, which is both a compliment to Goggins and a quiet indictment of the human leads' material.

Linda Marric of HeyUGuys gave Hexed 4 out of 5, noting that it may not be the studio's most polished spell, but it is one of its most human, and that humanity is a magic worth showing up for. Laughing Place gave it four stars, calling it a bold original. Odie Henderson of the Boston Globe gave it 3 out of 4, and ComingSoon's Jonathan Sim gave it 7 out of 10, calling it a pretty fun fantasy adventure that delivers a good amount of heart, laughs and magical mayhem.

The highest score on Metacritic came from AwardsWatch, which gave the film 91 and called it classic Disney magic, brewing the signature potion of nostalgia, wit and dazzling animation. The review argued that the formula for this spell has not been broken for nearly a century and remains enchanting today. The San Francisco Chronicle, scoring it 63, wrote that the film starts out feeling like a lukewarm cauldron of tired tropes, but by the end manages to conjure up something unexpectedly enchanting — a mixed verdict that still lands on the side of recommending it.

Several of the positive reviews picked up on something the negative ones largely ignored: the film's themes. The most generous readings describe Hexed as a nuanced take on classism and generational trauma, and as a surprisingly edgy young-adult fantasy rather than a standard family adventure. Those are not the words critics usually reach for with a Disney Thanksgiving release, and they suggest the film is trying to do something more ambitious than its familiar plot would suggest. Whether it succeeds at that ambition is precisely where the critics split.

What the Critics Did Not Love: Familiar Magic, Tired Tropes

The negative and mixed reviews are just as consistent as the positive ones, and they cluster around a single complaint: the film feels familiar. Not bad, not broken — familiar, in the way that a song you have heard a hundred times can be pleasant and forgettable at once.

Benjamin Lee of the Guardian gave Hexed just 2 out of 5, writing that there is no real joy or fizz in Billie's discovery of her powers. Tim Grierson of Screen International called it an animation about witches that lacks sufficient magic. Jacob Oller of the AV Club gave the film a C+, writing that even in its most enjoyable moments, Hexed is cursed to be original in name only — a line that stings precisely because originality is the film's entire selling point. Peter Sobczynski of RogerEbert.com gave it 2.5 out of 4, one of the kinder mixed takes, writing that the film often seems on the verge of becoming something intriguing but never quite makes that final leap, preferring to relapse into the familiar, particularly during the blandly chaotic finale.

Sarah Musnicky of But Why Tho? scored it 6 out of 10, writing that Disney's Hexed needed more time to brew a magic strong enough to make this otherwise safe tale memorable beyond its otherwise pretty witchy aesthetic. Matt Fernandez of Geeks of Color, also at 6 out of 10, called it an enjoyable movie, but still just okay, while respecting the originality behind the story and enjoying the four main characters. One review on Metacritic, scoring the film just 6, called it a beautiful and entertaining animated movie that feels like two films condensed into one.

The recurring words across the mixed reviews are "familiar", "safe", "by the numbers" and "blandly chaotic". The critics who liked the film and the critics who did not largely agree on what it is: a visually strong, emotionally sincere film with a plot they have seen before. They disagreed only on whether that is enough. For a family audience on Thanksgiving weekend, it very possibly is. For a $200 million original that Disney needs to turn into a franchise, it may not be. The early audience reactions posted on Rotten Tomatoes on Wednesday morning leaned positive — viewers calling it the animation comeback Disney needed, great family fun, and one of the best Disney movies they had seen — but those are the opening-night faithful, and opening-night audiences are usually kinder to Disney than critics are. The CinemaScore, the grade those audiences give the film, will arrive later this week, and it will be a better guide to word of mouth than either the critics' score or the first fan reactions.

The Story: Billie, the Bracelet and the Realm of Hexe

Hexed follows Billie Doe, an impulsive and unconventional teenage girl voiced by Hailee Steinfeld, who discovers she has secret magical abilities. The trigger is a bracelet: for years it has been holding her magic back, and when she loses it, her powers are unleashed all at once. The surge of magic sweeps her out of her suburban life and into Hexe, a vast realm of witches she never knew existed. Her mother Alice, voiced by Rashida Jones, is pulled along for the ride, and together mother and daughter embark on a magical adventure that uncovers long-hidden family secrets — secrets with the power to change the world of witches forever.

The hidden realm is ruled by Queen Celeste, voiced by Jodie Foster in what is reported to be her animation debut, a villain whose presence gives the story its stakes. Along for the journey is Beef, a cat voiced by Walton Goggins, who has emerged from the early reviews as the film's breakout character. The film runs 1 hour 43 minutes and is rated PG for action and some rude humour. It is directed by Jason Hand and Fawn Veerasunthorn, the latter a co-director of Moana 2, and it is Walt Disney Animation Studios' 65th animated feature.

The mother-daughter relationship is the film's centre of gravity, and it is the element the positive reviews keep returning to. Alice is described in the coverage as a cautious, Type A mother, a contrast to her impulsive daughter, and the story's emotional engine is the way the two of them are forced to understand each other once the magic strips away their ordinary lives. That is a familiar Disney structure — the parent and child who must learn to see each other clearly — but the reviews that praise the film argue that Steinfeld and Jones give it real feeling, and that the film's treatment of family secrets and generational trauma gives the familiar structure something genuine to say.

There is also a short film attached to the release. Hexed opens in theatres with the short "Lilo and Scratch", a piece of programming that ties the new original to one of Disney's most beloved recent characters and gives families one more reason to choose it on a crowded weekend. It is a small detail, but it is the kind of detail Disney uses to signal confidence in a release — and to give audiences a bonus that streaming at home cannot match.

The film's development, by most accounts, was bumpy. It was announced at Disney's D23 fan event in August 2025, and the concept art shown then differed noticeably from the final computer-animated look of the finished film. Director Jason Hand has publicly called some of the rumours around the production bizarre and not true. Nine days before the reviews landed, Disney's chief creative officer Jared Bush suggested in interviews that Hexed may be the last original in the classic Disney style, with the next original, a film called Clay, using computer animation designed to look like claymation. That comment, reported widely in the days before release, added an odd note to the launch: the suggestion that the studio is already moving past the style Hexed represents, before audiences have had a chance to judge it.

The Cast: Steinfeld Leads, Foster Turns Villain, Goggins Steals It

The voice cast is one of the strongest Disney has assembled for an original film in years, and it is built around four names that each bring a different audience with them.

Hailee Steinfeld voices Billie Doe, the lead. Steinfeld has been a Disney-adjacent star for years — she voices Gwen Stacy in the Spider-Verse films and played Kate Bishop in Marvel's Hawkeye — but Hexed is her first time leading a Walt Disney Animation feature, and the studio has leaned on her heavily in the marketing. She appeared on Live with Kelly and Mark on Thursday, November 5 to promote the film, and her cover of The Cars' song "Magic" arrived on October 23 as the first single from the Hexed soundtrack, her first music release of 2026. The promotion has been a big week for Steinfeld in other ways too: she and her husband, the NFL quarterback Josh Allen, married in May 2025 and announced the birth of their daughter Harper on April 2, 2026, and the Hexed launch has coincided with her first major public appearances since becoming a mother.

Rashida Jones voices Alice, Billie's mother. Jones is a veteran of both comedy and animation, and her casting gives the mother a warmth and comic timing that the reviews have singled out. The mother-daughter dynamic between Jones and Steinfeld is the relationship the film's best reviews praise most, and it is the reason the story's emotional beats land as well as they do.

Jodie Foster voices Queen Celeste, the villain who rules the hidden realm of Hexe. Foster's involvement is one of the film's biggest talking points: a two-time Academy Award winner, making what is reported to be her animation debut, as the antagonist of a Disney family film. The positive reviews credit her with giving the villain real menace, and her presence lends the film a prestige it would not otherwise have. For Foster, who has spent a career choosing live-action roles with care, the decision to voice a Disney villain is itself a statement about the film's ambitions.

Walton Goggins voices Beef, the cat. Goggins is the cast member the critics keep naming as the film's secret weapon, and the praise has been unusually specific: TheWrap's review said plainly that Goggins steals the show, and other positive notices have echoed the point. A scene-stealing animal sidekick is a Disney tradition, and Beef appears to be the film's most successful version of it in years — the character audiences are most likely to remember, and the one most likely to be quoted on the way out of the cinema.

The supporting cast and the crew behind the film matter too. Jason Hand and Fawn Veerasunthorn direct, with Veerasunthorn bringing the experience of co-directing Moana 2, one of the biggest animated hits of the decade. That pairing — a veteran of Disney's sequel machine directing an original — is part of the film's central tension: it is an original story made by the people who know best how to build a franchise, and the question is whether the franchise machinery helps the originality or smothers it.

The Money: A $200 Million Gamble and a $500 Million Break-Even

The budget is the number that makes everything else about Hexed feel high-stakes. According to trade reports carried by Koimoi, Hexed is one of the most expensive animated films Disney has ever made. The reported production cost is around $200 million — below Tangled's $260 million, which remains the most expensive animated film ever made, but on par with Wish, which also cost a reported $200 million. For an original story with no built-in audience, no pre-existing fans and no guaranteed opening weekend, $200 million is an enormous gamble.

The maths of that gamble is unforgiving. Based on the industry's standard 2.5-times multiplier rule — the rough formula that accounts for production cost plus global marketing plus the share of ticket revenue that goes to theatres — Hexed must earn an estimated $500 million at the worldwide box office just to break even and get out of the danger zone. It needs a lot more than $500 million to be considered profitable. For comparison, Wish finished its theatrical run far below that level, and Strange World did worse still. The last Disney Animation original to clear $500 million worldwide with ease was Encanto, and even that film needed a long tail and a streaming afterlife to become the cultural phenomenon it eventually became.

The $200 million figure also explains why the forecasts matter so much. If Hexed opens to $45 million to $50 million over five days, as the middle forecasts suggest, it would be on a pace that makes $500 million a serious stretch — possible with strong legs and a long holiday run, but far from assured. If it opens to $23 million, as Box Office Theory's estimate suggests, the break-even conversation is effectively over before the weekend ends, and the film's fate becomes a question of streaming and home viewing rather than theatrical profit. If it opens to $70 million, the top of the range, the conversation flips entirely, and Disney has a new franchise on its hands.

There is one piece of context that softens the picture slightly. Animated films, more than almost any other kind, earn money long after they leave theatres. Encanto is the obvious example: a modest theatrical run followed by a streaming afterlife that turned its songs into global hits and its characters into merchandise staples. Disney's calculation with Hexed is almost certainly not limited to the opening weekend, and the studio has the patience and the platform to let a film find its audience over months. But patience has limits, and a $200 million production cost is the kind of number that makes even Disney's executives want a strong opening.

The Forecasts: $23 Million to $70 Million — Why Nobody Agrees

The range of forecasts for Hexed is one of the widest for any major release this year, and the disagreement itself is informative. When the trackers agree, it usually means the signals are clear. When they disagree by $47 million, it means the signals are genuinely mixed.

At the top of the range sits Box Office Pro, at $50 million to $70 million over the five-day Thanksgiving weekend. That forecast, made back in October, assumes the film behaves like a classic Disney Thanksgiving release: strong family turnout, good word of mouth, and a holiday weekend that rewards films parents can take children to without hesitation. It also assumes the Hexed brand — a witch story with a teenage heroine, released at the start of the holiday season — will play well with the same audience that made Moana a $82 million five-day opener a decade ago.

In the middle sit Variety, at $40 million to $45 million, and the trade coverage that projects $45 million to $50 million and a second-place finish behind The Hunger Games. Those forecasts assume the film performs roughly in line with its reviews: good enough to draw families, not good enough to become an event. They also assume the competition eats into it, with The Hunger Games holding the top spot and Focker-in-Law splitting the adult comedy audience.

At the bottom sits Box Office Theory, at about $23 million over five days, a figure it dropped to on November 20 after tracking cooled. That forecast assumes the worst case: that the mixed reviews and the crowded weekend keep families away, that the film's marketing has not broken through, and that Hexed follows Strange World and Wish into the Thanksgiving basement rather than following Moana and Zootopia 2 into the record books. It is worth noting that Box Office Theory's $23 million would still be better than Strange World's $18.8 million five-day opening in 2022 — but only just, and for a film that cost $200 million, "slightly better than Strange World" is not a result anyone at Disney would celebrate.

The forecasts have been moving in one direction for weeks: down. In October, Box Office Pro's range topped out at $70 million. Deadline later had the film at $40 million to $50 million. By November 20, Box Office Theory had cut its estimate to $23 million. The reviews, which arrived on Tuesday, have not obviously reversed that slide — 68 percent fresh is better than the 57 percent the film briefly held, but it is not the kind of score that sends tracking upward. What could reverse it now is word of mouth: if opening-night audiences love the film and tell their friends, the weekend could build day by day in the way Thanksgiving weekends allow, with families choosing their film on Wednesday, Thursday and Friday rather than all at once.

The Thanksgiving Curse: Strange World, Wish and the Sequel Years

To understand why Hexed matters so much to Disney, you have to understand what Thanksgiving has meant for the studio over the last four years. The holiday weekend is Disney Animation's traditional showcase, the slot where the studio puts its biggest family film of the year. For two years running, that slot produced disasters. Then, for two years running, it produced billion-dollar hits. The difference between the two pairs of years was one word: originals versus sequels.

In 2022, Disney released Strange World, an original science-fiction adventure, into the Thanksgiving slot. It opened to $18.8 million over five days, one of the worst openings in the studio's modern history, and finished its run as one of Disney's biggest theatrical losses. A year later, Wish — the film made to celebrate the studio's 100th anniversary, with a reported $200 million budget — opened to $31.6 million over five days. Better than Strange World, but still a disappointment, and still a loss.

Then the sequels arrived. In 2024, Moana 2 took the Thanksgiving slot and became a billion-dollar hit. In 2025, Zootopia 2 did the same, topping $1 billion worldwide. The two Thanksgivings that followed the originals' failures belonged entirely to franchise films, and they restored the holiday's reputation as a reliable moneymaker — but they also raised an uncomfortable question inside Disney. If only sequels can open Thanksgiving, what happens to original animation? Hexed is the studio's answer to that question, and the answer will be written in its opening numbers.

The broader picture for original animation in 2026 has not been encouraging. Pixar's Hoppers, an original, took in $389 million worldwide against a $150 million budget — a respectable total that still counts as a disappointment against its cost, once marketing and the theatres' share are factored in. DreamWorks' Forgotten Island earned just $79.2 million worldwide despite strong reviews, a result that showed critical praise alone cannot save an original animated film that audiences do not discover. The trade press has taken to calling this the original-animation curse, and Hexed is the latest film asked to break it.

There is a counter-argument, and it is worth taking seriously. The original Moana, released in 2016, earned an unadjusted $82 million over its five-day Thanksgiving opening — a strong result for an original that had no pre-existing audience. Encanto, released in 2021, opened more modestly but became a phenomenon through streaming. Originals have worked on Thanksgiving before, and they have worked recently enough that nobody at Disney should have forgotten how. The question is whether Hexed, with its 68 percent score and its $23 million to $70 million forecast range, is the film to do it again.

The Competition: Hunger Games, Focker-in-Law and a Weak Holiday

Hexed does not open into an empty market. It opens into the most crowded Thanksgiving frame in years, against two other major releases and the second weekend of the biggest film of the month — and the overall market is expected to be soft.

The film to beat is The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, which opened to $120 million last weekend and now enters its second frame. Trade coverage expects it to hold the number-one spot again, with at least $50 million over the five-day Thanksgiving period. A second-weekend hold that strong would leave Hexed fighting for the same family audience from a position of clear second place, and it would cap the new film's ceiling regardless of how well it plays.

The other new release is Universal's comedy Focker-in-Law, which also opens today, November 25, with Ariana Grande in a prominent role. Its reviews are far worse than Hexed's — critics have it split at 26 percent — but it has a different audience, and trade coverage suggests a $40 million to $50 million Thanksgiving opening is on the table for it. If Focker-in-Law overperforms, it takes screens and attention from Hexed. If it underperforms, it may still split the adult audience that Hexed needs for its evening shows.

Brad Pitt's The Further Adventures of Cliff Booth also debuts on Thanksgiving Eve, adding a third new wide release to a weekend that already has a lot to choose from. Page Six, reporting on the holiday frame, quoted Boxoffice Pro's Daniel Loria saying plainly that Thanksgiving will see a big drop in box office revenue compared to the last two years, and that even Disney cannot conjure up a happy ending with Hexed. The trade press expects the overall weekend to fall below last year's $290 million total, and well short of the 2024 record of $423 million. The last two Thanksgivings were lifted by Wicked, Jon M. Chu's two-part adaptation, which drove the holiday to extraordinary figures for two consecutive years. This year there is no Wicked. There is a witch movie, a Hunger Games sequel and a comedy, and the market is expected to be smaller for all of them.

That context matters for Hexed because a soft overall weekend makes a soft opening look worse than it is, and because it limits the film's ability to grow through word of mouth. Thanksgiving is usually a weekend where a family film can build day by day, as different families go on different days. But if fewer families go to the cinema at all, the film that wins is the one they have already decided to see — and right now, more of them have decided to see The Hunger Games.

What Happens Next: Legs, Word of Mouth and the Streaming Afterlife

The opening weekend will not be the end of the Hexed story, whichever way it lands. Animated films live or die on their legs — the ability to keep earning week after week, long after the opening headlines fade — and Hexed has two things working in its favour on that front. The first is the calendar: after Thanksgiving, the Christmas season brings weeks of school holidays, and a family film with a 68 percent score and a PG rating is exactly the kind of film parents reach for when they need something to take the children to. The second is the score itself: 68 percent fresh is not a rave, but it is a recommendation, and for a family audience a recommendation is often enough.

The CinemaScore, which arrives later this week, will be the first real test of word of mouth. Opening-night audiences are usually kinder to Disney than critics are, and the early fan reactions posted on Wednesday morning leaned positive. If the CinemaScore comes in at an A or A-minus, the film has a real chance of holding well through December, and the $500 million break-even line, while still a stretch, becomes conceivable. If it comes in at a B or below, the film's theatrical run will likely be short, and the conversation will shift quickly to streaming.

The streaming afterlife is where Disney's calculation may ultimately be made. Encanto proved that a Disney animated film can be a modest theatrical performer and a massive cultural success, and Hexed has the ingredients for a similar second life: a young heroine, a catchy soundtrack single in Steinfeld's cover of "Magic", a memorable animal sidekick in Beef, and a story about mothers and daughters that plays just as well on a living-room television as it does on a cinema screen. Disney has the platform to give Hexed that second life, and the patience to wait for it. What the studio does not have is unlimited patience for $200 million originals that do not perform theatrically — and that is why the opening weekend, for all the talk of legs and streaming, still matters as much as it does.

In the UK, Hexed opens on Friday, November 27, two days after the North American launch, and the international rollout will continue from there. The global numbers, when they arrive, will tell the fuller story: whether the film's appeal travels, whether the witch story plays outside the United States, and whether the $200 million gamble was a reasonable one. For now, the American opening is the number everyone is watching, and it arrives today.

The Bottom Line

Hexed opens today as the most interesting test Disney Animation has set itself in years. It is a $200 million original, released into a soft Thanksgiving market, against the second weekend of a $120 million opener and a star-driven comedy, with reviews that climbed from rotten to fresh in a single evening and forecasts that disagree by tens of millions of dollars. If it opens at the top of the range, Disney has a new franchise and original animation has a future on Thanksgiving. If it opens at the bottom, the studio will have spent $200 million to confirm what the last four years have already suggested: that original animated films are the hardest sell in Hollywood, and that the audience that once turned out for them now waits for the sequel.

The most likely outcome sits in the middle, and it is the one the numbers already point to: a $40 million to $50 million five-day opening, a second-place finish, a 68 percent score that reads as a recommendation rather than a rave, and a long December in which word of mouth decides whether the film is a disappointment or a slow-burn success. That is not the result Disney dreamed of when it announced Hexed at D23 in August 2025. But it is a result the film can build on — if families go, if children love Beef the cat as much as the critics did, and if the magic, once unleashed, turns out to be enough.
