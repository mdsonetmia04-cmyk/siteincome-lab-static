TITLE: Dodgers Owner Todd Boehly Just Signed a $10 Billion Deal to Buy Russia's Lukoil Empire — With the US Government as His Partner LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Todd Boehly's consortium, backed by the US government, agreed to buy Lukoil's $16 billion international oil empire in a $10 billion share swap ---BODY---

Todd Boehly, chairman and CEO of Eldridge, at SXSW London in 2026. His consortium has signed a deal to buy Lukoil's international assets. Photo: DavidPMaynard via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

One of the strangest and most consequential energy deals of the decade was signed on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, and it involves a baseball owner, the United States government, a sanctioned Russian oil giant, and US$10 billion in shares that will sit in a bank account in the United Arab Emirates for five years. Todd Boehly, the American billionaire who owns the Los Angeles Dodgers and co-owns Chelsea Football Club, said on Wednesday that a consortium led by his investment firm, Eldridge, had signed a deal with Lukoil to acquire the sanctioned Russian oil company's international energy assets — a sprawling portfolio of oil fields, refineries, pipelines and petrol stations spread across 11 countries and valued at around US$16 billion. The consortium includes the US International Development Finance Corporation, the overseas investment arm of the American government, and it is a deal that, if it closes, will pull one of Russia's largest energy empires out of Moscow's hands and put it under American control.

The structure of the deal is, by design, unlike anything the oil industry has seen before. No cash is changing hands. Instead, the consortium will swap Lukoil securities worth US$10 billion — shares bought from American and British funds and from US depositary banks that held them for retail investors — for the assets held by Lukoil's Austrian unit, Lukoil International GmbH, which houses the company's entire overseas business. The swapped shares will be held in an escrow account in the United Arab Emirates, rather than going straight to Lukoil, and the Russian company will only be able to access them after five years, irrespective of whether US sanctions have been lifted. The deal still needs approval from the US Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control, and Boehly said that closing it could take up to a year. But the signing, after more than a year of negotiations, is, by any measure, a landmark: the first time the American government has joined a private consortium to buy a sanctioned Russian company's assets, and the largest transfer of Russian energy assets out of Moscow's control since the war in Ukraine began.

This is the story of that deal — of the assets at stake, of the people behind it, of the sanctions that made it necessary, and of the questions it raises about the role of the American government in the world's energy markets.

A US$16 billion empire, for sale by sanctions

Lukoil is, by any measure, one of the giants of the global oil industry. It is Russia's second-largest oil company, and, before the sanctions, it was one of the most international of Russia's energy firms, with operations that stretched from the Caspian Sea to the North Sea, from the deserts of Central Asia to the petrol stations of the American suburbs. Its international portfolio, which is the subject of Wednesday's deal, encompasses operations in 11 countries that produce around 350,000 barrels of oil per day, including major oilfields in Azerbaijan and Kazakhstan, refineries in the Netherlands, Bulgaria and Romania, and a network of service stations that includes some in the United States. Two people familiar with the terms of the deal valued the portfolio at about US$16 billion, although it had been valued at US$22 billion as recently as early November, and the Austrian subsidiary that houses the international operations said in its most recent annual report that its net worth stood at €19.5 billion (US$21.4 billion) at the end of 2024.

The portfolio has been, for more than a year, an asset in search of an owner. In October 2025, the United States added Lukoil and its fellow Russian producer Rosneft to its blacklist of sanctioned entities, a move that cut the company off from the Western financial system and left its international assets stranded — too valuable to abandon, too sanctioned to operate normally, and too entangled with the Russian state to be sold in the ordinary way. The sanctions were part of a broader American effort to choke off the revenues that fund Russia's war in Ukraine, and they were, in that sense, a success: Lukoil's international business, which had once been a source of hard currency for Moscow, became a liability, a sprawling empire that the company could neither run nor sell.

That is where Todd Boehly came in. Boehly, the chairman and chief executive of Eldridge, an asset management and insurance holding company, is best known to the public as the owner of the Los Angeles Dodgers and the co-owner of Chelsea Football Club, but he is, in the world of finance, a dealmaker of formidable reputation, and he has spent more than a year assembling the consortium that signed on Wednesday. In September 2026, the Financial Times reported that Boehly had put together a new group that included the US International Development Finance Corporation, and on Wednesday that group — Eldridge, the DFC, the US investment bank Xtellus Partners, and the UAE-based Catena Investment Holding — signed the agreement that, if it is approved, will transfer Lukoil's international empire to American hands.

The deal, in detail: US$10 billion, no cash, five years in escrow

The terms of the deal are, by the standards of the oil industry, extraordinary. The consortium will exchange Lukoil securities worth US$10 billion for the company's international assets, in a share swap in which no cash changes hands. The securities — shares in Lukoil itself, and in other Russian companies — were bought from US and British funds and from US depositary banks that held them for retail investors, and they had, in the words of one person familiar with the matter, effectively become worthless. Boehly said the deal would give American investors an opportunity to cash out shares that had, since the sanctions, been frozen and untradeable, and that, in that sense, the transaction was a rescue, not just for Lukoil's assets, but for the American and British investors who had been left holding Russian stock.

The shares, however, will not go to Lukoil. Instead, they will be held in an escrow account in the United Arab Emirates, outside the scope of US jurisdiction, and Lukoil will only be able to access them after five years, irrespective of whether US sanctions have been lifted. The structure was designed, in the words of one person familiar with the terms, to win approval from the Treasury Department, which has barred any windfall to the Russian company, and it is a structure that the Treasury had, in an earlier bid, rejected. That it has now been accepted, in a form that keeps the shares out of Moscow's hands for half a decade, is a measure of how far the negotiations have come, and of how determined the American government is to see the deal done.

The agreement provides for a 60-day exclusivity period in which the transaction is to be finalised, and it is still subject to approval from the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control, among other conditions. Boehly said that closing the deal could take up to a year, and that the venture would be renamed once the transaction is complete. The consortium, in a statement, said that the deal was structured "to stabilize the operations of energy assets in emerging and other markets, strengthen energy security, support reliable energy supply for Americans families and businesses, and return billions to American taxpayers." It is, in the consortium's telling, a deal that serves American interests on every front: it stabilises energy supplies, it strengthens American energy security, and it returns money to American taxpayers.

But the most striking feature of the deal is the role of the American government itself. The US International Development Finance Corporation, the DFC, will take a roughly 15 percent equity stake in Lukoil's international assets, without investing any capital up front — a first, it appears, for the agency, which was created by Congress in 2018 as a counterweight to China's Belt and Road Initiative, and which mobilises private capital for projects in mostly emerging markets that advance US foreign policy and development objectives. The DFC's stake will give the United States a share of future profits, after the consortium's private investors have recouped their capital and an agreed rate of return, as well as a role on the company's governing board. And, according to the New York Times, the US side — Boehly and the government together — would control a majority of the board. It is, in short, a deal in which the American government is not just a regulator, or a sanctioning authority, but a shareholder, a board member, and a partner in the ownership of a global oil empire.

Why the US government is buying an oil company

The DFC's participation is, for students of American foreign policy, the most significant part of the deal, and it is the part that raises the most questions. The agency was created, in 2018, to counter China's Belt and Road Initiative, by mobilising private capital for infrastructure and development projects in emerging markets that advance American foreign policy. It has, until now, invested in ports, in power plants, in telecommunications, and in other infrastructure, but it has never, until now, taken a stake in an oil company, and it has never done so without putting up capital. The Lukoil deal is, in that sense, a departure, and it is one that reflects a broader shift in American policy: a willingness, under the Trump administration, to use the tools of the state — sanctions, licences, and now direct equity participation — to reshape the global energy market.

For the Trump administration, the deal is, by the account of the Center for Strategic and International Studies, a key foreign policy win. It would pull a roughly US$20 billion portfolio of refineries, oil fields and petrol stations out of Russian hands, and it would do so in a way that, the administration argues, serves American interests: it stabilises energy supplies in emerging markets, it strengthens energy security, and it returns money to American taxpayers. It is, in the administration's telling, a deal that turns sanctions from a punishment into a prize, and that uses the leverage of the American financial system to acquire, at a discount, assets that would otherwise remain in the hands of a sanctioned Russian company.

But the deal also raises questions that go beyond the immediate politics. The first is about oversight. Under American law, the DFC is required to officially notify projects to Congress after providing support in excess of US$20 million. Because of the profits-interest structure of the Lukoil deal, the DFC will not be putting any appropriated funds into the transaction, and the agency's lawyers will likely contend that the transaction does not need to be officially notified to Congress. Critics will counter that the law covers any "financial commitment," not just cash outlays, and that the DFC is receiving a multibillion-dollar equity stake in exchange for the US government's participation and approval role — consideration that can be valued, and that should, in their view, be subject to congressional scrutiny. The transaction structure, the Center for Strategic and International Studies noted, "will make it harder for Congress to exercise any oversight," and that is a concern that is likely to be raised, forcefully, in the weeks ahead.

The second question is about the precedent. If the American government can take a 15 percent stake in a sanctioned Russian oil company's assets, without putting up capital, and without notifying Congress, then what is to stop it from doing the same with other sanctioned assets, in other industries, in other countries? The Lukoil deal is, in that sense, not just a transaction. It is a template, and it is one that will be studied, and copied, by governments and by dealmakers around the world. For the Trump administration, that is, presumably, the point. For its critics, it is a cause for concern.

Twelve licences, one deadline, and a year of negotiations

The deal did not happen overnight. It is the product of more than a year of negotiations, conducted under a licence from the US Treasury Department that has been extended 12 times, and that is set to expire on December 19, 2026. The latest extension, issued on November 19, allows negotiations to continue until that date, and it is, in that sense, a deadline that has concentrated minds. The consortium's signing on November 25, six days after the latest extension, and less than a month before the licence expires, is not a coincidence. It is the result of a negotiation that has been conducted, for more than a year, under the constant pressure of a clock that keeps running out.

The negotiations were, by every account, difficult. The Treasury Department had, in an earlier bid, rejected the share-swap structure that is now at the heart of the deal, and it was only after the State Department threw its support behind the transaction, and after the escrow arrangement in the UAE was agreed, that the deal became viable. The consortium, for its part, had to assemble a group of investors willing to take on the risk of acquiring the assets of a sanctioned Russian company, and to do so in a structure in which no cash would change hands, and in which the closing could take up to a year. That it succeeded is a testament to Boehly's persistence, and to the determination of the American government to see the assets out of Russian hands.

There is also a human dimension to the negotiation that is easy to overlook. The Lukoil securities that are being swapped for the assets were bought from American and British funds, and from US depositary banks that held them for retail investors — ordinary people who had, in many cases, held Russian shares in their pension funds and their investment accounts, and who had, since the sanctions, been unable to sell them. For those investors, the deal is a lifeline, a chance to recover something from shares that had, in the words of one person familiar with the matter, "effectively become worthless." Boehly said the deal would give American investors an opportunity to cash out, and that, in itself, is a significant part of its appeal. It is a deal that, in the consortium's telling, serves not just the American government, or the American taxpayer, but the American investor, too.

What the assets are worth — and what they could become

The value of the assets at the heart of the deal is, like so much about it, a matter of dispute. Two people familiar with the terms valued the portfolio at about US$16 billion. In early November, Reuters reported that the assets had initially been valued at US$22 billion, and the Austrian subsidiary that houses them said in its most recent annual report that its net worth stood at €19.5 billion, or US$21.4 billion, at the end of 2024. The difference between those figures — US$16 billion, US$22 billion, US$21.4 billion — is, in part, a reflection of the uncertainty that surrounds any valuation of a sanctioned company's assets, and in part a reflection of the discount that the consortium is, in effect, paying. If the portfolio is worth US$16 billion, and the consortium is paying US$10 billion in shares that will not reach Lukoil for five years, then the deal is, by any measure, a bargain, and it is a bargain that reflects the leverage that sanctions give to the buyer.

But the assets are also, in a longer sense, worth more than their current valuation suggests. The portfolio includes oil and gas fields, refineries, pipelines and downstream operations spread across multiple countries in Western and Southeast Europe, the Middle East, Central Asia, North and sub-Saharan Africa, and North America. It includes major oilfields in Azerbaijan and Kazakhstan, which are, by any measure, among the most productive in the world. It includes refineries in the Netherlands, Bulgaria and Romania, which supply fuel to millions of Europeans. And it includes a network of service stations, some of them in the United States, that sell petrol to American drivers. It is, in short, a global energy business, and it is one that, under new ownership, and freed from the constraints of sanctions, could be worth considerably more than the US$16 billion at which it is currently valued.

That is, presumably, the calculation that Boehly and his partners have made. They are not buying a distressed asset at a discount. They are buying a global energy business at a price that reflects the sanctions that have been imposed on its current owner, and they are betting that, under American ownership, and with the support of the American government, that business will be worth far more than they are paying for it. It is a bet that, if it pays off, will make them very rich, and it is a bet that, if it does not, will leave them holding assets that are, in the words of one observer, "a sprawling portfolio" of oil fields, refineries and petrol stations in some of the most politically volatile regions of the world.

Boehly, the Dodgers, and the dealmaker

Todd Boehly is, to most Americans, a sports owner. He is the owner of the Los Angeles Dodgers, one of the most storied franchises in baseball, and the co-owner of Chelsea Football Club, one of the most famous teams in English football. But he is, in the world of finance, something more: the chairman and chief executive of Eldridge, an asset management and insurance holding company that he founded, and a dealmaker who has, over the past decade, built a reputation for assembling complex transactions in industries that others find too difficult, or too controversial, to touch.

The Lukoil deal is, in that sense, characteristic of Boehly's approach. It is complex, it is controversial, and it involves a level of political risk that most dealmakers would avoid. It requires the cooperation of the American government, the approval of the Treasury Department, the participation of an Emirati investment firm, and the acquiescence of a sanctioned Russian company. It involves a share swap in which no cash changes hands, an escrow account in the UAE, and a closing that could take a year. And it is a deal that, if it succeeds, will give Boehly and his partners control of a global oil empire, and a seat at the table of the world's energy markets.

Boehly has declined to detail the equity stakes of the four consortium partners, and he has said that the venture will be renamed once the deal is complete. But he has been clear about the ambition behind it. The deal, he has said, is about more than the assets themselves. It is about the opportunity to build a global energy company, under American ownership, with the support of the American government, and to do so at a moment when the world's energy markets are being reshaped by war, by sanctions, and by the transition to cleaner fuels. It is, in that sense, a bet not just on Lukoil's assets, but on the future of energy itself.

The questions that remain

For all the excitement that the deal has generated, there are, inevitably, questions that remain unanswered, and they are questions that will shape its fate in the months ahead. The first, and the most immediate, is whether the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control will approve it. The agreement is, by its own terms, subject to OFAC approval, and the Treasury, which sanctioned Lukoil in October 2025, will review the deal to ensure that American national security is safeguarded. A source familiar with the process told Reuters that the Treasury would conduct that review, and that it would be, in the nature of such reviews, thorough. The escrow arrangement, which keeps the shares out of Lukoil's hands for five years, was designed to win that approval, but it is not a guarantee of it.

The second question is about Congress. The DFC's participation in the deal, without the investment of appropriated funds, and without, in the agency's view, the need for formal notification, is likely to be challenged, and it is likely to be challenged forcefully. The law, as the Center for Strategic and International Studies noted, requires the DFC to notify Congress of projects in which it provides support in excess of US$20 million, and critics will argue that a multibillion-dollar equity stake, received in exchange for the government's participation, is a "financial commitment" within the meaning of the law. If Congress demands a say in the deal, and if it does so before the closing, the timetable — already a year, by Boehly's own account — could stretch further, and the deal could become a political football in a way that it has not yet been.

The third question is about Russia. Lukoil has signed the deal, but it is a sanctioned company, and its ability to honour its commitments — and, in five years' time, to collect the shares that are sitting in escrow in the UAE — is uncertain. The shares will be held in a blocked account, outside the scope of US jurisdiction, and Lukoil will be able to access them after five years, irrespective of whether sanctions have been lifted. But five years is a long time in the world of sanctions, and in the world of Russian politics, and it is far from certain that the company that signed the deal will be the company that collects the shares. The deal, in that sense, is a bet not just on the assets, but on the future of Russia itself.

And there is a fourth question, one that goes beyond the deal itself, and that concerns the role of the American government in the world's energy markets. The DFC's participation in the Lukoil deal is, as noted, a first, and it is one that sets a precedent. If the American government can take a stake in a sanctioned Russian oil company, then it can, in principle, take a stake in any sanctioned asset, in any industry, in any country. That is a power that, once exercised, is difficult to un-exercise, and it is a power that will, in the years ahead, be used, and debated, and challenged, in ways that are, at present, impossible to predict. The Lukoil deal is, in that sense, not just a transaction. It is a turning point, and it is one whose consequences will be felt long after the closing, whenever that may be.

By the numbers: the Lukoil deal

Those are the numbers, and they are the story of a deal in which a baseball owner, the American government, and a sanctioned Russian oil giant have, together, redrawn the map of the world's energy markets — and of a transaction whose consequences, for American foreign policy, for the oil industry, and for the future of sanctions themselves, will be felt for years to come.
