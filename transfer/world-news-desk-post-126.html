Nvidia's most powerful artificial intelligence chips — the ones the United States has banned from China — have turned up in Shanghai anyway. Bloomberg reported on Thursday, 27 November 2026, that a pair of Chinese firms installed Nvidia Blackwell semiconductors at a data centre in the city, in documents that provide the first public evidence that the restricted hardware reached a site linked to Alice Huang, the businesswoman at the heart of the scandal that has shaken the world's most valuable company.

The evidence is not a rumour, a satellite photograph or an anonymous tip. It is in loan paperwork filed with Chinese regulators. According to documents reviewed by Bloomberg, Shanghai Shuoyao offered a GB300 NVL72 rack — among Nvidia's most powerful products, containing 72 Blackwell Ultra chips — as collateral to obtain a 96 million yuan loan, about $14 million, from Shanghai Innovation Bank. The filing included the rack's serial number. Bloomberg typed that serial number into a public warranty database and found that the rack was manufactured by Taiwan's Gigabyte Technology, which assembles Nvidia chips into servers and racks. Gigabyte declined to tell Bloomberg who it originally sold the hardware to, or when.

A second firm, Shanghai Kezhan, described a GB200 server — which also relies on Blackwell chips — at the same facility, in paperwork for a 90 million yuan loan it obtained in late 2025 from a finance-leasing firm. Kezhan's majority owner is a Tokyo-based firm that the Chinese outlet Caixin has reported is controlled by Alice Huang. The site is the Yangtze River Delta Project, a Shanghai data centre that local media have described as a facility meant to house as many as 800,000 servers. Megaspeed, the Singapore-based cloud company Alice Huang founded, used a rendering of the project in 2024 to describe its largest data centre to investors, calling the site a "specific area" facility in an unnamed country.

Blackwell processors have always been illegal to ship to China without a United States licence, and President Donald Trump has explicitly said he does not want them going there, at least for now. The documents do not establish how the equipment reached the site, or whether any shipment received approval. But they establish something that, until now, had only been suspected: the chips are there.

The market noticed instantly. Nvidia's stock erased its gains and turned negative on the day after Bloomberg reported the finding, according to Times Now. For a company whose shares have swung wildly for weeks on every twist in the Huang saga, the discovery that its most advanced hardware is sitting in a Shanghai data centre tied to the woman at the centre of the scandal is not a footnote. It is the story.

The woman at the centre of it all

Alice Huang is, by now, one of the most consequential figures in the global technology industry — and one of the most mysterious. In 2023 she founded Megaspeed, a so-called neocloud company, and within four days of its creation Nvidia gave the new firm a coveted stamp of approval. Megaspeed went on to become Nvidia's largest customer in Southeast Asia. That rise, from nothing to Nvidia's biggest regional customer in a matter of months, is itself one of the strangest facts in the whole affair.

According to reporting by Bloomberg and others, United States intelligence suspects Alice Huang of working with Chinese intelligence during an affair with Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang. The scandal briefly cost Huang his job. He was reinstated as chief executive after an 11-day exile, and returned to Nvidia's headquarters on Monday, 23 November 2026 — the return that this newspaper covered in post 02, when the company's board brought him back after the internal investigation. But the questions that drove him out have not gone away, and Thursday's report is the most concrete evidence yet that the diversion of Nvidia hardware to China was real.

There are important limits to what the new evidence proves, and they matter. Bloomberg said it had no evidence that Megaspeed itself diverted hardware to the Shanghai site, and Megaspeed has denied wrongdoing in response to the United States probe. Shanghai Shuoyao, the company that procured the GB300 rack and offered it as collateral, has significant overlap with Megaspeed and is active at the data centre, and it confirmed this month, in a rare public statement, that it is one of Megaspeed's service providers, describing the partnership as "limited to ordinary commercial cooperation". But overlap is not proof of diversion, and a service-provider relationship is not proof that Megaspeed moved the chips.

There is also a counterweight from the United States government itself. According to a report cited by the South China Morning Post, the United States has found no evidence that China told Alice Huang to spy on Nvidia's chief executive. That finding does not clear the hardware question — the chips are in Shanghai either way — but it does complicate the simplest version of the story, in which a Chinese agent seduced a chief executive and stole his company's secrets. The reality, as so often in this affair, is murkier than either side's version.

What is not in doubt is that the United States is investigating. Megaspeed is under a United States probe over concerns that it smuggled Nvidia chips to China, Bloomberg has reported. And on 23 November, the Commerce Department blacklisted seven entities in Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand, Megaspeed among them, recovering none of the chips already moved. The blacklist is the administrative answer to a problem the government has not been able to solve at the border: the chips keep getting through.

Too big to smuggle? The claim that collapsed

Jensen Huang said in 2025 that he saw no evidence of artificial intelligence chip diversion into China, and argued that the systems are too big to smuggle. The claim was always a strange one. A GB300 NVL72 rack is a large piece of equipment — a cabinet-scale system, not something slipped into a suitcase. But large equipment moves around the world every day, in shipping containers, on cargo aircraft, through ports where paperwork is the only thing that distinguishes a legal shipment from an illegal one. The question was never whether a rack could physically fit in a container. It was whether the paperwork, the intermediaries and the shell companies could be arranged to get it past the controls. Thursday's report suggests that, for at least two racks, they could.

The loan documents are revealing precisely because they were never meant to be evidence of smuggling. They were meant to be evidence of collateral. Shanghai Shuoyao needed a loan, and it offered the GB300 rack as security. To do that, it had to list the rack's serial number in a filing with Chinese regulators. That serial number is what allowed Bloomberg to trace the hardware back to Gigabyte's factory in Taiwan. The same pattern appears with the second firm: Shanghai Kezhan described its GB200 server in paperwork for a loan from a leasing firm. And there is more. Shanghai Longjie, which shares a parent company with Shuoyao, reported servers with Hopper-generation chips from Super Micro Computer and Dell Technologies, and used them to secure a 200 million yuan loan from Shanghai Innovation Bank. Hopper is the generation before Blackwell, and it is also restricted.

The filings, Bloomberg noted, reveal for the first time that Nvidia's Blackwell processors found their way into a Chinese data centre with ties to Alice Huang and Megaspeed. But they also reveal something else: how much of the picture is still hidden. The documents only cover hardware offered as collateral for loans. They provide a small snapshot of the operators' ambitions, while also underscoring how some Chinese financial institutions, including those with government ties, were aware that restricted hardware was getting into China and were willing to lend capital on those assets. A data centre described as housing as many as 800,000 servers would need vastly more hardware than the handful of racks named in loan filings. What the filings show is the tip of something much larger — and the size of the rest is unknown.

Bloomberg was unable to reach either the leasing company that lent money to Shanghai Kezhan or Shanghai Innovation Bank, which, in addition to the money it lent Shanghai Shuoyao, also provided the 200 million yuan loan secured by Hopper servers. Nvidia did not respond to Bloomberg's questions. The silence from all sides is, in its own way, informative. Nobody in the chain — not the chipmaker, not the bank, not the leasing firm — wants to explain how the most restricted hardware in the world ended up in Shanghai.

The crackdown that came too late

The discovery lands in the middle of a sweeping American crackdown on chip diversion, and it raises an uncomfortable question about whether that crackdown is working. On 16 and 19 November, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security tightened export controls to block Chinese companies from renting artificial intelligence computing power through foreign cloud providers. Nvidia then told its manufacturing partners, including Dell and Supermicro, to stop shipping servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia — the countries through which, American officials believe, chips have been diverted to China. That story, which this newspaper covered in post 111, was presented as a decisive move. But the Shanghai filings show that the hardware was already there.

The timeline matters. The GB300 rack's serial number appears in documents submitted to Chinese regulators in November 2026 — the same month the new controls were announced. The GB200 server was described in paperwork for a loan obtained in late 2025. In other words, the diversion was not a response to the crackdown. It was already under way long before the crackdown began, and it continued while the crackdown was being designed. The controls, however tough, are chasing a problem that has a head start measured in months, if not years.

The human cost of the crackdown is also becoming visible. When Nvidia froze shipments to four Southeast Asian countries, it did not only hurt suspected diverters. It hit legitimate cloud providers, loan suspensions followed for some of them, and markets across Asia gyrated. The BBC reported that Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia caused volatility in Asian markets, with TSMC and Samsung highlighted as critical artificial intelligence infrastructure players. The chip war is no longer a narrow question of export licences. It is a question that moves stock markets from Taipei to Seoul to Tokyo, and it is a question that has now cost Nvidia's chief executive his job, briefly, and may yet cost him more.

There is also the question of what the United States does next. President Trump, according to Decrypt, said he could still take a government stake in Nvidia — a suggestion that would have been unthinkable a year ago and is now, apparently, on the table. The idea of the United States government owning a piece of the world's most valuable chipmaker, as a way of controlling where its chips go, tells you how seriously Washington now takes the diversion problem. It also tells you how far the normal tools — licences, blacklists, export controls — are perceived to have failed. If the chips can be found in Shanghai despite all of those tools, then perhaps, the thinking goes, the government needs to own the company that makes them.

That is a radical idea, and it is far from settled policy. But it is a measure of the moment. The United States spent years building a wall of export controls around China's access to advanced chips. On Thursday, the world learned that the wall has a hole in it, and that the hole is big enough for a GB300 NVL72 rack.

What happens now

The immediate question is what the United States does with the new evidence. The Commerce Department has already blacklisted Megaspeed and six other entities. It could go further, adding Shanghai Shuoyao, Shanghai Kezhan and Shanghai Longjie to the entity list, and pressuring the banks that lent against the restricted hardware. It could also tighten the controls further, extending the shipment freeze to more countries, or requiring more intrusive tracking of where Nvidia's most powerful systems end up. Each step carries a cost: the more countries are frozen, the more legitimate business is disrupted, and the more the American chip industry's customers look for alternatives.

For Nvidia, the problem is existential in a way that goes beyond any single shipment. The company's business model depends on selling its most advanced chips to the largest cloud providers in the world, many of them in Asia. If the United States government concludes that Nvidia cannot control where its chips end up, the government may decide to control it for them — through tighter licences, through a government stake, or through something else entirely. Jensen Huang's argument, that the systems are too big to smuggle, has now been tested against the evidence, and the evidence says otherwise. His return as chief executive was supposed to close the chapter. Thursday's report suggests the chapter is still being written.

For China, the discovery is a demonstration — and a warning. It is a demonstration that, despite the most extensive export-control regime in modern history, the country can still obtain the hardware it needs to train frontier artificial intelligence models. It is a warning because the discovery was made public, and because the publicity itself may be the point: the United States now has a concrete, documented case of diversion, with serial numbers, loan filings and a manufacturer's name attached. That is the kind of evidence that turns a suspicion into a sanctions package.

And for Alice Huang, the woman at the centre of it all, the report changes nothing and everything. It does not prove that she diverted the chips. It does not prove that she spied for China. But it places the most restricted hardware in the world at a data centre tied to her, at a company she founded, in a project her company showed to investors — and it does so with documents, not allegations. The affair that briefly cost Jensen Huang his job was always a story about people. Thursday's report made it a story about hardware, with a serial number, a loan and a bank. That is a much harder story to dismiss.

The chips are in Shanghai. The question now is what the world does about it.

The chips that cannot be bought — and the price of getting them

To understand why a single server rack in Shanghai matters so much, it helps to understand what a GB300 NVL72 actually is. It is not a graphics card. It is not a computer. It is a cabinet-scale system that packs 72 Blackwell Ultra graphics processors and 36 central processors into a single rack, linked together by Nvidia's fastest interconnects, designed to train the largest artificial intelligence models in the world. A single rack can cost several million dollars. A data centre full of them can cost billions. And the models they train — the frontier systems built by OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and their rivals — are the most strategically valuable software on the planet.

That is why the United States banned them from China. The export controls are not about consumer electronics or video games. They are about the raw computing power needed to build artificial intelligence that can design weapons, break codes, run cyberattacks and out-think an adversary. Blackwell is the current frontier: the chip that every major lab wants, and the chip that Washington has decided China must not have. President Trump has said explicitly that he does not want Blackwell chips shipped to China, at least for now — a position that puts the administration at odds with parts of the technology industry, which would happily sell to the world's second-largest economy, and in line with the national security establishment, which views every Blackwell chip in China as a chip that could be used against the United States.

The loan documents show what that hardware is worth on the grey market. Shanghai Shuoyao borrowed 96 million yuan — about $14 million — against a single GB300 rack. Shanghai Kezhan borrowed 90 million yuan against a GB200 server. Shanghai Longjie borrowed 200 million yuan against Hopper-generation servers from Supermicro and Dell. Add them up and the three firms raised roughly 386 million yuan, more than $50 million, using restricted American hardware as collateral. The banks that lent the money — Shanghai Innovation Bank and a leasing firm owned by a Shanghai government agency — knew what the collateral was. They lent anyway. The filings, as Bloomberg put it, underscore how some Chinese financial institutions, including those with government ties, were aware that restricted hardware was getting into China and were willing to lend capital on those assets.

There is a bitter irony in the financing. The same hardware that the United States government spent years trying to keep out of China was used, inside China, to raise capital from Chinese banks — capital that can be used to buy more hardware. It is a self-reinforcing loop: the chips arrive, they are pledged as collateral, the loans fund the next purchase, and the next purchase brings more chips. The export controls were supposed to starve China's artificial intelligence industry of computing power. Instead, the filings suggest, the industry found a way to turn the restricted hardware into a financial asset, and to use that asset to grow.

The market has already begun to price in the consequences. Moody's cut Nvidia's outlook to stable, according to the Century Report, as Citi and JPMorgan shopped artificial intelligence loan portfolios and Compass reportedly delayed a $380 million data-centre bond after an outage. The artificial intelligence build-out — the trillion-dollar construction boom in data centres, power plants and chips that has driven the stock market for three years — depends on a fragile chain of credit, and the discovery that the most valuable hardware in that chain is leaking to China is the kind of news that makes lenders nervous. If the chips cannot be controlled, the loans that finance them cannot be priced. And if the loans cannot be priced, the boom slows down.

A scandal with a body count of careers

The Blackwell discovery is the latest chapter in a scandal that has already claimed careers, moved markets and forced the world's most valuable company to change its chief executive — twice. Jensen Huang's 11-day exile in November, and his return on Monday, 23 November, was presented by Nvidia's board as a resolution. The internal investigation was complete, the chief executive was back, and the company could move on. But the questions that drove him out have not been answered, and Thursday's report makes them harder to ignore.

The first question is the simplest: how did the chips get to Shanghai? The filings do not answer it. They show the hardware at the destination, not the route it took. Was it shipped through Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand or Indonesia — the countries Nvidia has now frozen? Was it routed through a third country, re-labelled, re-packaged and re-exported? Was it bought by a legitimate customer and resold? Gigabyte, which manufactured the GB300 rack, declined to say who it originally sold the hardware to. Until someone in the chain talks, the route remains a mystery — and the mystery is itself a problem, because a route that worked once can work again.

The second question is about accountability. Megaspeed has denied wrongdoing. Nvidia has not responded to Bloomberg's questions. The banks have not responded either. Nobody has been charged, nobody has been named as a smuggler, and nobody has explained how the most restricted hardware in the world crossed the most watched border in the world. The Commerce Department has blacklisted seven entities, but blacklists are a blunt instrument: they punish companies that are already known, and they do nothing about the networks that have not been found yet. The Shanghai filings were discovered because a company needed a loan and filed paperwork. How many racks were moved by companies that never needed a loan?

The third question is about Jensen Huang himself. His return to Nvidia was supposed to be the end of the story — the founder restored, the company stabilised, the market reassured. But the return happened before the Blackwell discovery, and the discovery changes the context of everything that came before it. Huang said in 2025 that he saw no evidence of diversion and that the systems were too big to smuggle. The evidence now says otherwise. Whether he knew, or should have known, or was misled by the people around him, is a question that Nvidia's board will have to answer — and a question that the United States government, which is still investigating, may answer for them.
