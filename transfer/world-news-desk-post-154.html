TITLE: The Supreme Court Is About to Decide the AR-15's Fate — 40 Million Rifles, 11 State Bans, 1 Hour of Arguments LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: On Wednesday, December 2, the Supreme Court hears two cases on whether states may ban AR-15-style rifles — the biggest gun case in years. ---BODY---

The United States Supreme Court building in Washington, D.C. On Wednesday, 2 December, the nine justices hear one hour of arguments on whether states and cities may ban AR-15-style rifles. Photo: Library of Congress, Carol M. Highsmith (public domain).

On Wednesday morning, 2 December, the nine justices of the United States Supreme Court will take up a question they have avoided for years: can a state or a city ban the AR-15? It is the first time the Court has agreed to hear a direct challenge to bans on AR-15-style rifles — the semi-automatic rifles at the centre of America's gun debate — and the answer will reach far beyond the two cases on the docket. Eleven states have approved bans on these rifles. About 40 million of them are in private hands. Roughly 26 million Americans own one. And in a single hour of argument, starting at 10 a.m., the Court will begin to decide whether any of those bans can survive.

The cases arrive together. One, Khaya v. Cook County (No. 25-238), challenges the ban on assault weapons that Cook County, Illinois — which includes Chicago — passed in the 1990s. The other, Grant v. Higgins (No. 25-566), challenges Connecticut's ban, passed in the aftermath of the Sandy Hook Elementary School shooting. On 30 June 2026, the Court agreed to hear both cases together, for one hour of argument on a single question: do the Second and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee the right to possess AR-15-platform and similar semi-automatic rifles? A ruling is expected by the end of the Court's term, in late June or early July 2027. Nothing changes on the day of the arguments. But by the time the justices leave the bench on Wednesday, both sides will have a much better sense of where the Court is headed — and so will the rest of the country.

Fourteen years after a lone gunman carried out the deadliest elementary school shooting in American history, the Supreme Court is turning to a bitter legal fight over whether communities may outlaw the type of weapon used in that massacre. On 14 December 2012, a gunman killed 20 first graders and six educators at Sandy Hook Elementary School in Newtown, Connecticut, with a semi-automatic rifle. Connecticut's ban followed within months. The Cook County ban is older, and the Chicago ban it covers is older still. Both were upheld by the lower courts — the Second Circuit in Connecticut's case, the Seventh Circuit in Cook County's — and both are now before a Court with a 6-3 conservative majority that has, in recent years, expanded gun rights further than any Court in modern history. The stakes, for both sides, could hardly be higher.

The two cases

The Illinois case began in Cook County, which banned what it called assault weapons in the 1990s — a ban that covers Chicago, America's third-largest city, and that restricts AR-15s, AK-47-pattern rifles and magazines holding more than 10 rounds, with violations carrying up to six months in prison and a $5,000 minimum fine. Gun-rights groups, led by the Second Amendment Foundation and the Firearms Policy Coalition, challenged the ban, arguing that it violates the Second Amendment right to keep and bear arms. The Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals upheld the ban, and the gun-rights groups asked the Supreme Court to step in.

The Connecticut case, Grant v. Higgins, challenges a law passed after Sandy Hook that makes it a felony to possess semi-automatic rifles — including the AR-15 — acquired after 3 April 2013. The law was part of a package of gun measures Connecticut adopted in the months after the shooting, and it has been defended ever since as the state's direct response to the deadliest school shooting in American history. The Second Circuit upheld it, and the challengers — individual gun owners, the Firearms Policy Coalition, the Second Amendment Foundation and the Connecticut Citizens Defense League — asked the Supreme Court to strike it down.

The Court granted both cases on 30 June 2026, and consolidated them for one hour of argument on a single question: whether the Second and Fourteenth Amendments protect AR-15-platform and similar semi-automatic rifles in common use. It is the first rifle-ban case the Court has agreed to hear since it declined a similar case from Maryland, Snope v. Brown, in 2025 — a decision that left the bans in place but told the gun-rights movement, in effect, to come back with a better case. They have come back, and this time the Court said yes.

There is a complication in the Illinois case, and it is one the justices will have to deal with before they reach the merits. After the Court granted review, the lead petitioner in the Cook County case, Cutberto Viramontes, asked to be dismissed from the case under the Court's Rule 46.2. Cook County then argued that without Viramontes, it was doubtful that any remaining petitioner had standing to litigate the question, and asked the Court to dismiss the case as moot or as improvidently granted. The challengers opposed that request. On 23 November, the Court granted Viramontes's request to withdraw — the case was renamed Khaya v. Cook County — but deferred the mootness question itself, saying it would deal with that at oral argument. The case number and the 2 December date did not change. Connecticut's case, Grant v. Higgins, is not part of the mootness request, and it will be heard whatever happens to the Illinois case. But the mootness fight matters, because if the Court dismisses the Cook County case, the Connecticut case alone would decide the fate of the bans — and the Cook County ban, which covers Chicago, would survive by default, at least for now.

The "common use" argument

The gun-rights groups have a simple core argument, and it is one that turns on a single phrase from the Supreme Court's own decisions: "in common use." In District of Columbia v. Heller, the 2008 decision that first recognised an individual right to own a gun, the Court said that the Second Amendment protects arms that are "in common use" for lawful purposes, and that it does not protect arms that are "dangerous and unusual." The challengers' argument is that the AR-15 is, by any measure, in common use — and that a ban on a weapon owned by tens of millions of law-abiding Americans is therefore unconstitutional.

The numbers they cite are striking. A new national survey, the 2026 National Firearms Survey conducted by Georgetown University Professor William English and published on 24 August 2026, found that Americans own approximately 40 million AR-15-type rifles, with another 20 million similarly styled semi-automatic rifles — about 60 million rifles in total that fall within the class of weapons the bans target. The survey, which polled 51,398 American adults and identified 16,688 validated gun owners across all 50 states and the District of Columbia in July and August 2026, found that 25 percent of gun owners own an AR-15-type rifle specifically, and that 30 percent — approximately 26.4 million adults — own at least one AR-15 or similar semi-automatic rifle. About 22.1 million adults own an AR-15 specifically. The petitioners' brief relies on those findings repeatedly, telling the Court that the latest available data indicate approximately 26 million Americans own at least one covered rifle and approximately 22 million own an AR-15 specifically.

The challengers use the phrase "common use" 56 times in their main brief, according to CNN's review of the filings — a measure of how central the argument is to their case. They describe the AR-15 as the "paradigmatic American rifle." And they have a helpful line from the Court itself: in a unanimous opinion last year, the Court noted that the weapons are "widely legal," purchased by "many ordinary consumers," and that the AR-15 "is the most popular rifle in the country." That opinion, in a case about whether Mexico could sue American gun manufacturers, was not about bans — but the challengers will quote it on Wednesday as evidence that the Court has already recognised the rifle's place in American life.

The states and Cook County answer that popularity is not the test, and that the AR-15's military pedigree is. The AR-15 is the civilian version of the M16, the rifle carried by American soldiers since Vietnam, and the bans' defenders argue that it is, in the words of the lower courts that upheld them, a weapon of war that was designed for the battlefield and has no place on the streets of Chicago or the towns of Connecticut. They point to the mass shootings in which AR-15-style rifles have been used — Sandy Hook, Parkland, Uvalde, Buffalo, Las Vegas — and argue that the bans are a reasonable response to a weapon that has become the instrument of choice for mass killers. And they lean on the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen, which set the modern test for gun laws: if the Second Amendment's text covers the conduct, the government must justify its regulation by showing a historical analogue consistent with the nation's tradition of firearm regulation. The bans' defenders argue that there is such a tradition — that governments have long restricted particularly dangerous weapons, and that the AR-15, with its military origins and its capacity for rapid fire, falls within it.

Counting to five

The arithmetic of the Court is the part of Wednesday that will be watched most closely. The Court is 6-3 conservative, which means the three liberal justices would need at least two more votes to uphold the bans. At least four conservative justices have signalled scepticism with the bans to varying degrees — Justices Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito and Brett Kavanaugh have dissented from past decisions by the Court not to take up challenges to assault-style rifle bans, or dissented in their roles on appeals courts from rulings upholding the bans. If that holds, it means the government will need to find at least two justices to reach a five-vote majority, assuming the Court's three-justice liberal wing supports them.

That puts an enormous amount of focus on two justices in particular: Chief Justice John Roberts and Justice Amy Coney Barrett. Justice Neil Gorsuch has been less clear, though he voted last year to hear the Maryland case that the Court declined. Justice Kavanaugh, a conservative who sits closer to the centre than Justices Thomas or Alito, wrote in the Maryland case that the state was "something of an outlier" by having a ban on the weapons — a line the challengers will quote, and one that suggests he regards the bans as unusual rather than normal. But Kavanaugh has also shown, in other cases, a willingness to uphold regulations that he regards as consistent with the nation's history, and the bans' defenders will be listening for any sign that he sees a historical analogue for them.

The federal government will also be heard on Wednesday, and its position is a measure of how much the politics of guns have changed. Solicitor General D. John Sauer asked for ten minutes of argument time, and the Court agreed on 9 November to give the government a slot. The United States supports the challengers — the Trump administration's Justice Department is on the side of striking the bans down. That is a reversal from the position the government took in earlier administrations, and it means that on Wednesday, the lawyers defending the bans will be arguing against both the gun-rights groups and the federal government at once.

The arguments will last one hour, and they will be the most closely watched hour of the Court's term so far. The courtroom will be full. The lines outside will form before dawn. And the questions the justices ask — who asks them, how they are phrased, and who struggles to answer them — will be parsed within minutes by the lawyers, the advocates and the journalists who cover the Court, for signs of where the five votes are.

What a ruling would mean

A ruling for the challengers would invalidate assault weapons bans in the states that have them — 11 states, according to CNN's count, with other estimates putting the number of affected jurisdictions at 14 states plus the District of Columbia, depending on how the bans are defined. It would, in the words of one analysis of the case, functionally invalidate every state-level assault weapons ban, and it would give the Court's blessing to the argument that the AR-15 is constitutionally protected — a holding that would shape gun litigation for a generation. A ruling for the states would do the opposite: it would give governments a constitutional template for categorical bans on semi-automatic rifles, affirm the "dangerous and unusual" standard the lower courts have used to uphold them, and leave the bans in place — including in the states that have been waiting for the Court to decide before passing their own.

The consequences would not be limited to the AR-15. The Court's reasoning in this case will be applied, in the years that follow, to every other gun law that is challenged — to bans on high-capacity magazines, to restrictions on particular features, to the laws that other states are considering now. The cases the Court is holding — Duncan v. Bonta, on California's magazine ban, and National Association for Gun Rights v. Lamont, on Connecticut's ban — have not been scheduled for a conference since 29 June 2026, and the common reading is that the Court is holding them until it decides the consolidated rifle cases. A decision in Khaya and Grant will, in effect, decide them too. And Massachusetts's rifle ban is now before the Court as well: in Recchia v. Campbell, the Court has asked the state to respond by 7 December.

For the people who live under the bans, the ruling will be personal. In Connecticut, it will decide whether the law passed after Sandy Hook — the law that the state's legislature adopted in the months when the funerals were still being held — can stand. In Cook County, it will decide whether Chicago, a city that has lived with gun violence for decades, can keep a ban that has been on the books since the 1990s. For the gun owners who challenged the bans, it will decide whether they can own, in their own homes, a rifle that 26 million of their fellow citizens already own. And for the rest of the country, it will decide whether the AR-15 — the most popular rifle in America, and the weapon of choice in its worst mass shootings — is a right or a privilege, a tool or a threat.

The long road to Wednesday

The path to this argument has been long, and it runs through the Court's own history. For years, the Court declined to hear challenges to assault-weapon bans, even as the lower courts split over them and the bans spread from state to state. Justice Thomas dissented from those decisions, arguing that the Court was treating the Second Amendment as a second-class right. Justice Kavanaugh, writing from the appeals court, called Maryland's ban an outlier. And the gun-rights movement, patient and well-funded, kept bringing cases, kept losing in the lower courts, and kept asking the Court to take one.

The turning point was Bruen, in 2022, which struck down New York's requirement that residents show cause to obtain a carry permit, and which replaced the old balancing test for gun laws with a new one: history and tradition, or nothing. After Bruen, the lower courts had to decide whether the bans had a historical analogue, and they split. The Second and Seventh Circuits said yes, and upheld the bans. Other courts, looking at the same history, said no. The split, and the sheer number of Americans who own the rifles, made the case impossible for the Court to avoid forever. On 30 June 2026, it stopped avoiding it.

The year since then has been a study in how a Supreme Court case is built. The petitioners filed their opening brief on 28 August. Cook County and Connecticut filed theirs on 21 October. The United States filed its brief supporting the challengers, and asked for argument time. The 2026 National Firearms Survey was published on 24 August, in time to be cited in the petitioners' brief, and its findings — 40 million AR-15-type rifles, 26 million owners — have become the factual spine of the "common use" argument. The mootness fight in the Cook County case, which began on 29 October when Viramontes asked to withdraw, has added a layer of procedural drama that the justices will have to resolve before they reach the merits, or alongside them.

And the country has been watching. The Sandy Hook families, who appealed to the Court to uphold the bans, have been among the most visible voices in the case, and their presence — in the briefs, in the coverage, in the courtroom on Wednesday — is a reminder of what the bans were passed for. The gun-rights groups, for their part, have framed the case as a test of whether the Second Amendment means what it says, and whether a right that belongs to tens of millions of Americans can be taken away by a state legislature. Both sides believe the Constitution is on their side. On Wednesday, they will find out whether the Court agrees.

What to watch on Wednesday

There are a few things to listen for when the arguments begin at 10 a.m. The first is the mootness question in the Cook County case: whether the justices treat it as a technicality to be disposed of quickly, or as a genuine obstacle that could end the Illinois case before it begins. The second is the justices' treatment of the "common use" argument: whether they accept the challengers' framing — that 40 million rifles and 26 million owners settle the question — or whether they press on what "common use" means, and whether a weapon's popularity is enough to protect it. The third is the history: whether the justices find, in the nation's tradition of firearm regulation, an analogue for banning a particular class of rifle, or whether they conclude, as the challengers argue, that no such analogue exists.

And then there is the question that will matter most, and that no one can answer until the ruling comes: how the justices vote. The Court's 6-3 conservative majority has, in recent years, moved gun law decisively in one direction, and the bans' defenders know it. But the Court has also shown, in cases like the unanimous opinion last year that called the AR-15 the most popular rifle in the country, that it is willing to speak about these weapons in terms that cut both ways. A Court that calls the AR-15 the most popular rifle in the country may be a Court that is preparing to protect it — or a Court that is simply describing a fact, and leaving the legal question for another day.

Wednesday will not answer that question. The ruling will come months from now, in late June or early July 2027, and the months between the argument and the decision will be filled with speculation, with leaks, with the slow work of drafting an opinion that will shape American law for a generation. But Wednesday will tell us a great deal about how the argument went, and about who is winning it. The justices' questions are the best early evidence of their thinking, and the lawyers who argue the case will be listening for them as carefully as anyone.

For the rest of the country, Wednesday is the day the gun debate comes to the Supreme Court in its starkest form. It is not about a single shooting, or a single law, or a single state. It is about whether the most popular rifle in America can be banned by the states that have banned it, and whether the Second Amendment protects a weapon that 26 million Americans own and that has been used in the worst mass shootings in the country's history. The Court has avoided that question for years. On Wednesday, at 10 a.m., it stops avoiding it. And by the summer, the whole country will know the answer.
