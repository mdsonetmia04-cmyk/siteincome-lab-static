TITLE: Meta Just Put Its Muse AI Inside Your Instagram DMs — 5 Countries Get It First, and It Can Plan Your Whole Trip LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Meta's Muse AI assistant is now inside Instagram DMs in the US, Canada, Mexico, Japan and Singapore, turning saved posts into trips, lists and deals ---BODY---

Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg. His company's Muse AI assistant has now moved into Instagram direct messages, putting an AI agent inside the inbox where billions of people already spend their day. Photo: Eirik Solheim, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0).

Meta has put its Muse AI assistant inside Instagram direct messages, and the move turns the place where people already share posts, places and products into the place where an AI agent can act on them. The company announced the rollout on Tuesday, November 24, and the feature has begun reaching iPhone and Android users in the United States, Canada, Mexico, Japan and Singapore — five countries first, with more to follow. It is the latest step in Meta's campaign to put Muse into just about every corner of its apps, and it is the most consequential one yet, because Instagram DMs are where a lot of people actually spend their day. You no longer need to leave the app, open a separate assistant, and paste in a link to ask a question about something you saw. You can now DM Muse itself, or share something you see on Instagram straight to it, and ask it to do something with it — to add a coffee shop to your travel itinerary, to find a deal on a pair of shoes, to sort your saved posts into collections, or to catch you up on a conversation you missed.

The announcement, reported by TechCrunch and confirmed by Instagram, is built around two ideas that sound simple and are, in practice, a significant change in how people use Instagram. The first is that Muse is now a conversation inside your DMs. You can chat with the assistant in a dedicated DM thread, ask it about a post, a place or a product, or get a recap of conversations you missed — and if you use the Muse app, those chats show up there too, as a side chat in the main Muse app. The second is that Muse can now do something with the content you save. Instagram says Muse can sort your saves into collections or itineraries, find saved Reels, and turn a recipe into a grocery list. In a post on X, Instagram announced that Muse can now organise saved posts into Collections and turn travel content into itineraries, and that when you save a post, Instagram will prompt you to let Muse organise it. The saved-content features are initially available in the United States and Canada, while the DM integration itself is rolling out in all five countries. The rollout started on Tuesday and will reach more users over the coming days, because features like this usually arrive in stages, and not everyone will see it on the same day.

What Muse can actually do inside Instagram is broader than a chatbot that answers questions. According to Meta, users can share a post, a place or a product with Muse and ask for more context about it. You can ask about a post you are looking at, or share a post with Muse and ask it to take action — such as adding a coffee shop to your travel itinerary, or finding a deal on shoes. You can ask it to organise your saved posts into folders, called Collections, or to turn the travel content you have saved into a proper itinerary. You can ask it to find a saved Reel you remember but cannot locate, or to turn a recipe you saved into a grocery list. You can ask it to catch you up on a conversation you missed, or to summarise a thread. And there is a nosier option, too: the Instagram Messages connector, which lets Muse help you write a message or summarise a thread with other people — although you set what the agent can access, and you can change those limits at any time. To use the feature, you need to connect your Instagram and Muse accounts, and the functionality is more limited than the actual Muse app or web interface, because you still need to use the Muse app or website to see what the assistant does with all your Instagram messages.

The control question is the one Meta has been most careful to answer, and it is the one that will decide whether people trust the feature. Instagram says Muse will not make changes or take any action unless users give it the go-ahead, and that Muse will not send, share or publish anything unless users approve it. Meta says Muse will not send a message, publish anything, or take an action without the user's approval, and that the user stays in control. You can ask Muse to forget information or undo actions at any time, and you can tap Muse's avatar, then Activity or Approvals, to see what it has done. Connector permissions can be edited, too. Chatting is free, with a usage cap — if you hit it, you wait for a refresh or pay for a Muse subscription — and paid subscribers in the United States can already have Muse make phone calls for them. The message is consistent across every report of the launch: the agent is inside your inbox, but it does not act without your say-so, and you can see what it has done and take it back.

Why Instagram DMs, and why now

The reason Meta has put Muse inside Instagram DMs is that DMs are where Instagram actually lives now. The feed is still there, and Reels are still the growth engine, but the sharing — the sending of a post to a friend, the discussion of a restaurant, the planning of a trip in a group chat — happens in direct messages, and it happens there because that is where people are comfortable being specific about what they want. A public comment is a performance. A DM is a plan. Meta has understood that for years, and the company has been steadily building its messaging products into the centre of its apps, from the merging of Instagram and Messenger infrastructure to the addition of channels, notes and broadcast tools. Putting Muse into DMs is the logical next step: if the planning is already happening in the inbox, then the assistant that helps with the planning should be in the inbox too, rather than in a separate app that you have to remember to open.

The timing is also significant. Meta launched its Muse agent in the United Kingdom only yesterday, according to reports, although it is not currently available on Instagram for British users just yet — which means the Instagram DM integration is arriving in five countries while the standalone Muse agent is still rolling out elsewhere. The company has been squeezing Muse into just about every corner of its apps lately, as Digital Trends put it, and Instagram is the latest stop. The pattern is familiar from the history of Meta's AI products: a feature appears in one app, then in another, then in the glasses, then on the watch — Gadgets360 noted, alongside the Instagram announcement, that the Meta Muse AI assistant has also arrived on Apple Watch with a watch face shortcut — and each time, the assistant becomes a little harder to avoid and a little easier to use. The Instagram integration is the most important of these steps so far, because Instagram has more than two billion monthly users, and because the DM inbox is the most intimate surface Meta owns.

There is a competitive logic to the move as well. Every major technology company is now racing to put an AI agent inside the products people already use, rather than asking people to come to a separate AI product. Google has Gemini inside Gmail and Docs. Microsoft has Copilot inside Office and Windows. OpenAI has ChatGPT inside an ever-growing list of partnerships and integrations. Meta's answer is to put Muse inside Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook and Messenger — the apps where its users already spend hours a day — and to make the assistant useful in the specific context of each app, rather than as a generic chatbot. In Instagram, that means the assistant knows about posts, places, products and saved content, because those are the things people share on Instagram. The trip-planning example is the telling one: you save a dozen posts about a city over several months, and then Muse can turn them into an itinerary, with the coffee shops and the restaurants and the places to stay, drawn from the content you already saved. That is a use case that only makes sense inside Instagram, and it is the kind of use case that makes an AI assistant feel less like a novelty and more like a tool.

What it can do with your saves

The saved-posts features are the most practical part of the announcement, and they solve a problem that every Instagram user knows. Saving a post on Instagram is easy; finding it again is not. People save recipes, travel ideas, outfits, restaurants and products, and then forget they saved them, or cannot find them when they need them, because the saved collection grows into an unsearchable pile. Muse is supposed to fix that, by organising saved posts into Collections, by finding saved Reels on request, and by turning saved content into something usable — a travel itinerary from the travel posts you saved, a grocery list from the recipe you saved, a set of deals from the products you saved. Instagram will prompt you to let Muse organise a post when you save it, which means the organisation happens at the moment of saving, rather than as a chore you have to do later. The saved-content features are initially available in the United States and Canada, while the DM integration is rolling out in all five countries, and the split reflects the way Meta usually rolls out features: the chat first, in more places, and the deeper integration second, in fewer places, until it is ready to expand.

The travel example deserves a closer look, because it is the one Meta keeps returning to, and because it shows what the company thinks Muse is for. The Muse account on X posted that you can now DM it on Instagram and turn saved posts into plans, "like that trip you keep meaning to take." The scenario is familiar: you see a post about a café in Lisbon, you save it; you see a Reel about a hotel in Kyoto, you save it; you see a post about a hiking trail in the mountains, you save it — and then, months later, when you are actually planning the trip, you have to remember what you saved, find it, and put it together. Muse is supposed to do that work for you: to take the saved posts, sort them into a collection, and turn them into an itinerary, with the places in order, the practical details attached, and the gaps filled in. You can share a post with Muse and ask it to add a place to the itinerary, or ask it to find a deal on a product you saw. It is a small thing, in the sense that it is about organising content you already have, but it is also the kind of small thing that makes an app feel indispensable — the difference between an app you scroll through and an app you plan your life in.

The shopping features work the same way. You see a product in a post or a Reel, you share it with Muse, and you ask it to find a deal on it — and Muse, which can see the product and knows what it is, can look for the best price, or for similar products, or for the information you need to decide whether to buy it. You can ask about a product you are looking at, or about a place, or about a post, and Muse will give you more context, without you having to leave the app, open a browser, and search for it yourself. The point, in every case, is the same: the assistant is inside the app where the content is, and it can act on the content directly, rather than asking you to copy and paste it somewhere else. That is the promise of the agentic AI that Meta and its competitors are all building — an assistant that does things, not just an assistant that answers questions — and Instagram DMs are the latest place where that promise is being tested.

The privacy question nobody can avoid

Putting an AI agent inside people's direct messages raises a question that no amount of reassurance about approvals and controls can fully answer, and it is the question that will follow this feature for as long as it exists: what does it mean for an AI to be inside your private conversations? Meta's answer is that Muse will not send, share or publish anything unless you approve it, that you set what the agent can access and can change those limits at any time, and that you can ask Muse to forget information or undo actions at any time. The company has been careful to say that the user stays in control, and that nothing is shared or sent without approval. But the feature also requires you to connect your Instagram and Muse accounts, and it gives Muse access to your saved posts, your DMs and your conversations — or, at least, to the parts of them you allow it to see. The Instagram Messages connector, which lets Muse help you write a message or summarise a thread with other people, is the most sensitive part of the integration, because it involves the assistant reading conversations that were not addressed to it.

The history of Meta's AI products gives both reassurance and reason for caution. The company has built its AI assistant into WhatsApp, Messenger and Facebook, and it has done so with a set of controls that let users limit what the assistant can see and do — and it has also, repeatedly, expanded what its AI products can access, in ways that have drawn criticism from privacy advocates and regulators. The European Union, in particular, has pushed back on Meta's use of user data to train AI models, and the company has had to adjust its approach in Europe more than once. The Instagram DM integration is not launching in Europe yet, and the fact that the UK got the standalone Muse agent only yesterday, without the Instagram integration, suggests that the company is being careful about where and how it rolls out the feature. The five launch countries — the United States, Canada, Mexico, Japan and Singapore — are all markets where Meta has a relatively free hand, and the absence of the UK, the EU, India and Pakistan from the list is notable. Pakistan is not on the list, as one report pointed out, and there has been no announcement of the chat feature for Pakistan, and no date given for when, or whether, it will arrive there. India, too, has not yet got the feature, although Muse is expected to bring it there soon, along with the new integration.

The privacy question is not only about what Meta does with the data, but about what the feature does to the nature of the inbox itself. Direct messages are, for most people, the most private space they have on the internet — the place where they talk to their friends, their family and their partners, without an audience. Putting an AI assistant in that space changes the feeling of it, even if the assistant does nothing without approval, because the assistant is there, in the list of conversations, with an avatar and a name, and because the option to share a conversation with it is always present. Some people will find that useful. Others will find it intrusive. The test of the feature will not be whether it works — it almost certainly does — but whether people want an AI in their inbox, and whether the convenience of having it there outweighs the discomfort of knowing that it is there. Meta is betting that the answer is yes, and that the trip planning, the grocery lists and the deal-finding will be enough to make people comfortable with an agent in their DMs. The next few months, as the rollout reaches more users in the five launch countries, will show whether that bet pays off.

What to watch next

The first thing to watch is the rollout itself, and how quickly it reaches users in the five launch countries. The feature began rolling out on Tuesday, and Meta says it will reach more users over the coming days, but staged rollouts can take weeks, and the experience of the first users will shape the reputation of the feature. The second thing to watch is the saved-content features, which are initially available only in the United States and Canada, and whether they expand to Mexico, Japan and Singapore, and then to the rest of the world. The third thing to watch is whether the Instagram integration arrives in the UK and the EU, and what changes Meta has to make to satisfy regulators there. The fourth thing to watch is the usage cap, and whether the free tier is generous enough to make the feature useful, or whether the cap pushes people towards a paid Muse subscription — and whether the phone-calling feature, currently available to paid subscribers in the United States, expands to more countries and more users.

The fifth thing to watch is the competition, and how quickly the other platforms respond. Google, Microsoft and OpenAI are all building agents into their products, and the race is now about which assistant becomes the one people actually use, in the apps they already open every day. Meta's advantage is distribution: Instagram, WhatsApp, Facebook and Messenger reach billions of people, and putting Muse inside them gives the assistant an audience that no standalone AI app can match. The disadvantage is trust: Meta has spent years rebuilding its reputation on privacy, and putting an AI inside people's private messages is the kind of move that can undo that work quickly if it goes wrong. The Instagram DM integration is a bet that the convenience will win, and that the controls — the approvals, the activity log, the ability to forget and undo — will be enough to keep people comfortable. It is a bet that will be decided, one DM at a time, by the people who use it.

The launch is also a reminder of how quickly the AI landscape is changing. A year ago, the idea of an AI assistant inside your Instagram DMs would have sounded like a science-fiction premise. Today, it is a feature rolling out to users in five countries, with a set of capabilities — trip planning, grocery lists, deal-finding, conversation summaries — that would have been impressive as a standalone product, and that are now being offered as a side feature inside an app you already have. The question is no longer whether AI assistants will be everywhere. They are already everywhere, or on their way. The question is which ones people will actually use, and which ones will earn the trust to sit inside the most private spaces people have. Muse in Instagram DMs is Meta's answer to that question, and the answer will be written in the coming weeks, in the five countries where the rollout has begun, by the users who decide whether to DM the assistant — or to leave it alone.
