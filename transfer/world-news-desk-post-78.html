TITLE: Judge Just Blocked Trump's FCC From Reviewing ABC's Licences Early — "This Is Not a Close Case" LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A federal judge blocked the FCC's early review of ABC's licences, ruling the Trump administration's move was retaliation for speech it disliked ---BODY---

Judge Loren AliKhan of the United States District Court for the District of Columbia. She ruled on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, that the FCC's early review of ABC's broadcast licences was likely retaliation for speech protected by the First Amendment. Photo: Public domain via Wikimedia Commons.

A federal judge has blocked the Trump administration from threatening ABC's broadcast licences, ruling that the Federal Communications Commission's extraordinary early review of the network's stations was, in all likelihood, punishment for programming the president did not like. In a decision handed down on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, United States District Judge Loren AliKhan granted ABC's request for a preliminary injunction, halting the FCC's review of eight ABC-owned television stations while the network's First Amendment lawsuit moves through the courts. Her words were blunt. "This is not a close case," she wrote.

The ruling is a significant defeat for FCC Chairman Brendan Carr, a close ally of President Donald Trump, and for an agency that has spent months arguing that its scrutiny of Disney's ABC was a routine exercise of its licensing power. Judge AliKhan did not accept that account. She found that Disney's lawyers had presented substantial evidence that the early renewal proceedings were motivated, at least in part, by retaliation for protected speech — and she concluded that ABC is likely to succeed on its First Amendment claim. In the judge's telling, the timing alone told the story: the FCC launched its review on April 28, 2026, just one day after President Trump publicly criticised remarks by Jimmy Kimmel, ABC's late-night host. As Judge AliKhan put it in her ruling, Chairman Carr "has taken President Trump's rhetoric as his marching orders."

The injunction means the FCC cannot continue its early review of ABC's station licences while the legal process plays out. The eight stations at the centre of the case — KFSN-TV in Fresno, KABC-TV in Los Angeles, KGO-TV in San Francisco, WLS-TV in Chicago, WABC-TV in New York, WTVD in Raleigh-Durham, North Carolina, WPVI-TV in Philadelphia and KTRK-TV in Houston — were not due for renewal until 2028 at the earliest, with some licences running until 2031. The FCC's April order would have forced Disney to defend all eight of them years ahead of schedule, in a process that could, in theory, have ended with the licences being revoked. For now, that threat is frozen.

The decision also denied the FCC's motion to dismiss Disney's lawsuit outright, meaning the case will proceed. It is, by any measure, one of the most consequential First Amendment rulings of the year — a federal court telling a regulator, in plain language, that it cannot use the licensing power of the state as a weapon against a broadcaster whose coverage the president dislikes. And it arrives at a moment when the relationship between the Trump administration and the American press has rarely been more strained, with the FCC at the centre of a series of confrontations over what broadcasters may say, show and joke about on air.

Eight stations, one extraordinary order

To understand why Judge AliKhan's ruling matters, it helps to understand just how unusual the FCC's action was. Broadcast licences in the United States are renewed on a staggered, eight-year cycle. A station files for renewal when its licence comes due, the FCC reviews the application against the public interest standard written into the Communications Act, and — in the overwhelming majority of cases — the licence is renewed without drama. The system is designed to be predictable. Stations know when their renewals are coming, they prepare for them, and the process is, by design, boring.

What happened on April 28, 2026 was anything but boring. On that day, David J. Brown, the chief of the FCC's video division, wrote to Disney, ABC and the eight stations directing them to file licence renewals for all of their licensed television stations within 30 days — by May 28, 2026. "The FCC determines that calling in Disney's ABC licenses for early renewal, at this time, under the Communications Act's public interest standard is essential within the meaning of agency regulations," Brown wrote. The letter gave no detailed explanation of what, exactly, had changed to make an early review "essential" in April 2026, when the licences were not due for years.

The FCC's stated justification, offered later, was that the agency had launched its review to determine whether Disney's diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives violated the agency's prohibition on unlawful discrimination. But the timing raised obvious questions. The early review was ordered on April 28 — one day after President Trump criticised remarks by Jimmy Kimmel, the host of ABC's late-night programme. The FCC's own chairman, Brendan Carr, is a staunch ally of the president, and the agency's move came amid a broader pattern of administration criticism of ABC's programming. Disney's lawyers argued that the sequence of events was not a coincidence at all: the review was retaliation, dressed up as regulation.

Judge AliKhan agreed that the evidence supported that reading. In her ruling, she found that Disney had "sufficiently cast the early license renewal proceedings as a retaliatory act." That finding — that the proceedings were, at least in part, motivated by retaliation for protected speech — is the heart of the case, and it is what the preliminary injunction rests on. The judge also noted the broader context: the FCC's decision to call in the licences came after months of public criticism of ABC from the president, and the agency's own explanation did not account for why the review had to happen years early, rather than on the normal schedule.

The eight stations named by the FCC are not small, regional outlets. They are ABC's owned-and-operated stations in eight of the largest media markets in the country: New York, Los Angeles, Chicago, Philadelphia, San Francisco, Houston, Fresno and Raleigh-Durham. Together they reach tens of millions of viewers, and they are the backbone of ABC's local news and programming operations. Losing even one of their licences would have been a serious blow to Disney; losing all eight would have been a catastrophe. That is what made the FCC's order so alarming to the network — and to press-freedom advocates, who saw in it a template that could be used against any broadcaster whose coverage displeased the government of the day.

"This is not a close case"

The phrase that will be remembered from Judge AliKhan's ruling is the one she used to describe the strength of Disney's case: "This is not a close case." It is not the kind of language judges use lightly. Federal judges, particularly in high-profile cases involving the government, tend to write in careful, hedged terms, acknowledging the force of the other side's arguments even when they rule against them. Judge AliKhan did not hedge. Her ruling was direct, and its message was unmistakable: the evidence that the FCC's action was retaliatory was strong, and the First Amendment claim was likely to succeed.

The judge's reasoning turned on the doctrine of retaliation. Under the First Amendment, the government may not punish a person or a company for engaging in protected speech. That principle applies with particular force when the government uses a regulatory power — like the power to grant or revoke a broadcast licence — as the instrument of punishment. The question in this case was whether the FCC's early review was a legitimate exercise of regulatory authority, or whether it was a pretext for punishing ABC for speech the administration disliked. Judge AliKhan concluded that Disney had presented enough evidence to show that retaliation was, at least in part, the motive.

Central to that conclusion was the timing. The FCC's April 28 letter came one day after President Trump's criticism of Jimmy Kimmel's remarks. The judge noted that the FCC had offered no persuasive explanation for why the review had to begin in April 2026, rather than waiting for the licences to come due on their normal schedule between 2028 and 2031. The agency's stated concern — Disney's diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives — did not explain the urgency, and it did not explain why the review had to cover all eight stations at once, years ahead of schedule.

The judge also rejected the FCC's attempt to have the lawsuit thrown out. The agency had argued, among other things, that the court lacked jurisdiction and that Disney had failed to establish that the scrutiny was retaliation over its programming. Judge AliKhan denied the motion to dismiss, clearing the way for the case to proceed to a full hearing on the merits. The preliminary injunction she granted is not a final ruling — it is a temporary order, designed to preserve the status quo while the case is litigated — but the strength of her language suggests that the FCC will face an uphill battle when the case is finally decided.

There was, however, one limit to the judge's ruling. She declined ABC's request for a broader injunction that would have barred the FCC from threatening the company over its editorial decisions in the future. The judge reasoned that the network had not specified what those future threats could entail, beyond the early licence renewal proceedings themselves. The ruling also does not stop the FCC from continuing its investigations into Disney's employment practices or into "The View," ABC's daytime talk show. Those inquiries, which the FCC has said are separate matters, may continue.

The FCC's response: "wrong on the facts and wrong on the law"

The FCC did not accept the ruling quietly. A spokesperson for the agency said the decision granting Disney's motion was "wrong on the facts and wrong on the law" and that the agency "looks forward to prevailing in full on this matter." The spokesperson added that the FCC had "developed a voluminous record" and would "continue to follow the facts and the law wherever they lead." The tone was defiant, and it suggested that the agency intends to appeal — most likely to the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit, which hears most challenges to FCC decisions.

The FCC's position, as it has articulated it in court filings and public statements, is that its review of ABC's licences was a legitimate regulatory action, driven by concerns about whether Disney's diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives violated the agency's prohibition on unlawful discrimination. The agency has argued that Disney filed its lawsuit in the wrong court at the wrong time, based on what it called "erroneous legal and factual assertions." In September, when the FCC asked a federal judge to dismiss the case, a spokesperson described the lawsuit as "meritless."

Judge AliKhan's ruling rejects that characterisation. By granting a preliminary injunction — a form of relief that requires the party seeking it to show a likelihood of success on the merits — she signalled that Disney's First Amendment claim is not only plausible but strong. And by denying the motion to dismiss, she rejected the FCC's argument that the court should not hear the case at all.

The ruling also drew a sharp line between the FCC's stated justification and its actual conduct. The agency said it was concerned about discrimination in Disney's employment practices. But the early renewal proceeding it launched was not a narrow inquiry into hiring practices — it was a full review of the licences of eight major television stations, years before they were due. The judge's ruling effectively says that the scale and timing of the action did not match the explanation offered for it, and that the gap between the two was best explained by retaliation.

Who is Judge Loren AliKhan?

The judge at the centre of this case is, herself, a figure worth understanding. Loren Linn AliKhan, born in 1983 in Baltimore County, Maryland, has served as a United States district judge for the District of Columbia since December 13, 2023. She was nominated to the post by President Joe Biden and confirmed by the United States Senate on December 5, 2023, by a vote of 51 to 50 — with Vice President Kamala Harris casting the tie-breaking vote, a moment that made history by giving Harris the most tie-breaking Senate votes of any vice president.

Before her appointment to the federal bench, AliKhan served as an associate judge of the District of Columbia Court of Appeals from February 2022 to December 2023, and as the solicitor general of the District of Columbia from 2018 to 2022. In that role, she handled nearly 3,000 cases in the District's federal and local courts, and in 2020 she received the National Association of Attorneys General's "Senior Staff of the Year" award. She graduated from Bard College at Simon's Rock in 2003 with a Bachelor of Arts, summa cum laude, and from Georgetown University Law Center in 2006 with a law degree, magna cum laude, where she was an editor of the Georgetown Law Journal.

AliKhan's background is in appellate litigation and public service, not in media law. But her ruling in the ABC case is grounded in a principle that is central to First Amendment law: that the government may not use its regulatory powers to punish speech it dislikes. It is a principle that has been tested in many contexts — from the denial of government contracts to the revocation of licences — and the courts have consistently held that retaliation for protected speech is unconstitutional, even when the government action in question would otherwise be within its lawful authority.

That principle matters here because the FCC's licensing power is, in a sense, the most potent tool the government has over broadcasters. A broadcaster that loses its licence loses its ability to broadcast. The threat of losing a licence — even if the threat is never carried out — can have a chilling effect on what a broadcaster is willing to say. That is precisely what ABC argued in its lawsuit: that the FCC's early review, and the threat it carried, had a chilling effect on the network's free speech rights. Judge AliKhan agreed.

A lawsuit that began in August

The legal battle that led to Wednesday's ruling began in August 2026, when ABC sued the FCC in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia, requesting a temporary restraining order and a preliminary injunction to halt the agency's licence-renewal process. The lawsuit was the culmination of months of tension between the network and the regulator, tension that had been building since the FCC's April order.

In September, the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press filed a friend-of-the-court brief urging the court to grant the injunction. The brief, authored by former United States Solicitor General Donald B. Verrilli Jr. of the law firm Munger, Tolles & Olson, argued that the FCC's action threatened the First Amendment rights not only of ABC but of all broadcasters. The Reporters Committee, which represents journalists and news organisations, warned that allowing the FCC to use early licence reviews as a tool of retaliation would set a dangerous precedent.

The FCC, for its part, moved to dismiss the lawsuit, arguing that the court lacked jurisdiction and that Disney had failed to make its case. The agency's lawyers argued that the early renewal proceeding was a routine regulatory matter, and that the court should not interfere with the agency's exercise of its licensing authority. Judge AliKhan rejected both arguments, denying the motion to dismiss and granting the injunction.

The case now moves forward to a full hearing on the merits, unless the FCC's appeal succeeds first. The preliminary injunction will remain in place while the case is litigated, which means the FCC cannot proceed with its early review of ABC's licences in the meantime. For Disney, that is a significant victory — and for the broader media industry, it is a signal that the courts are willing to push back when regulators appear to be using their power to punish speech.

What this means for free speech — and for every broadcaster in America

The significance of Judge AliKhan's ruling extends well beyond ABC and Disney. The FCC regulates every broadcast television and radio station in the United States. Its licensing power touches thousands of stations, from the largest network affiliates in New York and Los Angeles to the smallest local stations in rural America. If the agency could use that power to punish broadcasters for their coverage, the effect on American journalism would be profound.

That is why press-freedom advocates have followed this case so closely, and why the Reporters Committee intervened. The concern is not only about what happened to ABC — it is about what could happen to any broadcaster if the FCC's action were allowed to stand. A regulator that can call in licences years early, without a clear justification, holds a powerful weapon over every station it regulates. The mere existence of that weapon, even if it is never used, can shape what broadcasters are willing to say.

Judge AliKhan's ruling addresses that concern directly. By finding that Disney is likely to succeed on its First Amendment claim, and by halting the early review, she has sent a message that the courts will scrutinise regulatory actions that appear to be motivated by retaliation. The ruling does not strip the FCC of its licensing authority — the agency can still review licences on their normal schedule, and it can still investigate genuine violations of its rules. What it cannot do, according to the judge, is use the licensing process as a tool to punish a broadcaster for its speech.

The ruling also matters because of the context in which it was issued. The Trump administration has repeatedly criticised major media outlets, and the FCC under Chairman Carr has been at the centre of several confrontations with broadcasters. The ABC case is the most prominent of those confrontations, but it is not the only one. The agency's investigations into Disney's employment practices and into "The View" continue, and the broader question of how the FCC uses its power over broadcasters remains very much alive.

For now, though, the immediate consequence is clear: ABC's eight stations are safe from early review, the FCC's motion to dismiss has failed, and the case will proceed. Disney has won the first round — and it has won it decisively. As Judge AliKhan put it, this is not a close case.

What happens next

The FCC is expected to appeal the preliminary injunction to the D.C. Circuit, and the case will continue in the district court in the meantime. An appeal could take months, and the outcome is far from certain — appellate courts give considerable deference to trial judges on preliminary injunctions, but they also review the legal questions involved with fresh eyes. If the FCC loses on appeal, the early review will remain blocked while the case proceeds to trial. If it wins, the agency could resume its review of ABC's licences.

Either way, the case is likely to be closely watched by the media industry, by First Amendment lawyers, and by anyone who cares about the independence of American broadcasting. The questions it raises — about the limits of regulatory power, about retaliation for protected speech, and about the role of the FCC in a democracy — are among the most important in American media law. Judge AliKhan's ruling is an early answer to those questions, and it is an answer that favours free speech. The final answer will come from the courts in the months ahead.

For ABC, the ruling is a reprieve — and a vindication. The network argued from the start that the FCC's early review was not about diversity initiatives or unlawful discrimination, but about punishing a broadcaster whose programming the president had criticised. A federal judge has now agreed that the network is likely right. The licences of eight stations in eight major American cities are safe, at least for now. And the principle that the government may not use its licensing power to punish speech has been reaffirmed, in the plainest language a judge can use.
