TITLE: Russia Just Approved a $609 Billion War Budget — 1 in Every 3 Rubles Goes to the Military LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Russia's parliament approved a record $609B defence budget for 2027–29 — 35% of all state spending. War costs since 2022 could top $1.3 trillion. ---BODY---

Russian President Vladimir Putin in February 2026. Russia's parliament has just approved the biggest military budget since the collapse of the Soviet Union — more than 50 trillion roubles, or about $609 billion, for defence between 2027 and 2029. Photo: Kremlin.ru, via Wikimedia Commons.

Russia has just voted itself the biggest war budget since the Soviet Union fell apart. The country's parliament, the State Duma, has approved a federal budget for 2027 to 2029 that sets aside more than 50 trillion roubles — around $609 billion — for military spending over the three years. It is the largest defence allocation any government in Moscow has planned since the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991, and it lands at a moment when the war in Ukraine is about to enter its fifth year, when Europe cannot agree on how to pay for Kyiv's defence, and when the American president is publicly pressing Vladimir Putin to pull back.

The vote came in the Duma's third and final reading on Tuesday, 24 November. The numbers are stark enough on their own. In 2027, the first year of the plan, Russia will spend 17.15 trillion roubles on defence — about $208.8 billion. That is roughly a third more than the 12.93 trillion roubles, or $157.4 billion, earmarked for defence in 2026 under the previous budget plan. The following two years fall back only slightly: 16.61 trillion roubles, or $202.2 billion, in 2028, and 16.30 trillion roubles, or $198.4 billion, in 2029. Every one of those years is far above what Russia is spending today.

Put another way, more than one in every three roubles the Russian state spends in 2027 will go straight to the military. Defence will absorb 35.2 per cent of total federal spending next year. Total federal expenditure in 2027 is projected at 48.74 trillion roubles, about $593.3 billion — which means the defence line alone, at 17.15 trillion roubles, is more than a third of everything the state does: courts, schools, hospitals, pensions, roads, and the military, all divided up, with the military taking the biggest slice by a wide margin.

This is not a one-year spike. It is a three-year commitment, written into law, that locks in war spending at record levels through the end of the decade. And it comes with a second, even larger number attached: if the allocations for 2027 to 2029 are fully implemented, Russia's cumulative military spending since the start of its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 could exceed 108 trillion roubles — around $1.31 trillion — by the end of 2029.

The vote that locked in three years of war spending

The State Duma passed the federal budget for 2027–29 in its third and final reading on 24 November, according to reporting by The Moscow Times and Russian parliamentary television channel Duma TV. The third reading is the decisive one: by the time a budget reaches it, the numbers are settled, and the vote is a formality that confirms what the government has already decided. The defence lines in the bill were the headline, and they were the point.

Under the new budget, the increases over the previous three-year plan are themselves enormous. Defence allocations were raised by around 3.58 trillion roubles, or $43.6 billion, for 2027 and by 3.56 trillion roubles, or $43.3 billion, for 2028, compared with earlier projections. Across the whole three-year plan, total national defence spending exceeds the figure in the previous three-year budget plan by approximately 10.5 trillion roubles, or about $127.8 billion. In other words, even against a plan that was already a record, Moscow has added another $128 billion.

The figures cover both the public and the classified portions of budget expenditure under the National Defence section, according to the military outlet Militarnyi, which reported the Duma committee's conclusions on the bill. That matters, because it means the published totals already fold in spending that is never openly itemised — the secret lines, the closed procurement programmes, the parts of the budget that outsiders cannot audit. Even so, Russia's actual war-related spending could be higher still, because some military expenses are accounted for under other budget heads entirely, outside the National Defence section.

The timing of the vote is part of the story. It came in the same week that the European Union's leaders spent more than 12 hours in Brussels and failed to agree on the bloc's next long-term budget or on an additional €45 billion loan for Ukraine in 2027 — a deadlock reported by World News Desk in post 121. It came days after President Donald Trump invited President Putin to the G20 summit in Florida on 14–15 December, telling reporters, "If it were up to me, I'd like to have him" — the subject of post 104. And it came as Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelenskyy warned that delays to Patriot missile deliveries are costing Ukrainian lives, the subject of post 130. Moscow's message, written into law, is the opposite of a wind-down: the war economy is not a wartime emergency measure any more. It is the plan.

The numbers, year by year

The three-year plan is worth setting out in full, because the shape of it tells its own story. The heaviest year is the first one, and the spending stays near that peak for the whole period.

In 2027, Russia plans to allocate 17.15 trillion roubles to defence — approximately $208.8 billion, or 6.91 per cent of gross domestic product. That is the record year: the biggest single-year defence budget since the Soviet era, and about 33 per cent higher than the 12.93 trillion roubles, or $157.4 billion, allocated for 2026. One year, one jump of nearly a third.

In 2028, the plan calls for 16.61 trillion roubles, or $202.2 billion — 6.3 per cent of GDP. In 2029, it calls for 16.30 trillion roubles, or $198.4 billion — 5.79 per cent of GDP. The decline across the three years is gentle, a little over 5 per cent from the 2027 peak to the 2029 figure, and the 2029 number is still more than $40 billion above what Russia is spending on defence this year.

Added together, the three years come to 50.05 trillion roubles, or about $609.3 billion. That is the figure that has travelled around the world this week: $609 billion, three years, one purpose. Defence24, the Polish defence outlet, put it bluntly: it will be Russia's largest defence budget since the collapse of the Soviet Union, and total expenditure in the Russian Federation's budget next year will amount to $593.3 billion, meaning that as much as 35 per cent of all budget spending will go directly towards national defence.

For scale, consider what $208.8 billion in a single year means. It is more than the entire annual defence budget of every European country except, arguably, the largest spenders combined in some groupings — and it is being spent by a country whose total federal budget is only $593.3 billion. The military is not one priority among many in Moscow's 2027 budget. It is the priority, and everything else — courts, schools, pensions, roads — divides the remaining 65 per cent between them.

There is another way to see the scale. Russia's defence spending in 2027 alone, at 6.91 per cent of GDP, is a share of national output that no large peacetime economy devotes to its military. The United States, the world's biggest military spender in absolute terms, spends a far smaller share of its economy on defence. Russia is spending like a country at war — because it is a country at war, and because its government has now decided, in law, to keep spending that way for three more years.

The $1.3 trillion tally: what the war has already cost

The three-year plan does not stand alone. It sits on top of nearly five years of war spending that has already reached staggering levels — and the combined total is the number that stops people in their tracks.

According to calculations by Janis Kluge, a researcher at the German Institute for International and Security Affairs (SWP), Russia had already spent around 58.3 trillion roubles, or approximately $710 billion at the exchange rates applicable at the time, on military needs since the invasion of Ukraine began in February 2022. That figure includes both publicly disclosed and classified expenditure under the National Defence budget section. It is, by itself, one of the largest military spending totals any country has accumulated in so short a period in modern history.

Add the new three-year plan, and the tally climbs fast. Under the budget plans, Russia's cumulative military spending since the invasion will reach 75.4 trillion roubles, or $918 billion, in 2027 alone. If the allocations for 2027–29 are fully implemented, the cumulative total could exceed 108 trillion roubles, or around $1.31 trillion, by the end of 2029. One war, eight years, $1.3 trillion — and that is the conservative count, because, as noted, some military expenses sit under other budget heads and are not included in the National Defence totals at all.

The $1.3 trillion figure is worth pausing on. It is roughly double the total that Russia spent on its military in the entire decade before the invasion, compressed into eight years. It is money that has gone to shells and missiles, to soldiers' pay and sign-up bonuses, to new factories and expanded production lines, to replacing the equipment destroyed on Ukrainian battlefields. Every rouble of it is a rouble that did not go to a hospital, a school, a road, or a pension.

And the spending is not abstract. The war it funds is still killing people every day. In the same week the Duma approved the budget, Russia launched 155 drones at Ukraine overnight, including 83 jet-powered Shahed drones; Ukrainian air defences downed 110, but strikes hit 27 locations and one man was killed in Zaporizhzhia. Estonia issued an air-threat warning and scrambled NATO jets over possible drone activity. The budget Moscow has just approved is the budget that pays for those drones, those missiles, and the next wave after them.

There is also a question the numbers raise but cannot answer: how long can Russia keep this up? A defence share of 35.2 per cent of federal spending, and 6.91 per cent of GDP, is the kind of burden that peacetime economies do not carry for long. But the Duma's vote is an answer in itself — Moscow intends to try, for at least three more years, and it has written that intention into law.

What gets cut to pay for it

A budget is a choice, and the other side of Russia's choice is visible in the same document. According to The Moscow Times, whose reporting on the budget was cited by The New Voice of Ukraine and others, the increase in military spending will be accompanied by cuts in funding for a number of civilian sectors. Healthcare, housing and social welfare are among the areas facing reduced allocations, according to Outlook India's summary of the budget.

This is the part of the story that does not make the headlines as often as the $609 billion figure, but it is the part that ordinary Russians will feel first. When a third of the federal budget goes to the military, the money for everything else has to come from somewhere, and the somewhere is the civilian economy: the clinics, the housing programmes, the welfare payments that millions of people depend on.

The pattern is not new. Russia's war economy has been squeezing civilian spending since 2022, and each successive budget has pushed the military share higher. What is new is the scale and the duration: three years, locked in, at record levels, with civilian cuts written into the same plan. The Duma has not just approved a big military budget for next year. It has approved a structure in which the military comes first and everything else fits around it, through 2029.

The human side of that choice is hard to quantify from outside, because Russia's budget documents do not publish the local detail — which clinics lose funding, which housing projects are shelved, which welfare payments shrink. But the direction is unambiguous, and the scale of the shift is now a matter of public record: defence up by $43.6 billion in a single year against earlier projections, civilian sectors cut to help pay for it.

There is a political logic to it as well. A government that has staked its legitimacy on a war cannot afford to look as though it is winding the war down, and a budget is the most concrete statement of intent a government makes. By raising defence spending by a third in one year and locking in three years at that level, the Kremlin is telling its own population, its own military, and the outside world the same thing: this war is not ending soon, and Russia is prepared to pay whatever it costs to keep fighting it.

What it means for Ukraine — and for Europe

For Ukraine, the budget is grim news delivered in the plainest possible form. Kyiv's war effort depends on Western aid, and that aid is itself under strain. European Union leaders spent more than 12 hours in Brussels this week and failed to agree on the bloc's 2028–2034 budget or on an additional €45 billion loan for Ukraine in 2027. Twenty-four states backed the loan, but no final decision will come until December at the earliest. Hungary's Prime Minister Peter Magyar said after the emergency summit that Hungary will reject any seven-year budget deal that cuts its funding and raises its contributions, and that he sees little chance of a December compromise.

Meanwhile, President Zelenskyy has warned of renewed Russian attacks against Ukraine's electricity network, and in a BBC interview on 27 November he described President Trump's habit of calling President Putin "my friend" as "strange" — "like calling Hitler a friend" — while complaining that delays to Patriot missile deliveries are costing Ukrainian lives. That interview was the subject of World News Desk post 130. The contrast could hardly be sharper: as Kyiv waits for air defences and Europe argues over a loan, Moscow has just guaranteed its own war funding for three years.

The budget also lands in a week of wider Russian pressure on Europe. Research from the International Institute for Strategic Studies, highlighted by Reuters on 27 November, found that recorded Russian sabotage incidents in Europe increased by 16 per cent during the year ending September 2026. Estonia scrambled NATO jets this week over possible drone activity. The pattern — military spending up, sabotage up, pressure up — is the backdrop against which European governments are trying to decide how much more they can spend on their own defence, and how fast.

For Europe's leaders, the arithmetic is uncomfortable. Russia will spend $208.8 billion on defence in 2027. The European Union's member states, taken together, spend far more than that in absolute terms — but they spend it through 27 separate national budgets, with 27 separate procurement systems, and with a political process that has just failed, after 12 hours of talks, to agree on a €45 billion loan. Moscow's advantage is not money. It is unity of purpose, written into a single budget, voted through in a single parliament, without a veto to block it.

That is the strategic meaning of the $609 billion figure. It is not just a big number. It is a statement that Russia's war economy is now a settled, long-term fact — and that anyone planning for the war to end because Russia runs out of money will have to wait until at least 2030.

Trump, Putin and the diplomacy that isn't working — yet

The budget's approval comes at a curious moment in the diplomacy around the war. President Trump has spent the past weeks pressing President Putin to scale back military aggression across Europe, and he has said he asked Putin to "pull back" — the subject of recent reporting covered in World News Desk's daily coverage. At the same time, Trump has invited Putin to the G20 summit in Florida on 14–15 December, saying, "If it were up to me, I'd like to have him." The two gestures sit side by side: pressure and invitation, in the same week, from the same president.

Moscow's budget is, in effect, its reply to the pressure. A government that was preparing to wind down a war would not raise its defence budget by a third and lock in three years at record levels. A government that was preparing to negotiate from a position of strength, on the other hand, would do exactly this: guarantee the money, signal endurance, and arrive at any summit with the war funded through 2029.

That does not mean diplomacy is dead. It means the diplomacy, if it happens, will happen against a backdrop in which Russia has already paid for the next three years of fighting. The budget removes one of the West's quiet hopes — that economic pressure would force Moscow to the table — and replaces it with a harder reality: Russia can afford this war, on its own terms, for years, and it has just voted to do so.

There is also the question of what the money actually buys on the battlefield. The budget's public lines do not break down procurement in detail — much of it is classified — but the scale of the increase, $43.6 billion more in 2027 than previously planned, points to a military that is still expanding its production of the weapons it uses every day in Ukraine: the drones, the missiles, the artillery shells, and the air-defence systems. The 155-drone overnight barrage this week, including 83 jet-powered Shaheds, is the product of exactly that production base. The budget guarantees it keeps growing.

For the United States, the budget poses a direct question about the value of its own leverage. Washington has tools — sanctions, export controls, the threat of secondary measures against buyers of Russian oil — but the Duma's vote suggests Moscow believes it can absorb them. The Kremlin's bet is that a war economy, once built, can sustain itself, and that the political cost of ending the war is higher than the economic cost of continuing it. The $609 billion is the price tag on that bet.

Why this budget matters more than the last one

Russia has approved big military budgets before. What makes this one different is the combination of three things: the size, the share, and the duration. The size — $609 billion over three years — is the largest since the Soviet era. The share — 35.2 per cent of all federal spending — means the military now takes more than a third of everything the state does. And the duration — three years, locked in by law — means this is not a one-off surge but a settled direction of travel.

Consider the trajectory. In 2026, defence was 12.93 trillion roubles, or $157.4 billion. In 2027, it jumps to 17.15 trillion roubles, or $208.8 billion — a rise of about a third in a single year. In 2028 and 2029 it stays above $198 billion. The war economy is not tapering. It is stepping up, and then holding at the higher level.

The comparison with the previous three-year plan makes the same point. The new plan is about 10.5 trillion roubles, or $127.8 billion, bigger than the one it replaces. Even the plan that was already a record was not big enough for the war Moscow is fighting, so Moscow made it bigger — by more than the entire annual defence budget of most countries on Earth.

There is a final number that captures the shift. If the plan is fully implemented, Russia will have spent more than $1.3 trillion on its military since February 2022 by the end of 2029. That is the cost of the war so far, counted in roubles and converted to dollars, and it is still rising by roughly $200 billion a year. It is a figure that will define Russia's economy, its politics, and its place in the world for a generation — and it was approved, in a single vote, on a Tuesday in November.

What to watch next

The budget now goes to the Federation Council, Russia's upper house, and then to President Putin for signature — formalities, in practice, for a bill the Kremlin itself drafted. The real tests come later, and they are worth watching.

First, watch whether the money is actually spent as planned. Russia's budgets have a habit of being revised mid-year, and the classified portions are impossible to verify from outside. The 2027 figure of 17.15 trillion roubles is a plan, not a receipt. But the direction has been consistent for five years: up, every year, by more than planned.

Second, watch the civilian economy. The cuts to healthcare, housing and social welfare that accompany this budget will show up in Russian life gradually — in longer hospital waits, in shelved housing projects, in tighter welfare budgets. How much ordinary Russians notice, and how much they blame the war, is a political question that matters as much as the military one.

Third, watch Europe. The EU's December summit is now the deadline for the €45 billion Ukraine loan and the long-term budget, and the failure in Brussels this week has made it the most important meeting on the continent's calendar. If Europe cannot agree to fund Ukraine while Russia funds its own war at $200 billion a year, the imbalance will only grow.

Fourth, watch the G20 in Florida on 14–15 December, and whether President Putin attends at President Trump's invitation. A summit with the war funded through 2029 is a different negotiation from a summit with a government running out of money. Moscow will arrive knowing it can fight on. The question is what, if anything, it is willing to trade for stopping.

And finally, watch Ukraine's air defences. The Patriot missiles that President Zelenskyy says are delayed are the difference between a drone barrage that kills one person in Zaporizhzhia and a barrage that kills many more. Russia's budget guarantees the barrages will keep coming. Whether Ukraine gets the defences to stop them is now the most urgent question of the war — and it is a question for Washington and Brussels, not Moscow.

Russia has made its choice, and it has put a number on it: $609 billion, three years, one war. The rest of the world now has to decide what its own number is.
