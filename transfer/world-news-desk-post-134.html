TITLE: Black Friday 2026: Americans Spent a Record $7.1 Billion on Thanksgiving — $12.9 Billion More Today LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Thanksgiving online spending hit a record $7.1B, up 10.4%. Adobe expects $12.9B on Black Friday and a $275.1B holiday season — the biggest ever. ---BODY---

Black Friday shoppers crowd outside the Apple Store on Fifth Avenue, New York City. Americans spent a record $7.1 billion online on Thanksgiving Day 2026, and Adobe Analytics expects $12.9 billion more on Black Friday alone. Photo: via Wikimedia Commons.

Americans have already spent a record amount of money before Black Friday even began. Shoppers spent $7.1 billion online on Thanksgiving Day alone — a record for the holiday, up 10.4 per cent from last year — and the spending spree is only getting started. Adobe Analytics expects $12.9 billion in online purchases on Black Friday itself, up 9.2 per cent year on year, and $15.1 billion on Cyber Monday, 30 November. Add it all up, and the five days from Thanksgiving to Cyber Monday — the stretch the industry calls Cyber Week — are forecast to bring in $47.5 billion online, up 7.4 per cent, inside a holiday season that is set to cross $275 billion for the first time ever.

It is a remarkable set of numbers for a year in which almost everything was supposed to work against the American shopper. Prices are high. Tariffs have pushed up the cost of imported goods. A nor'easter battered the East Coast over Thanksgiving, knocking out power to roughly 961,000 customers and forcing New Jersey to declare a state of emergency. And yet the spending did not slow down. It accelerated — online, at least — and the records started falling before the turkey was cold.

The Thanksgiving figure, reported by Adobe Analytics and covered by Seeking Alpha, is the clearest sign of the shift. Shoppers, the firm found, worked off their Thanksgiving meals online, spending a record $7.1 billion, up 10.4 per cent from last year. Pulled-forward discounts, strong demand for gaming and electronics, and AI shopping bots all contributed to the record, according to the report. People did not wait for Friday. They started on Thursday, from their sofas, on their phones — and they spent more than ever before.

The forecast for the rest of the weekend is just as striking. Black Friday, which falls on 27 November this year, is expected to generate nearly $13 billion in US online purchases, up 9.2 per cent from a year earlier. Online sales on Cyber Monday, 30 November, could reach $15.1 billion, up 6.2 per cent — the first single day in American history expected to pass $15 billion in online spending. Consumers are forecast to spend about $47.5 billion over the five days from Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday, a figure that represents 17.3 per cent of the entire holiday season's online spending.

And the season itself is the biggest on record. Adobe expects US online sales to hit $275.1 billion between 1 November and 31 December 2026, growing 6.7 per cent year on year. The National Retail Federation, the industry's trade body, forecasts that total US holiday retail sales — online and in stores combined — will exceed $1 trillion in November and December. One trillion dollars, in two months, in one country. That is the scale of what American shoppers are doing right now.

The numbers behind the record

The headline figures tell the story, but the detail underneath them tells it better. Adobe's forecast, published on 28 September and based on more than a trillion visits to US retail sites, 100 million products and 18 product categories, breaks the season down day by day — and almost every day is a record.

Thanksgiving itself: $7.1 billion online, up 10.4 per cent, a record. Black Friday: $12.9 billion expected, up 9.2 per cent. Cyber Monday: $15.1 billion expected, up 6.2 per cent, and the biggest online shopping day of the season. Cyber Week as a whole: $47.5 billion, up 7.4 per cent. The full holiday season: $275.1 billion, up 6.7 per cent. Every one of those numbers is higher than last year's, and last year's numbers were themselves records: $11.8 billion online on Black Friday 2025, up 9.1 per cent; $44.2 billion for Cyber Week, up 7.7 per cent; $257.8 billion for the season, up 6.8 per cent. Five consecutive record years, climbing from $8.9 billion on Black Friday in 2021 to a forecast $12.9 billion in 2026.

Bain & Company widens the frame further. The consultancy expects Black Friday through Cyber Monday sales to top $85 billion, up 8.75 per cent and equal to 8.5 per cent of all holiday spending, with more than 90 per cent of consumers planning to shop the events. That $85 billion covers online and in-store spending together — the full weight of the American consumer, concentrated into four days.

There are records inside the records, too. Buy now, pay later — the financing that lets shoppers split a purchase into instalments — is forecast to drive $21.3 billion in US holiday online purchases this season, $1.3 billion more than in 2025. On Black Friday alone, buy now, pay later is set to drive $807 million in online spend. On Thanksgiving Day itself, it handled $747.5 million. Shoppers are not just spending more. They are borrowing more to do it, in smaller and smaller pieces, spread across more and more purchases.

And then there is the phone. Mobile is now the default checkout surface: 57.4 per cent of 2026 holiday e-commerce spend is forecast to happen on phones, and that share rises to 63 per cent during Cyber Week. The desktop computer, once the home of online shopping, is now the minority device. The American shopper buys from the sofa, from the train, from the queue outside the store — and, increasingly, from an app that found the deal for them before they even looked.

The robots are doing the shopping now

The most striking change in this year's numbers is not how much people are spending. It is who — or what — is doing the spending for them. Artificial intelligence has arrived in the checkout, and the figures are startling.

Adobe forecasts that AI-referred traffic to retail sites will run 130 per cent above last year for the season as a whole — up 159 per cent on Thanksgiving, 95 per cent on Black Friday, and 88 per cent on Cyber Monday. On Black Friday itself, AI-driven traffic to retail sites climbed 805 per cent, according to figures cited in industry coverage. Salesforce figures reported by the US Chamber of Commerce put $3 billion of US online sales on Black Friday alone through AI and AI agents, converting at eight times the rate of conventional traffic. Eight times. A shopper who arrives at a store through an AI assistant is eight times more likely to buy than one who arrives on their own.

What does that mean in practice? It means shoppers are asking chatbots and AI assistants to find deals, compare prices, and sometimes complete purchases on their behalf. It means the traditional journey — search, browse, compare, decide — is being compressed into a conversation: tell the assistant what you want, and it finds the cheapest option. And it means retailers are racing to be the store the AI recommends, because the AI's recommendation is increasingly the sale.

The shift is visible in the numbers from the year's earlier sales events. During Amazon's Prime Big Deal Days in October, online shoppers spent $9.86 billion, up 8.5 per cent year on year, across two days — and AI traffic was already surging. The pattern has only accelerated into the holiday season. The $3 billion that Salesforce attributes to AI and AI agents on Black Friday is not a footnote. It is nearly a quarter of the day's entire online total.

For retailers, the message is blunt: the customer is no longer only the person. The customer is also the software the person uses, and the software shops differently. It does not browse. It does not impulse-buy. It compares, it calculates, and it picks the best price — and it does so at a scale and speed no human can match. The stores that win this holiday season will be the ones the machines choose, and the stores that lose will be the ones the machines skip.

There is a second, quieter revolution in the same numbers: the discounts themselves. Adobe expects the deepest discounts of the season to land between Thanksgiving and Cyber Monday, with Black Friday the peak day for deals on televisions, apparel, appliances, toys and furniture. The discounts are the bait, and they are working — but they are also, as Poland Insight noted in its coverage, putting pressure on retailers' margins, as promotion costs rise and new customs charges reshape competition. Record sales do not automatically mean record profits, and the stores selling $12.9 billion of goods on Friday are doing so at prices they cut to the bone to win the sale.

Why shoppers are spending — and what it costs them

The obvious question is why. Why are Americans spending record amounts in a year of high prices, tariffs, and a storm that knocked out power to nearly a million homes? The answer, as always, is a mix of forces pulling in the same direction.

First, the discounts came early. Amazon's Black Friday Week kicked off on 19 November and runs through Cyber Monday, giving the season its first major catalyst more than a week before the day itself. Retailers across the industry pulled their promotions forward, and shoppers responded by pulling their spending forward too — which is why Thanksgiving Day, traditionally a day off from shopping, produced a record $7.1 billion online. The deals did not wait for Friday, so neither did the shoppers.

Second, the categories people want most are the ones with the deepest discounts. Gaming and electronics drove the Thanksgiving record, according to Adobe's analysis, and televisions, apparel and appliances carry the biggest Black Friday cuts. When the thing you have been waiting to buy is 30 or 40 per cent cheaper than it was last month, the decision makes itself — even if everything else is more expensive.

Third, the economy itself is sending mixed signals that happen to favour spending. Wall Street has just had a strong run — the Nasdaq rose 1.58 per cent and the S&P 500 rose 1.11 per cent in the session reported in World News Desk post 123, with the Dow closing at 50,544.06 — and the US economy grew 4.3 per cent in the third quarter, its fastest pace in three years, as reported in post 52. People who feel richer spend more, even when the prices in front of them are higher than they were a year ago.

But there is a harder side to the story, and it is written in the buy now, pay later numbers. Shoppers are forecast to finance $21.3 billion of holiday purchases through instalment plans this season — $1.3 billion more than last year, and more than an additional Cyber Monday's worth of instalment spending. On Black Friday alone, $807 million will go through buy now, pay later. That is not the behaviour of shoppers who are comfortably flush. It is the behaviour of shoppers who want the deal, need the purchase, and cannot — or would rather not — pay for it all at once.

The same tension shows up in the broader retail picture. Total US retail sales on Black Friday last year rose only 3 per cent, to $18 billion, while online sales rose 9.1 per cent to $11.8 billion — e-commerce captured nearly all of the incremental spend. The stores, in other words, are not growing. The internet is. The record is real, but it is a record for online shopping, driven by discounts, by phones, by AI, and by instalment credit — and the high street, the physical store, is increasingly a showroom for a purchase that happens on a screen.

There is also the tariff question. President Trump's new tariffs, announced this week and covering 14 countries, with China facing 20 per cent on top of 25 per cent — the subject of post 45 — have raised the cost of imported goods, and retailers have warned that prices will rise further. Some of this week's spending may be shoppers buying now, before the tariffs bite harder. If so, the record is partly a rush to beat the price rises — a pull-forward of spending that would have happened later, at higher prices. That would make the coming months weaker, not stronger, as the shoppers who bought in November have nothing left to buy in January.

Amazon, Walmart and the trillion-dollar season

Behind the consumer numbers sit the companies that take the money, and their results tell the same story of a season concentrating into fewer, bigger hands. Amazon ended the third quarter of 2026 with revenue of $201.9 billion, up 12 per cent year on year — a figure that shows the scale of the machine the holiday season now feeds. Its Black Friday Week, running from 19 November through Cyber Monday, is the longest promotional event in the company's history, and it sets the pace for the entire industry.

Shopify, the platform that powers hundreds of thousands of smaller online stores, tells the other side of the story. Its merchants alone booked $14.6 billion across Black Friday and Cyber Monday last year, up 27 per cent — and the company is projected to cross $2 trillion in cumulative historical gross merchandise volume around the end of 2026, with Black Friday sales momentum playing a central role. The independent store is not dead. It is selling through Shopify, on a phone, to a customer an AI assistant sent it.

The scale of the season is hard to grasp in the abstract, so consider it this way. Last year, shoppers spent about $5.1 million per minute online on Black Friday. At that rate, a single minute of Black Friday shopping buys more than most Americans earn in a lifetime. This year, at $12.9 billion for the day, the rate will be higher still — roughly $9 million a minute, every minute, for 24 hours. The money moves faster than any person can follow, and it moves, increasingly, without a person at all.

The National Retail Federation's forecast — more than $1 trillion in total holiday retail sales in November and December — puts the season in its proper context. A trillion dollars is roughly the annual economic output of a country like the Netherlands or Indonesia, spent by one country's shoppers in two months. It is the largest consumer event in human history, repeated every year, and this year it is the largest it has ever been.

But the concentration matters, too. The $1 trillion is not spread evenly. It flows to the biggest online platforms, the largest retailers, and the stores the AI assistants recommend. The small shop on Main Street, the independent retailer without a website, the store that closes on Thanksgiving — they see little of it. The record is a record for the system as a whole, and the system is increasingly a handful of giant platforms and the millions of small sellers who rent space on them.

What comes next: Cyber Monday, and the bill

Black Friday is only the middle of the story. Cyber Monday, on 30 November, is expected to be the biggest online shopping day of the season — $15.1 billion, up 6.2 per cent, the first single day in American history to pass $15 billion online. It will be the peak of the $47.5 billion Cyber Week, and the day on which the season's records are either confirmed or broken.

After that, the season runs to 31 December, and the total will land somewhere around $275.1 billion online — up 6.7 per cent, and the biggest holiday season ever recorded. The retailers will count their sales, the analysts will count the margins, and the shoppers will count the instalments. The buy now, pay later bill for the season — $21.3 billion — will come due in the months that follow, in weekly and monthly payments that stretch into the spring.

That is the part of the story that will not be visible on Friday, while the records are being celebrated. A record built partly on instalment credit is a record with a cost, and the cost arrives later. The shoppers who financed their Black Friday will be paying for it in January, February and March, when the discounts are gone and the prices are higher and the tariffs have had time to bite. Whether they can keep paying — and keep spending — is the question that will decide whether 2027 starts with a boom or a hangover.

For now, though, the numbers are what they are, and they are extraordinary. Thanksgiving: a record $7.1 billion, up 10.4 per cent. Black Friday: $12.9 billion expected, up 9.2 per cent. Cyber Monday: $15.1 billion expected, the first $15 billion day in history. Cyber Week: $47.5 billion. The season: $275.1 billion. The total: more than $1 trillion. The shopper: on a phone, through an AI, paying in instalments, and spending more than ever before.

The tills are ringing. The question is who, in the end, pays for the ring.

There is one more number worth remembering as the weekend unfolds. In 2021, Black Friday online sales were $8.9 billion. Five years later, the forecast is $12.9 billion — a rise of 45 per cent in half a decade, through a pandemic, an inflation surge, a war in Europe, and a tariff shock. The American shopper has absorbed every one of those blows and kept spending, each year a little more than the last. That resilience is the real story of Black Friday 2026: not the discounts, not the robots, not the instalments, but a consumer who refuses to stop. Whether that refusal is strength or stubbornness, wisdom or habit, will be decided by the bills that arrive in the new year. For today, the record stands — $7.1 billion on Thanksgiving, $12.9 billion expected on Black Friday, and a season that has never been bigger.
