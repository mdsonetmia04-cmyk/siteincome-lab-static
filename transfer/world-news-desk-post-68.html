TITLE: Ukraine Just Hit Russia's Savasleyka Air Base 300 km From Moscow — 5 Warplanes Knocked Out, Including 3 MiG-31s LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Ukraine says it struck Russia's Savasleyka air base, damaging three MiG-31 jets and two Su-34 bombers used to attack Ukraine ---BODY---

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy of Ukraine. Kyiv said on November 25, 2026, that Ukrainian forces had struck the Savasleyka air base deep inside Russia, damaging five warplanes used to launch missiles and bombs at Ukraine. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

Ukrainian forces have struck a major Russian military air base deep inside Russia, damaging five warplanes used to launch missiles and bombs at Ukraine, Kyiv's General Staff said on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, in one of the most audacious long-range attacks of the war. The Savasleyka air base, in the Nizhny Novgorod region, about 300 kilometres east of Moscow, was hit in the latest operation aimed at crippling Moscow's war effort, the General Staff said, and the damage, by its account, was severe. Three MiG-31 jets — the aircraft that carry Russia's Kinzhal hypersonic missiles — and two Su-34 fighter-bombers were knocked out of operation, the General Staff said, and all five, by its preliminary assessment, will be unable to fly for some time. Russia did not immediately comment on the report, and the full extent of the damage, and the final number of aircraft hit, were still being assessed. But the message, from Kyiv, was unmistakable: nowhere in Russia, not even 300 kilometres from the Kremlin, is safe from Ukraine's long-range reach.

The strikes, according to Ukraine's General Staff, were carried out on November 24 and overnight into November 25, and they were, by Kyiv's account, part of a deliberate and sustained campaign, a campaign that has, in recent months, targeted not only the front line but the military and energy infrastructure deep inside Russia, the airfields, the refineries, the depots, and the factories that keep Moscow's war machine running. The Savasleyka strike was, in that sense, not an isolated raid but a piece of a larger strategy, and it was a piece that, if Kyiv's account is correct, removed, in a single night, five of the aircraft that Russia uses to launch some of its most dangerous weapons at Ukrainian cities.

The base, and the planes that were hit

Savasleyka is not a household name, and it is not, to most people outside Russia, a familiar place. But to military analysts, and to the Ukrainian General Staff, it is a base that matters, and it matters for a specific reason: it is one of the homes of Russia's MiG-31 fleet, and the MiG-31 is, in the current war, one of the most important aircraft in the Russian inventory. The MiG-31, a long-range interceptor, is the only Russian aircraft that carries the Kinzhal, the hypersonic missile that Russia has used, repeatedly, to strike Ukrainian cities, and that travels, by Moscow's account, at many times the speed of sound, and is, by the account of Western analysts, one of the hardest weapons in Russia's arsenal to intercept. Three of those aircraft, by Kyiv's account, were damaged at Savasleyka, and put out of operation, and that, if it is confirmed, is a significant blow to Russia's ability to launch Kinzhal strikes.

The other two aircraft that were hit were Su-34 fighter-bombers, the workhorses of the Russian air force, the aircraft that carry the guided bombs that have, in recent months, devastated Ukrainian towns and cities along the front line, and that have, in the words of Ukrainian officials, become one of the most feared weapons in the war. The Su-34, which flies from bases across western Russia, is, by any measure, a central part of Moscow's air campaign, and the loss of two of them, even temporarily, is a loss that matters. The General Staff said that all five damaged aircraft will be unable to fly for some time, and that the extent of the damage, and the final number of aircraft hit, were still being assessed, which is a reminder that the first reports from a strike like this are always preliminary, and that the true toll may be higher, or lower, than the figures that were announced on Wednesday.

300 kilometres from Moscow — and what that means

The distance matters, and it matters for a reason that goes beyond the military arithmetic of how many planes were damaged. Savasleyka is about 300 kilometres east of Moscow, and it is, by any measure, deep inside Russia, far from the front line, and far from the border with Ukraine. For Ukraine to strike it, and to strike it successfully, is a demonstration of a capability that, a year ago, would have seemed impossible, and it is a demonstration that will be noted, in Moscow, and in Washington, and in every capital in Europe, as evidence of how far Ukraine's long-range strike capability has come. The drones, and the missiles, that Kyiv has developed, and that it has deployed, in recent months, can now reach targets that were, until recently, considered safe, and that changes, in a fundamental way, the geography of the war.

It is, in that sense, a strike that is about more than five aircraft. It is about the fact that Russia's airfields, and its refineries, and its depots, are no longer sanctuaries, and that the war, which Moscow began, in February 2022, with the expectation of a swift victory, has now come home, in a very literal sense, to the Russian heartland. The Kremlin, which did not immediately comment on the Savasleyka report, has, in the past, dismissed Ukrainian claims of long-range strikes, or minimised them, or blamed them on debris, or on accidents. But the satellite imagery that has emerged, in recent days, of other strikes, and the reports from Russian social media, and the sheer accumulation of claims, have made it harder and harder for Moscow to pretend that the war is something that happens only in Ukraine, and only to Ukrainians.

A campaign, not a raid

The Savasleyka strike was, by Kyiv's account, part of a wider campaign, and the other pieces of that campaign were, on Wednesday, also becoming clear. In the same period, Ukraine struck two oil refineries 800 kilometres from the front line, according to reports, in a continuation of the campaign against Russia's energy infrastructure that has, in recent months, become one of the most effective parts of Kyiv's strategy. The refineries, like the airfields, are targets that matter, not only because they produce the fuel that keeps Russia's war machine running, but because they produce the revenue that keeps the Russian state running, and because every refinery that is hit is a refinery that cannot, for a time, do either.

That campaign, and the logic behind it, is worth understanding, because it is, in the end, the logic of a war that has become, for Ukraine, a war of attrition, and a war in which the side that can sustain its military effort, and its economy, for longer, will win. Ukraine cannot, and does not try to, match Russia plane for plane, or tank for tank, or soldier for soldier. What it can do, and what it has done, with increasing effectiveness, is to strike at the infrastructure that makes Russia's war possible, at the airfields, the refineries, the depots, and the factories, and to do so, again and again, until the cost of the war, for Moscow, becomes unsustainable. The Savasleyka strike, and the refinery strikes that accompanied it, are, in that sense, not isolated events but pieces of a strategy, and a strategy that is, by all accounts, working.

The other side of the war

But the war, as ever, has two sides, and the same day that Kyiv announced the Savasleyka strike, Russia, by its own account, was also striking, and was also killing. According to reports, Russia killed ten people in fresh attacks on Ukraine, in the same period, a reminder that for all of Kyiv's successes in striking deep inside Russia, the war, for Ukrainians, remains, above all, a war of survival, a war in which Russian missiles and drones fall, nightly, on Ukrainian cities, and in which the cost, in civilian lives, continues to rise. The Savasleyka strike, and the refinery strikes, do not change that, and they do not, in themselves, end the war. What they do, and what they are intended to do, is to make the war more expensive for Russia, and to make it harder for Moscow to sustain the campaign of bombardment that has, for nearly five years, been the central fact of Ukrainian life.

That is, in the end, the calculus of this war, and it is a calculus that is, by its nature, grim. Every MiG-31 that is knocked out of operation is a MiG-31 that cannot launch a Kinzhal at a Ukrainian city. Every Su-34 that is damaged is a Su-34 that cannot drop a guided bomb on a Ukrainian town. Every refinery that is hit is a refinery that cannot produce the fuel, or the revenue, that keeps the war going. The arithmetic is brutal, and it is, for both sides, a matter of endurance, of who can keep going, and who can keep absorbing losses, for longer. The Savasleyka strike, if Kyiv's account is correct, tips that arithmetic, by a small amount, in Ukraine's favour. But it is, by itself, only a small amount, and the war, which has now lasted nearly five years, will not be decided by a single strike, however audacious, however deep inside Russia it was carried out.

The chemical weapons question

The Savasleyka strike came in the same week as another development, one that, in a different way, also speaks to the conduct of the war, and to the rules that govern it. Officials from 34 countries, and the European Union, called on Russia on Wednesday to stop using riot-control gas on the battlefield, after the global chemical weapons watchdog confirmed that Ukraine had found traces of tear gas in a trench that Kyiv said had been hit with a Russian grenade. The Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons, the body that oversees the Chemical Weapons Convention, had, by its account, confirmed the presence of the agent, and the 34 countries, in a joint statement, called on Moscow to halt the practice, and to comply with its obligations under the Convention, which prohibits the use of chemical weapons, including riot-control agents, as a method of warfare.

That call, and the confirmation that prompted it, are, in their way, as significant as the Savasleyka strike, because they speak to a different kind of escalation, and to a different kind of risk. The use of chemical weapons, even in the form of tear gas, is a line that, once crossed, is hard to uncross, and it is a line that the international community has, for a century, treated as inviolable. The fact that 34 countries felt it necessary to call on Russia to stop is, in itself, a measure of how seriously the allegation is being taken, and a measure of how concerned the world is that the war, which has already broken so many rules, might break this one, too. Russia, which has denied using chemical weapons, has not, as of Wednesday, responded to the call in any substantive way.

What Kyiv is saying — and what Moscow is not

The contrast between Kyiv's announcement and Moscow's silence is, in itself, a story. Ukraine's General Staff announced the Savasleyka strike in a public statement, gave the numbers, named the aircraft, and described the operation, in broad terms, as part of a campaign to cripple Moscow's war effort. Russia, by contrast, did not immediately comment, and has, in the past, responded to Ukrainian claims of long-range strikes with silence, or with denials, or with claims that the damage was minimal, or that the aircraft were decoys, or that the strike had been intercepted. That pattern is, by now, familiar, and it is a pattern that reflects, in part, the difficulty that Moscow faces in acknowledging that Ukraine can strike, successfully, deep inside Russia, and in part, the simple fact that the Kremlin does not like to admit, in public, that it has been hit.

But the silence, and the denials, have become harder to sustain, as the evidence has accumulated, and as the satellite imagery, and the social media reports, and the sheer number of claims, have made it clear that Ukraine's long-range campaign is real, and that it is effective, and that it is, if anything, accelerating. The Savasleyka strike, and the refinery strikes, and the strikes that preceded them, are, taken together, a body of evidence that is difficult to dismiss, and that points, inescapably, to a conclusion: that Ukraine can now strike, at will, at targets deep inside Russia, and that Russia, for all its air defences, and for all its size, cannot stop it. That is a conclusion that will, in Moscow, be deeply uncomfortable, and that will, in Kyiv, be a source of considerable satisfaction.

The war, five years on

It is worth, at this point, stepping back, and remembering how long this war has lasted, and how much it has cost, and how far it has come from the swift victory that Moscow expected in February 2022. Nearly five years have passed since Russian tanks crossed the border, and in that time the war has become the largest conflict in Europe since the Second World War, and it has killed, by the most conservative estimates, hundreds of thousands of people, and displaced millions more, and devastated cities, and towns, and villages, across Ukraine, and it has, in the process, transformed the politics of Europe, and the economics of the world, and the military doctrines of every major power. And it has, in the last year, become a war in which Ukraine, the smaller, weaker side, has learned to strike back, deep inside Russia, at the airfields, and the refineries, and the depots, that sustain the invasion.

The Savasleyka strike is, in that long history, a small event, and it will not, by itself, end the war, or change its course, or bring peace any closer. But it is, in its way, a symbol of how the war has changed, and of how the balance of power, in the air, and in the long-range strike domain, has shifted, and of how a country that was expected, in February 2022, to fall in days, has, nearly five years later, learned to hit back, 300 kilometres from Moscow, at the very aircraft that Moscow uses to bomb its cities. That is a remarkable fact, and it is a fact that deserves to be remembered, not only for what it says about this particular strike, but for what it says about the war, and about the country that is fighting it.

The Kinzhal, and why three MiG-31s matter

To understand why the Savasleyka strike matters, it helps to understand the weapon that the three damaged MiG-31s exist to carry. The Kinzhal, which Russia calls a hypersonic missile, is one of the most feared weapons in Moscow's arsenal, and it is feared for reasons that are, by now, well understood. It travels, by Moscow's account, at many times the speed of sound, and it is, by the account of Western analysts, one of the hardest missiles in the world to intercept, because it flies so fast, and so low, and because it manoeuvres in flight, in ways that make it difficult for air defences to track, and to hit. Russia has used the Kinzhal, repeatedly, to strike Ukrainian cities, and to strike targets that, by Moscow's account, are of high military value, and the missile has, in the process, become a symbol of the war, and of the terror that it inflicts on civilians, who have, in many cases, only minutes of warning before it arrives.

The MiG-31 is the only Russian aircraft that carries the Kinzhal, and that fact, by itself, makes the loss of three of them significant. Russia does not have an unlimited supply of MiG-31s, and it does not have an unlimited supply of the modified aircraft, the MiG-31K, that are configured to carry the missile, and every one that is knocked out of operation is one that cannot, for a time, launch a Kinzhal at a Ukrainian city. The General Staff's claim, that all five damaged aircraft will be unable to fly for some time, is, if it is confirmed, a claim that matters, not only because of the numbers, but because of what those numbers represent: a reduction, however temporary, in Russia's ability to launch one of its most dangerous weapons, and a reduction that was achieved, not by shooting the aircraft down in the air, but by striking them, on the ground, at their own base, 300 kilometres from Moscow.

That is, in the end, the logic of the long-range campaign, and it is a logic that is, by its nature, attritional. Ukraine cannot, and does not try to, destroy Russia's air force in a single strike, or in a single night. What it can do, and what it has done, with increasing effectiveness, is to chip away at it, aircraft by aircraft, base by base, until the cumulative effect becomes significant, and until the cost of the war, for Moscow, becomes unsustainable. The Savasleyka strike, which removed, in a single night, three of the aircraft that carry the Kinzhal, and two of the fighter-bombers that drop the guided bombs, is, in that sense, a piece of a larger arithmetic, and an arithmetic that, over time, and with enough strikes, can change the course of a war.

The peace talks that never came

There is one more piece of context that is worth remembering, because it is the context in which the Savasleyka strike, and the war itself, must be understood. Nearly five years have passed since Russia invaded Ukraine, and in that time there have been talks, and negotiations, and proposals, and ceasefires, and each of them has, in the end, failed, or stalled, or been abandoned, and the war has gone on. The reasons are, by now, well known: Russia demands territory, and Ukraine refuses to give it, and Russia demands neutrality, and Ukraine refuses to accept it, and the two sides, which began the war with irreconcilable aims, have, in five years, found no common ground on which to build a peace. The Savasleyka strike, and the refinery strikes, and the chemical weapons allegations, are, in that light, not the cause of the war's continuation, but its consequence, and they are a reminder that, for as long as the war goes on, it will be fought, not only on the front line, but deep inside Russia, and deep inside Ukraine, and that the cost, in lives, and in infrastructure, and in human suffering, will continue to rise.

That is, in the end, the tragedy of this war, and it is a tragedy that the Savasleyka strike, however audacious, does not resolve. Ukraine can strike, deep inside Russia, and it can damage aircraft, and refineries, and depots, and it can make the war more expensive for Moscow, and it can, in time, make the war unsustainable for Russia. But it cannot, by itself, end the war, and it cannot, by itself, bring peace, and it cannot, by itself, undo the damage that has already been done, to Ukraine, and to its people, and to its cities. That will require a political settlement, and a political settlement will require a willingness, on both sides, to compromise, and that willingness, after nearly five years of war, is, as of today, nowhere to be seen. The Savasleyka strike is, in that sense, a reminder of how far the war has come, and of how far it still has to go, before it ends.
