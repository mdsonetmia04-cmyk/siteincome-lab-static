TITLE: Japan Just Set Aside $353 Billion for Defence — Its Biggest Military Build-Up Since World War II LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Japan plans ¥56.3 trillion ($353 billion) in defence spending over five years from 2027 — a 31% jump, but still far short of what its military wanted. ---BODY---

Sanae Takaichi in 2025. Japan's prime minister is preparing to finalise ¥56.3 trillion ($353 billion) in defence spending over five years from fiscal 2027. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0).

Japan is about to spend more on its military than at any time since the Second World War. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi will soon finalise total defence spending of ¥56.3 trillion — about $353.4 billion — over five years, according to the Asahi Shimbun, citing several government officials. The five-year period starts in fiscal 2027, and the figure will be written into early revisions of the country's three security documents, which are scheduled to be completed by the end of this year. It is the largest military build-up in Japan's post-war history, and it is still not as large as Japan's own military wanted.

The number is a compromise, and the compromise tells the story. Japan's defence establishment, backed by some ruling-party lawmakers, sought ¥80 trillion — about $502 billion — over the same five years. The Finance Ministry proposed a range of ¥50 trillion to ¥60 trillion. The ¥56.3 trillion figure sits inside that range, above the current plan's ¥43 trillion by ¥13.3 trillion, or about 31%, but a full ¥23.7 trillion short of what the generals asked for. The final amount could still be adjusted higher — the Mainichi newspaper has reported that the government is now coordinating around a figure closer to ¥60 trillion — but the shape of the decision is set: a historic increase, delivered grudgingly, against the resistance of a Finance Ministry worried about how to pay for it.

The number, and what it means

Put the figure in context and its scale becomes clear. Under the current five-year plan, which runs to fiscal 2027, Japan is spending ¥43 trillion on defence — itself a record, and the product of a historic decision in December 2022 to double defence spending to 2% of gross domestic product by 2027. The new plan, at ¥56.3 trillion, averages about ¥11.3 trillion a year, against roughly ¥8.7 trillion in the current fiscal year. In dollar terms, Japan would be spending about $70 billion a year on its military — more than Britain or France, and behind only the United States and China.

It is also, by Japanese standards, a political earthquake. For most of the post-war era, Japan held its defence spending to around 1% of GDP, an informal cap that survived for nearly half a century and became part of the country's identity as a pacifist power. The constitution written after the war — Article 9, which renounces war and forbids Japan from maintaining "war potential" — still stands. But the 1% cap is long gone, the 2% target is nearly reached, and the new plan pushes spending further still. A country that once apologised for having an army at all is now debating whether ¥56 trillion is enough.

Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama, asked about the reported figure on Friday 27 November, refused to confirm it — and her answer captured the tension inside the government. Spending, she said, should be determined by Japan's defence needs rather than a pre-set numerical target. It was a careful formulation: neither an endorsement of the ¥56.3 trillion figure nor a rejection of it, but a reminder that the number is a ceiling set by the Finance Ministry, not a floor set by the military.

Why now: China, North Korea and the Taiwan question

The reasons for the build-up are not secret, and the government does not pretend they are. China is the first and largest. Beijing's official defence budget, about 1.81 trillion yuan in 2025 — roughly $249 billion, up 7.2% on the year — is more than three times Japan's annual military spending, and it has grown for three decades without interruption. China's navy is now the largest in the world by number of ships. Its coast guard patrols the waters around the Senkaku Islands, the uninhabited rocks in the East China Sea that Japan administers and China claims, with a regularity that Tokyo describes as an attempt to change the status quo by force. Chinese and Russian warships sail together around Japan's coasts. Chinese aircraft cross into Japan's air defence identification zone hundreds of times a year.

Then there is Taiwan. Japan's southwestern islands — the Nansei chain, running from Kyushu toward Taiwan — sit at the centre of the most dangerous flashpoint in Asia. The island of Yonaguni, Japan's westernmost territory, lies about 110 kilometres from Taiwan. If China were to move against Taiwan, Japan would be involved from the first hours, whether it chose to be or not: American bases in Japan, including the huge facilities on Okinawa, would be part of any US response, and Chinese missiles could reach Japanese territory in minutes. The government in Tokyo has said, plainly, that a Taiwan emergency would be an emergency for Japan. The defence plan is built around that judgement.

North Korea is the third reason, and the most immediate. Pyongyang's missile programme has advanced to the point where its rockets can reach any part of Japan, and it tests them regularly — sometimes over Japanese territory, as it did in 2022 when a missile flew over Hokkaido and triggered emergency alerts across northern Japan. Kim Jong Un, whose nuclear ambitions were the subject of a separate report this week — Seoul is monitoring signs of a possible visit by Kim to China — has ordered tactical nuclear weapons deployed across all domains of his military. For Japan, North Korea is not a distant threat but a present one, and the new defence plan includes the missile defences and the counterstrike weapons designed to deal with it.

What the money buys

The plan is not just bigger; it is different in kind. The centrepiece is counterstrike capability — the ability to hit missile launch sites in an enemy's territory before they fire. For a country whose post-war doctrine was strictly defensive, this is a revolution. Japan has already agreed to buy hundreds of Tomahawk cruise missiles from the United States — Washington approved the sale of 400 Tomahawks in 2023 in a deal worth about $2.35 billion — and it is developing its own long-range missiles, including an extended-range version of its Type 12 surface-to-ship missile, with a range reported at about 1,000 kilometres. The new plan would fund more of both, along with the ships, aircraft and command systems to use them.

Air power is the second priority. Japan is buying F-35 stealth fighters — more than 140 of them, the largest F-35 fleet of any US ally — and it is jointly developing a sixth-generation fighter with Britain and Italy under the Global Combat Air Programme, a project meant to produce a new combat aircraft by the mid-2030s. The new money would accelerate both programmes, and fund the airfields, the hardened shelters and the munitions stockpiles that a real war would require. Japan's defence planners have been blunt about the gaps: in a conflict, the country would run out of some munitions within weeks, and its air bases are vulnerable to missile attack.

The sea is the third. Japan's navy — the Maritime Self-Defense Force — is already one of the largest in Asia, but the new plan would add ships, submarines and unmanned vessels, and upgrade the two Izumo-class helicopter carriers to operate F-35B fighters. The southwestern islands would get more garrisons, more missiles and more surveillance. And behind all of it, the plan funds the unglamorous things that decide wars: cyber defences, space capabilities, ammunition stockpiles, fuel reserves, and the industrial base to make more of all of them.

The gap: why the military wanted ¥80 trillion

If the plan is this ambitious, why did the military ask for ¥80 trillion — and why is it unhappy with ¥56.3 trillion? The answer is in the arithmetic of inflation, the weak yen and the cost of modern weapons. A fifth-generation fighter costs more than $100 million. A single destroyer costs more than $1 billion. A missile defence interceptor costs millions per shot. And Japan buys many of these things in dollars, with a yen that has weakened sharply against the dollar in recent years — which means the same budget buys fewer weapons than it did five years ago.

There are other constraints, too. Japan's defence industry is small and fragmented, and it has struggled to deliver on time and on budget. Procurement has been a recurring problem: Reuters reported on 13 November that Japanese officials had warned defence contractors of substantial reductions to anticipated orders, a sign of how tight the money already is. And the Self-Defense Forces face a personnel shortage that money alone cannot fix — Japan's population is shrinking and ageing, and recruiting young people into the military is harder every year. A bigger budget does not automatically mean a bigger force.

Then there is the debt. Japan's public debt is about 250% of GDP, the highest among advanced economies, and the government is already borrowing heavily. The Finance Ministry's resistance to ¥80 trillion is not stinginess; it is arithmetic. Every extra trillion yen for defence is a trillion yen that must be borrowed, taxed or taken from somewhere else. The ¥56.3 trillion figure is the point where the security argument and the fiscal argument met — and neither side is happy with it.

American pressure

The United States is the other force shaping the decision, and it has been applying pressure in public. Washington has pressed its allies across the board to spend more on defence — NATO members agreed in June 2025 to lift spending to 5% of GDP by 2035 — and Japan, America's most important ally in Asia, has not been exempt. US defence officials have pressed Tokyo to lift spending toward 3.5% of GDP, a figure that would imply a budget far larger than even the ¥80 trillion the military requested.

The pressure is not only about money. The United States wants Japan to be able to fight alongside it — to have the weapons, the stockpiles and the command structures to operate as a genuine partner in a crisis, not a junior one. The counterstrike missiles, the F-35s, the ships and the island garrisons are all part of that: a Japan that can defend itself, and help defend Taiwan and the sea lanes, without waiting for American ships to arrive from Hawaii or San Diego. Takaichi, who became Japan's first woman prime minister in October 2025 and has long been one of the ruling Liberal Democratic Party's most hawkish voices on defence, has embraced that logic. She expressed her intention to revise the three security documents a year earlier than scheduled, specifically to bolster Japan's defence capabilities — and the ¥56.3 trillion plan is the result.

Tokyo also intends to brief the United States before revising its security strategy and related documents by the end of the year, a sign of how closely the two allies are coordinating. The message to Washington is clear: Japan is spending more, and it is spending it on the things America wants it to buy.

What happens next

The timetable from here is short. The ¥56.3 trillion figure will be reflected in early revisions of the three security documents — the National Security Strategy, the National Defense Strategy and the Defense Buildup Program — which are scheduled to be completed by the end of this year. The final number could still rise: the Mainichi newspaper has reported that the government is coordinating around a figure closer to ¥60 trillion, and Takaichi herself will make the final call. The formal budget process then takes over, with the fiscal 2027 budget — the first under the new plan — to be drawn up in the months ahead and debated in the Diet.

The debate will not be quiet. The opposition will ask how the spending is to be paid for, and whether the government intends to raise taxes, issue more bonds, or cut other programmes. The ruling coalition will argue that the security environment leaves no choice. And the public — which has historically been sceptical of military spending, but which has watched China's rise, North Korea's missiles and Russia's war in Ukraine with growing alarm — will be the ultimate judge. Polls have shown support for higher defence spending rising steadily since 2022, but support for paying for it with tax increases is far weaker.

Whatever the final number, the direction is now set, and it is unlikely to reverse. Japan has crossed a line that it spent 80 years refusing to cross: it is building a military designed not only to defend its own islands, but to strike back, to fight alongside its allies, and to deter the largest military build-up in Asia. The ¥56.3 trillion is the price of that decision. Whether it is enough — the generals say it is not, the Finance Ministry says it is already too much — is the argument that will dominate Japanese politics for the next five years.

From Article 9 to counterstrike: how Japan got here

To grasp how extraordinary the ¥56.3 trillion plan is, you have to remember where Japan started. In 1947, two years after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Japan adopted a constitution that renounced war. Article 9, its most famous clause, declares that "the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation" and that "land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained". For the first years after the war, Japan had no military at all. The Self-Defense Forces were created only in 1954, and only after the Korean War convinced Washington that Japan needed some capacity to defend itself — and even then, they were called "self-defence" forces precisely because the constitution seemed to forbid anything else.

For the next half-century, the arrangement that governed Japan's security was known as the Yoshida Doctrine, after Prime Minister Shigeru Yoshida: Japan would keep defence spending low — an informal cap of about 1% of GDP, adopted in 1976 — concentrate on economic growth, and rely on the United States for protection under the 1960 security treaty. It worked, spectacularly, for the economy. Japan became the world's second-largest economy, and its people came to see the 1% cap not as a policy but as a part of the national identity: a country that had learned from its militarist past and chosen a different path.

The doctrine began to fray in the 1990s, as North Korea's missile programme advanced and China began its long military build-up. It cracked in 2014, when Prime Minister Shinzo Abe's government reinterpreted the constitution to allow collective self-defence — the right to fight alongside an ally under attack, even if Japan itself had not been attacked. And it broke in December 2022, when the government of Prime Minister Fumio Kishida published the three security documents that the current revision is updating: a National Security Strategy that named China as "the greatest strategic challenge", a commitment to double defence spending to 2% of GDP by 2027, and — most significantly — a decision to acquire counterstrike capability, the ability to hit enemy bases. Each step was controversial at the time. Each step, in retrospect, looks like a staging post on the road to the ¥56.3 trillion plan.

Takaichi's decision to revise the documents a year early, and to push spending to ¥56.3 trillion, is the latest step on that road — and it is the largest. The woman who took office in October 2025 as Japan's first female prime minister has long been the most hawkish figure in the ruling Liberal Democratic Party on defence and security, a protégé of Abe's who has spoken openly about the need for Japan to defend itself rather than rely entirely on American protection. Her government is not inventing the build-up; it is accelerating one that was already under way. But the acceleration is the point: the security environment, in Tokyo's judgement, is deteriorating faster than the original timetable allowed for.

How the region is reacting

Japan's neighbours are watching the build-up with a mixture of welcome and alarm, and the reactions divide along familiar lines. In Beijing, the response has been hostile. China has long argued that Japan's remilitarisation is a threat to the region, and the Global Times, the Communist Party's nationalist tabloid, warned this week that the spending increase showed a shift toward "offensive military capability". Chinese officials have said repeatedly that Japan should reflect on its wartime history rather than expand its armed forces, and they point to the counterstrike missiles — weapons designed to hit targets on foreign soil — as proof that the build-up goes beyond self-defence. For Beijing, a Japan that can strike back is a Japan that has abandoned the restraints of the post-war order.

In Seoul, the reaction is more complicated. South Korea, which was occupied by Japan until 1945 and has its own painful history with its former colonial ruler, has traditionally been wary of Japanese military expansion. But the two countries have drawn closer in recent years, united by the shared threats from North Korea and China, and by American pressure to cooperate. A stronger Japanese military that can help deter Pyongyang is, in Seoul's calculation, a net benefit — even if the history makes the politics of saying so difficult. The trilateral security cooperation between the United States, Japan and South Korea, formalised at Camp David in 2023, assumes exactly the kind of Japanese capability the new plan funds.

In Canberra and across Southeast Asia, the welcome has been warmer. Australia, which signed a reciprocal access agreement with Japan in 2022 — the first such pact Japan has concluded since the war — sees a stronger Japan as a partner in balancing China. The Philippines, Vietnam and other Southeast Asian states, several of which have their own territorial disputes with Beijing in the South China Sea, have quietly supported Japan's build-up, and have accepted Japanese patrol boats, radar systems and training. For them, Japan is a counterweight, not a threat.

And in Washington, the reaction is satisfaction — with a caveat. The United States has wanted Japan to spend more for years, and ¥56.3 trillion is a large step in that direction. But American officials will note, as Japan's own generals do, that it is still short of the ¥80 trillion the military requested, and far short of the 3.5% of GDP that some in Washington have floated. The message from the Pentagon is likely to be: good start, keep going. For Takaichi, that is both an endorsement and a warning — the build-up she is announcing is the beginning of a negotiation with her own Finance Ministry, her own military, and her American allies, all at once.
