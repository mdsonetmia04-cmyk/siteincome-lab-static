Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi. He said on November 26 that Qatari and Pakistani mediators are transmitting ideas between Iran and the United States, and that the Strait of Hormuz can be reopened once Iran's requests are met. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (Open Government Licence – Oman 1.0).

The war that began with a joint American and Israeli strike on Iran in February spread across the entire Gulf in a single day. On Thursday, November 26, 2026, Iranian drones killed two Italian technicians in Kuwait, Houthi missiles and drones struck a Saudi oil refinery and the Saudi capital, two oil tankers exploded after hitting sea mines in the Strait of Hormuz, and Israel announced that it is ready to attack Iran alone, with or without the Americans. It was the most violent day of regional spillover since the war began nine months ago — and the clearest sign yet that a conflict fought mainly over Iran's nuclear programme and its missile arsenal has become a war for the whole Gulf.

The day's events unfolded across four countries and one of the world's most important waterways. In Kuwait, drones that the Kuwaiti army described as an Iranian attack killed two Italian technicians working for the German defence group Rheinmetall and injured four other people. In Saudi Arabia, the Houthi militia in Yemen struck a refinery in the southern Jazan region, killing a Nepali worker and injuring several others, and fired ballistic missiles towards Riyadh, where residents and a journalist from the Agence France-Presse news agency heard an explosion. In the Strait of Hormuz, two tankers — one of them a supertanker carrying more than two million barrels of crude oil — exploded and caught fire after hitting naval mines. And in Tehran, Iran's Revolutionary Guards declared that they have enough firepower to keep the strait closed, while Iran's foreign minister said, in the same breath, that the waterway could be reopened if Iran's demands are met.

None of this happened in isolation. The war between the United States, Israel and Iran has been running since February 28, when Washington and Jerusalem launched what they described as a pre-emptive joint strike against Iranian targets. Nine months later, the fighting has settled into a grinding pattern of American and Israeli air strikes on Iranian missile launchers, nuclear sites and military bases, and Iranian retaliation against American bases, Gulf shipping and Israel itself. What changed on November 26 was the geography. For the first time, the war's violence landed heavily, on the same day, in Kuwait, in Saudi Arabia and in the Strait of Hormuz — three places whose stability the entire world economy depends on.

The human cost of the day was small in absolute numbers — three people confirmed dead — but the strategic cost was large. Kuwait, which has spent the war trying to stay out of it, was attacked on its own soil. Saudi Arabia, which leads the coalition fighting the Houthis in Yemen, saw its energy infrastructure hit again. And the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes, saw two more ships burning. Oil traders, Gulf stock markets and the OPEC+ group of oil producers all felt the shock within hours.

Two Italians killed in Kuwait

The most serious single incident of the day happened in Kuwait, a small Gulf state that has no part in the fighting and has worked hard to stay neutral. Kuwait's Defence Ministry said on Thursday that the country's army had detected a number of drones within its airspace and dealt with them, in what it described as an Iranian attack. Four people were injured in the incident, the ministry said, according to Reuters.

Hours later, the death toll became clear. Two Italians working for the German defence group Rheinmetall were killed after an attack by Iranian drones in Kuwait, Italy's foreign ministry said, according to Agence France-Presse. "An explosion caused by a drone attack … occurred at a site where two Italian technicians from the company Rheinmetall Italia were working," the ministry said, adding that both workers were killed. A source close to the matter told Reuters the same thing: the victims were technicians for the German defence group.

The killings drew an immediate response from Europe. Ursula von der Leyen, the president of the European Commission, called for more pressure on Tehran after the two Italians were killed. "We must step up pressure on Iran to put an end to these unacceptable attacks against its neighbours and our partners in the Gulf. Today's events are yet another reminder that security in the Gulf is a matter of security for us all," von der Leyen wrote on X, according to Agence France-Presse.

Iran denied responsibility. The country's senior Armed Forces spokesman, Abolfazl Shekarchi, denied that his country had attacked a "vital" site in Kuwait and said that no offensive or military operation by Iranian armed forces had taken place in the region, Agence France-Presse reported, citing Iranian state media. The denial, issued hours after the attack, did not explain how two Italian technicians had died in an explosion that both Kuwait and Italy attributed to Iranian drones.

The attack on Kuwait matters for a reason that goes beyond the two deaths. Kuwait hosts American military facilities, and its territory sits between Iraq, Saudi Arabia and the Gulf. An Iranian drone attack on Kuwaiti soil — if that is what it was — is a direct strike on a country that is not a party to the war, and it raises the question of whether any Gulf state can stay out of the fighting much longer. It also raises a practical question for the tens of thousands of foreign workers in the Gulf's defence and energy industries: if a Rheinmetall work site in Kuwait can be hit, few places in the region are safe.

A refinery burning in Saudi Arabia

While Kuwait was counting its dead, Saudi Arabia was fighting fires of its own. The Saudi-led military coalition fighting Yemen's Houthis said the group had struck a refinery in the southern Saudi region of Jazan. "The Jazan refinery was attacked by the Houthi terrorist militia. Targeting civilians and civilian objects will be met with a firm military response," the coalition's spokesperson, Turki al-Malki, wrote on X.

The attack killed one person and injured several others. A Saudi energy ministry official, citing initial information, said the attack on the oil refinery in the Jazan region had resulted in the death of a Nepali worker and several injuries of "varying severity among workers of different nationalities", according to the Saudi Press Agency. "The attack resulted in a fire, which was brought under control, and a temporary halt to some of the refinery's operations," the official said. The injured are receiving medical care, and emergency and specialist technical teams began work immediately to complete the damage assessment and verify the safety of the facilities.

The Houthis, who are backed by Iran, claimed a wider campaign. Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthis said they had hit Ras Tanura, Saudi Arabia's biggest oil refinery, as well as the capital's financial district, Reuters reported. The Saudi-led coalition said it had downed a Houthi drone near Jeddah Islamic Port, with no civilian injuries. The coalition also said during the night that it had intercepted two ballistic missiles launched by the Houthis towards Riyadh, where residents and an Agence France-Presse journalist heard an explosion.

It was not the first time the Houthis had struck deep inside Saudi Arabia during this war. Earlier in November, a Houthi drone hit the PIF Tower — Riyadh's tallest building and the headquarters district of the Saudi sovereign wealth fund — injuring eleven people, an attack this newspaper covered in an earlier report. But the Jazan strike was different in one respect: it hit energy infrastructure directly, in a region that refines and exports Saudi crude, and it killed a foreign worker whose only connection to the war was his job at the plant.

The Saudi response was swift. Houthi media reported a series of Saudi air strikes against territories held by the Iran-backed group, including the capital Sanaa, hours after it struck Riyadh, Agence France-Presse reported. The exchanges between the Houthis and the Saudi-led coalition in Yemen have become near-daily in the country's revived civil war, which first began more than a decade ago. The coalition, which backs Yemen's internationally recognised government, has been bombing Houthi positions for years; the Houthis have been firing missiles and drones into Saudi Arabia for just as long. The war with Iran has given that old conflict a new and more dangerous meaning.

Two tankers burning in the Strait of Hormuz

The third front of the day was the water. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said that two tankers had exploded and caught fire after hitting sea mines north of Oman's Khasab port while attempting to cross the Strait of Hormuz through an unauthorised route. One of the vessels was a supertanker carrying more than two million barrels of crude oil, the Guards said in a statement carried by the semi-official Tasnim news agency.

The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow channel between Iran and Oman through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil — and a large share of its liquefied natural gas — must pass. It is the single most important oil chokepoint on the planet, and it has been the centre of the war's economic front since the fighting began. Iran has threatened repeatedly to close it. The United States has said repeatedly that it will keep it open. The two burning tankers were the latest evidence of how close that contest has come to shutting the strait down in practice, whatever either side says.

The Revolutionary Guards made the point explicitly. The force's spokesperson, Brigadier General Hossein Mohebbi, said that the country has sufficient firepower to keep the Strait of Hormuz closed and is continuing to improve its missile capabilities, according to the state-run IRNA news agency. Mohebbi highlighted Iran's capacity to maintain the blockade, pressure the American economy and defend its territory. He said Iran's firepower was precise and destructive, adding that numerous vessels involved in fuel smuggling suffered incidents on a daily basis. And he stressed that Iran has not faced shortages of weapons, saying that the Islamic Republic would continue to increase its missiles' capabilities, range, accuracy and destructive power.

The Guards' naval commander made the same claim from the other side of the strait. Rear Admiral Ali Ozmaei, commander of the IRGC Navy, said Iran maintains full control over the Strait of Hormuz, Press TV reported. He stressed that safe passage through the strategic waterway requires respect for Iran's sovereignty.

The markets heard all of this. Most Gulf stock markets were subdued in early trading as investors weighed the impact of attacks on Saudi energy assets and ongoing perils for shipping in the region, Reuters reported. And the OPEC+ group of oil producers — the Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries and its allies, including Russia — is likely to maintain its oil output targets for at least the first quarter of 2027 at a meeting on Sunday, two people with knowledge of the talks told Reuters, as the Iran war continues to disrupt Middle East exports and disagreements persist over some nations' oil production capacity assessments. The group had been planning to use the results of its review of members' oil capacity, its first ever, to help determine output quotas for 2027 on Sunday, although disagreements from some countries appear to have made that more difficult.

Israel says it is ready to strike alone

As the Gulf burned, Israel sent its own message. An Israeli military spokesperson said Israel is ready to attack Iran with "force at any given moment – even independently without the Americans", adding that Tehran continues to pose an existential threat to Israel, Al Jazeera reported. The statement, delivered on the same day as the attacks in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, was a reminder that the war's original combatants have not gone anywhere — and that Israel's patience with a grinding, nine-month campaign may be wearing thin.

The statement also carried a warning about what Israel believes Iran is doing. Israel's defence minister, Israel Katz, said that Israel had shared intelligence with the United States regarding a potential Iranian chemical weapons programme, according to reports from the region. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, rejected the Israeli accusations that Tehran is pursuing a chemical weapons programme, saying that the Israeli regime itself is armed with weapons of mass destruction, state media reported. The exchange was the latest round in a long argument about Iran's weapons programmes — an argument that has been at the heart of the war since February.

That argument has a nuclear dimension as well. The head of the Atomic Energy Organisation of Iran, Mohammad Eslami, stressed on Thursday that the country's nuclear technology development is unstoppable and will continue despite efforts by enemies to stop its progress, Press TV reported. He emphasised the continuation of the country's nuclear advances, stressing that Iran's progress in nuclear technology is "unstoppable". He added that Iran's enemies opposed the country's development of advanced technologies because scientific capabilities translate into national strength and power, something they did not want the Iranian people to possess. "When the other side has no logic or substantiated evidence, it resorts to fabricating cases and making allegations, such as claiming that Iran is seeking to build nuclear weapons, in an attempt to halt the country's progress," Eslami said.

The nuclear question is the reason the war started. The United States and Israel launched their joint strike in February saying they were acting pre-emptively against Iranian targets, including nuclear facilities. Nine months later, Iran's nuclear chief is still insisting that the programme will continue, Israel is still warning that Iran poses an existential threat, and the two sides are still exchanging accusations about weapons of mass destruction. Nothing about the core dispute has been resolved. What has changed is that the fighting has spread far beyond the original battlefield.

Iran's Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, addressed the country on Thursday in a written statement marking Basij Week, the annual celebration of the Basij volunteer paramilitary force. He called on the force to work towards realising the slogan "every Iranian, a Basiji", saying the organisation should develop more advanced versions of its existing model by drawing on what he described as a nationwide resurgence of Basij spirit, Reuters reported. He said the organisation should also make use of people who were not formal members but admired its ideology, including members of the armed forces, government officials and people who had taken part in rallies and street gatherings, according to state media. The message was a domestic one — a call to mobilise Iranian society for a long war — but it was also a signal to the outside world that Iran's leadership is preparing its population for a conflict that will not end soon.

The diplomacy: mediators, conditions and a possible opening

For all the violence of November 26, there were also signs of diplomacy — the first in weeks that have suggested a possible way out. Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said that Qatari and Pakistani mediators are continuing their efforts and are transmitting ideas and suggestions between Iran and the United States, Reuters reported, citing a video published on Araghchi's Telegram channel. "Ideas have been put forward, I think paths can be opened, these ideas are being reviewed, however our main position has not changed. We have certain requests that have to be met and then the Strait of Hormuz can be opened," Araghchi said.

The statement was the most explicit link yet between Iran's demands and the fate of the strait. It suggested that Tehran sees the waterway not only as a weapon but as a bargaining chip: the strait can be reopened, but only if Iran's requests are met. What those requests are, Araghchi did not say in the video. But the logic was clear enough — Iran is offering to trade the free passage of oil for concessions from Washington, and Qatar and Pakistan are carrying the messages back and forth.

The mediation is busy. Pakistan's Deputy Prime Minister and Foreign Minister, Ishaq Dar, spoke on the phone on Thursday with Iran's Foreign Minister Araghchi, the Foreign Office in Islamabad said. They "exchanged views on the wider regional situation and efforts to promote peace and security in the region", and discussed cooperation at multilateral forums, including within the Economic Cooperation Organisation. Araghchi reiterated his invitation to Pakistan to participate in the 18th ECO Summit, scheduled to be held in Tehran on December 3–4. Dar also received a telephone call from Kuwait's Foreign Minister, Sheikh Jarrah Jaber Al-Ahmad Al-Sabah, and the two sides "exchanged views on recent regional developments and their implications for regional peace and security". Dar "underscored that dialogue and diplomacy remain the only viable path to resolving disputes and achieving lasting peace in the region", the Foreign Office said.

Qatar's Emir, Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani, and Egypt's President, Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, also discussed the "latest developments in the region", the Qatari emir's office said. They discussed the "importance of continuing consultations and coordinating joint efforts to address current challenges, in a way that helps safeguard our region's security and stability and fulfil its people's aspirations for peace and prosperity". Qatar has been one of the war's most active mediators since February, and its involvement alongside Pakistan suggests that the diplomatic track, for all the day's violence, is still alive.

Not every country wants to be involved. South Korea's Foreign Minister, Cho Hyun, told his Iranian counterpart Araghchi that Seoul has no intention of intervening or participating in the American war against Iran, Tehran-based IRNA reported. In a telephone conversation, Araghchi warned against any presence of foreign forces in the region, emphasising that their presence will not only fail to establish stability and security but also escalate tensions. Hyun assured the Iranian foreign minister that a plan to dispatch troops to the region was not on Seoul's agenda and expressed hope that a peaceful solution would be reached to end the war. The exchange was a small reminder that the war's coalition politics are complicated: the United States has allies in Asia who do not want to be drawn into a Middle Eastern war, and Iran knows it.

There was also a warning from Iraq. Iran-backed armed groups in Iraq are preparing to strike American military assets in the Middle East if Washington starts new attacks on Iran, Al Jazeera reported. The warning, delivered on the same day as the attacks in Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, suggested that the war's next front could be Iraq — where American troops are stationed, and where Iranian-backed militias have attacked American bases before. It was another reminder that the conflict now has more fronts than anyone can count, and that each new attack creates a new reason for the next one.

Nine months of war, and where it stands

To understand why November 26 mattered, it helps to remember how the war began. On February 28, the United States and Israel launched what they described as a pre-emptive joint strike against Iranian targets, opening a war that has now run for nine months. The American operation was called Epic Fury. The Israeli operation was called Roaring Lion. The stated aim was to destroy Iran's nuclear programme and its missile arsenal before they could be used. Nine months later, the nuclear programme's chief is still declaring it unstoppable, the Revolutionary Guards are still boasting about their missiles, and the war has spread to Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Iraq and the Strait of Hormuz.

The American debate about the war has not stopped either. Earlier this week, the United States Senate voted down a resolution that sought to stop military operations against Iran and require congressional approval for the war to continue. The vote, reported by IranWire, showed that the war retains political support in Washington even as its costs grow. The American defence secretary, Pete Hegseth, has told Congress that the war has cost 37.5 billion dollars and has requested 90 billion dollars in additional funding, according to reports from the region. Those are the kinds of numbers that make a war hard to stop: once a government has spent tens of billions of dollars on a conflict, admitting that it has not worked becomes politically almost impossible.

The war's human cost, beyond the battlefield, is also growing. In Tehran, families of prisoners said that food distribution and access to basic supplies had been disrupted in parts of the city's Evin prison following American strikes, according to Iran International. In Gaza, Israeli strikes killed thirteen people, including a family of six, on the same day as the Gulf attacks, according to reports from the region. The war in Gaza, which began long before the war with Iran, has continued alongside it — and the two conflicts have become entangled in ways that make either one harder to end.

The economic cost is visible everywhere in the region. Gulf stock markets fell as investors weighed the attacks on Saudi energy assets. Oil traders watched the burning tankers in Hormuz and wondered how much worse the shipping crisis could get. OPEC+ meets on Sunday to decide output targets for 2027, and the war is now the single biggest uncertainty hanging over that meeting. If the strait closes, even partially, oil prices will spike. If it stays open, the war will still have cost the region billions in damaged infrastructure, disrupted shipping and lost investment.

What happens next

The next few days will tell whether November 26 was a turning point or just a very bad day. Three dates matter. The first is Sunday, when OPEC+ meets to decide oil output targets for the first quarter of 2027 and for the year beyond. The group's decision will be read as a verdict on how bad the war's damage to Middle Eastern exports really is. The second is December 3–4, when Tehran hosts the 18th ECO Summit — a meeting that will bring regional leaders to the Iranian capital in the middle of a war, and that may become a venue for the mediation that Qatar and Pakistan are conducting. The third is Christmas, by which time the United States envoy Jared Kushner has said Russia and Ukraine will complete the largest prisoner exchange of their war — a reminder that the world's other great war is also approaching a moment of decision, and that the American government's attention is divided.

The most important question is whether the diplomacy that Araghchi described on Thursday can produce anything. The foreign minister said that ideas have been put forward, that paths can be opened, and that the Strait of Hormuz can be reopened if Iran's requests are met. That is the language of a negotiation, not of a surrender. It suggests that Tehran is willing to talk, but only on terms it can accept. Whether Washington is willing to meet those terms — and what they are — is unknown. What is known is that the mediators are still working, the ideas are still being reviewed, and the war, for all its violence, has not yet destroyed the possibility of a settlement.

The other question is whether Israel will act alone. The Israeli military's statement that it is ready to attack Iran "even independently without the Americans" was not a throwaway line. It was a public declaration that Israel does not consider itself bound by American restraint, and that it regards Iran as an existential threat that must be dealt with regardless of what Washington decides. If Israel does strike alone, the war will enter a new phase — one in which the United States may find itself dragged back in by an ally it cannot control, rather than leading a coalition it commands.

For the people of the Gulf, the questions are more immediate. Can Kuwait protect its foreign workers? Can Saudi Arabia protect its refineries? Can the ships in the Strait of Hormuz sail safely? On November 26, the answer to all three questions was no. The war that began as a strike on Iran's nuclear programme has become a war on the Gulf itself — its workers, its energy infrastructure and its shipping lanes. Until the diplomacy produces a result, or one side wins a victory decisive enough to end the fighting, the Gulf will remain the most dangerous waterway in the world.
