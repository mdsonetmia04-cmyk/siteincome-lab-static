TITLE: China Just Launched Shenzhou-24 — Pakistan's First Astronaut Is Now Flying to the Tiangong Space Station LABELS: Science SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Shenzhou-24 launched on November 25 carrying Pakistan's first astronaut, Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, to China's Tiangong space station. ---BODY---

A Long March 2F rocket lifts off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center. Shenzhou-24 flies on the same rocket type. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0).

China launched its Shenzhou-24 spacecraft on Wednesday, November 25, carrying three astronauts to the Tiangong space station — including Muhammad Zeeshan Ali of Pakistan, the first Pakistani ever to fly in space and the first foreign astronaut ever to visit China's space station. The Long March 2F rocket lifted off from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in northwest China at 8:18 a.m. Beijing time (00:18 GMT, 5:18 a.m. in Pakistan), beginning a mission that will make history twice over: the first time a Pakistani has orbited the Earth, and the first time a crew from another country has flown aboard a Chinese spacecraft. The commander, Tang Hongbo, is making his third visit to Tiangong — a record for a Chinese astronaut — and he is joined by first-time flyer Wu Yi. Together, the three will complete an in-orbit rotation with the crew already aboard the station, and carry out 90 scientific experiments in orbit.

For Pakistan, the launch is a moment of national pride on a scale that is hard to overstate. Ali, a 40-year-old Pakistan Air Force pilot who rose to the rank of lieutenant colonel, will spend about a week aboard Tiangong before returning to Earth on the Shenzhou-23 spacecraft. In the words he used at a press conference the day before the launch, he walked into China's astronaut training centre seven months ago "carrying the hopes of 250 million people" — and what he has gained, he said, goes far beyond his expectations. Pakistan's planning minister, Ahsan Iqbal, who travelled to China for the launch, called Ali "Pakistan's first astronaut" on social media. And for China, the mission is a statement of a different kind: that its space station, built alone after the country was excluded from the International Space Station, is now open to the world — starting with its closest partner.

The crew making history

The three-person crew was announced by the China Manned Space Agency just one day before the launch, in keeping with the practice of previous Chinese crewed missions. The commander is Tang Hongbo, 51, a veteran of two previous missions to Tiangong — Shenzhou-12 and Shenzhou-17 — which makes him the first Chinese astronaut to visit the station three times. His experience matters: Tang has lived and worked aboard Tiangong before, he knows the station's systems, and he is the person the mission depends on to bring the new crew safely home. He will command the mission and lead the six-month rotation aboard the station.

Beside him is Wu Yi, a flight engineer making his first journey into space. Wu, in his late thirties, is one of the newer generation of Chinese astronauts — trained for years for a mission that, until this week, he could not be sure would ever come. He will serve as the mission's pilot and flight engineer, and he will remain aboard Tiangong for the full six-month rotation alongside Tang, taking over from the crew that is currently in orbit. His first flight is, in its own way, as significant as Ali's: every space programme depends on a pipeline of new astronauts, and Wu's presence on Shenzhou-24 is the latest step in China's steady expansion of its corps of spacefarers.

And then there is Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, the payload specialist, and the reason this mission is being watched far beyond China. Ali is a Pakistan Air Force pilot, selected through a cooperation agreement between China and Pakistan, and trained at the Astronaut Center of China since April 2026 alongside his two crewmates. He learned Mandarin as part of his training. He trained in the mock-up capsule, in the spacesuit, in the simulators. And on Tuesday, at the pre-launch press conference at Jiuquan, he stood between Tang and Wu in front of a Chinese flag and spoke, in English, about what the mission meant to him: that it represented not only himself but his country, and that he was deeply grateful to the Chinese government for enabling him to fulfil his space dream. He will be the first foreign national to visit Tiangong, and the first international crew member to join China's manned space programme — a distinction confirmed by the China Manned Space Agency's spokesperson, Zhang Jingbo, and by China's foreign ministry.

A week in orbit — and a six-month stay

Ali's stay aboard Tiangong will be brief. He will spend about a week on the station — some reports put the duration at roughly five days — and then return to Earth aboard the Shenzhou-23 spacecraft, which docked with the station in May. His short stay is by design: he is a visiting astronaut, not a member of the long-duration crew, and his mission is to carry out a set of joint experiments and then come home. But the symbolism of that week is enormous. For the first time, a person who is not Chinese will live and work aboard the Chinese space station. For the first time, the flag of another country — the green and white of Pakistan, worn as a patch on Ali's flight suit — will be seen inside Tiangong.

Tang and Wu, by contrast, will stay. They will remain aboard the station for six months, completing an in-orbit rotation with the Shenzhou-23 crew that is already there. That crew is headed by Zhu Yangzhu, 40, alongside Lai Ka-ying and Zhang Zhiyuan, also 40. Zhu will be accompanied for the next six-month rotation by Tang and Wu, and he himself will remain in orbit for a year — one of the longest stays ever attempted by a Chinese astronaut. The handover between crews is one of the most delicate operations in spaceflight: the incoming crew must learn the station's current state, take over experiments in progress, and assume responsibility for a spacecraft that is travelling at 27,000 kilometres per hour, 400 kilometres above the Earth.

The docking itself is scheduled for later on Wednesday, November 25, after the spacecraft's journey to the station. Shenzhou-24 is the 41st flight mission of China's crewed space programme, the 18th crewed Chinese spaceflight, and the 24th flight overall of the Shenzhou programme — a lineage that stretches back to the first Shenzhou flight in 1999 and the first Chinese astronaut in space, Yang Liwei, in 2003. Each of those numbers is a measure of how far the programme has come: from a single astronaut on a single orbit, to a permanently crewed space station with international visitors.

Ninety experiments — and four with Pakistan

The science aboard Shenzhou-24 is as ambitious as the symbolism. A total of 90 scientific experiments and technology tests are planned for the mission, covering space life sciences, space materials science, microgravity fluid physics and combustion science, fundamental physics, space medicine and new space technologies. Among them are four joint China-Pakistan scientific projects, focused on biotechnology and materials science. Ali will be primarily responsible for the joint experiments: installing experimental samples and equipment, conducting the planned tests in orbit, and — in one of the most distinctive experiments of the mission — working with photosynthetic cyanobacteria, the blue-green algae that may one day help produce oxygen and food for astronauts on long journeys.

The experiments build on results from previous missions that the China Manned Space Agency has already reported. In space life sciences, researchers found that spaceflight reduces the differentiation efficiency of human induced primordial germ cells by about 50 per cent — a finding with implications for the long-term health of astronauts and, one day, for reproduction in space. In materials science, they obtained a millimetre-scale deep-ultraviolet nonlinear crystal in orbit for the first time, with improved growth and quality. In space medicine, new technologies now enable muscle imaging, blood protein detection and non-contact monitoring of astronauts' physiological parameters, while artificial intelligence models help assess exercise loads and fatigue. In space technologies, semi-solid-state batteries have completed their first in-orbit tests, laying the groundwork for future upgrades to the space station's and lunar exploration missions' energy systems.

For Pakistan, the four joint projects are the tangible return on the mission — the science that Ali will actually do, and the knowledge that will come back to Pakistan with him. The country's space agency, the Space and Upper Atmosphere Research Commission (SUPARCO), has said that Ali will spend about a week on the station, and that his work will focus on the joint experiments. It is a modest scientific programme by the standards of the International Space Station, but it is a beginning — and for a country that has never sent a person into space before, a beginning is what matters.

Seventy-five years of friendship — and a new frontier

The mission is also a piece of diplomacy. China and Pakistan are marking 75 years of diplomatic relations this year, and the decision to make a Pakistani the first foreign visitor to Tiangong was, in the words of one Chinese official, a reflection of the two countries' closeness. The mission is part of bilateral cooperation under Beijing's Belt and Road Initiative, the vast infrastructure and investment programme through which China has become Pakistan's most important partner. The China Manned Space Agency's announcement that Ali would fly was made alongside the crew announcement, and it was framed, in both countries, as a milestone in a relationship that both governments describe as an "iron brotherhood."

The symbolism was visible in the days before the launch. At the press conference on Tuesday, Ali held up a piece of Chinese calligraphy reading "Zhong Ba" — China-Pakistan — written in his own hand, a gesture that was carried on television and social media in both countries. In Pakistan, the news was greeted with celebration: on social media, users posted messages of pride, and the planning minister's announcement that Ali was "Pakistan's first astronaut" was shared thousands of times. In China, the state media presented the mission as evidence that the country's space programme is now a platform for international cooperation — a station built by one country, open to the world.

Whether that openness extends beyond Pakistan remains to be seen. China has said that it welcomes international participation in Tiangong, and other countries — including several in Europe, Asia and Africa — have expressed interest in sending astronauts or experiments to the station. The Shenzhou-24 mission is the first test of whether that welcome is real. If it goes well — if Ali completes his experiments, returns safely on Shenzhou-23, and the cooperation is seen as a success — it will be easier for China to invite the next country. If it does not, the programme may remain, for the foreseeable future, a Chinese station with a single foreign guest.

The station they are flying to

Tiangong — the name means "Heavenly Palace" — is China's permanently crewed space station, and it is the destination of Shenzhou-24. The station was built in orbit between 2021 and 2022, after China was effectively excluded from the International Space Station by a 2011 American law that bars NASA from cooperating with China without explicit permission from Congress. Denied a place on the ISS, China built its own station instead — and Tiangong has now been continuously occupied for years, hosting a rotating series of crews who conduct experiments, maintain the station, and prepare for the next phase of China's space programme.

The station is smaller than the International Space Station, but it is fully capable: it has laboratories, living quarters, solar arrays and docking ports, and it supports crews of three for months at a time. It is also the platform from which China plans its next great leap — a crewed mission to the Moon, targeted for before 2030, which would make China only the second country to put people on the lunar surface. The astronauts who fly to Tiangong now are, in effect, training for that mission: learning to live and work in space for months, testing the technologies that a lunar mission will need, and building the experience that a Moon landing will require.

For Ali, the station is also a first in a more personal sense. He is not only the first Pakistani in space. He is the first person from his country to see the Earth from orbit — to look down on the planet from 400 kilometres up, and to see Pakistan, and the rest of the world, as a single blue sphere. Astronauts who have had that experience often describe it as transformative: the "overview effect," in which the divisions of the world below seem, from above, to disappear. Ali said at the press conference that the mission represented not only himself but his country. From orbit, the country he represents will be a small patch of land on a vast planet — and that, perhaps, is the most important thing he will bring home.

The docking and the week ahead

The journey from the launch pad to the space station is, by the standards of spaceflight, short. Shenzhou-24 is expected to dock automatically with Tiangong around three and a half hours after reaching orbit, connecting to the forward docking port of the station's Tianhe core module. When the hatch opens, the station's population will briefly rise to six — the three newcomers and the three astronauts of the Shenzhou-23 crew who are already there — before the rotation begins in earnest. It is a carefully choreographed encounter: two spacecraft, each travelling at around 27,000 kilometres per hour, meeting in orbit with a precision measured in centimetres, and a crew transfer that has been rehearsed for months on the ground.

For Ali, the days after docking will be the busiest of the mission. As payload specialist, he is responsible mainly for the scientific experiments rather than for operating the spacecraft, and his week aboard Tiangong will be filled with the joint China-Pakistan projects: installing samples, running tests, and working with the cyanobacteria experiment that is one of the mission's most distinctive features. He will also be learning, in real time, how to live and work in weightlessness — how to eat, sleep, exercise and move in a place where up and down have no meaning, and where a dropped tool floats away rather than falling. His crewmates, who will be aboard for six months, have the luxury of time. Ali has about a week, and every hour of it is scheduled.

When that week is over, Ali will come home — but not on the spacecraft he arrived in. He will return to Earth aboard Shenzhou-23, alongside Lai Ka-ying and Zhang Zhiyuan, two of the three astronauts of the outgoing crew. The third member of that crew, Zhu Yangzhu, will remain aboard Tiangong, and in doing so will become the first Chinese astronaut ever to spend a year in orbit — a milestone that China regards as a critical test of endurance for the long-duration missions to the Moon and beyond that it is planning. The arrangement is a reminder of how interlinked the two missions are: Shenzhou-24 does not just bring a new crew to the station. It rearranges the people already there, extends one astronaut's stay to a full year, and sends three people home in a spacecraft that arrived months ago.

Pakistan's long road to orbit

Ali did not reach the launch pad alone. He was one of two Pakistanis selected for astronaut training under the cooperation agreement between China and Pakistan — the other is Khurram Daud, also a Pakistan Air Force pilot, who trained alongside Ali and remains on the ground as his backup and colleague. Both men are military pilots, and both were chosen through a process run jointly by Pakistan's space agency, the Space and Upper Atmosphere Research Commission (SUPARCO), and the China Manned Space Agency. Ali was the one chosen to fly first. Daud's turn, if it comes, will be on a later mission — and the fact that Pakistan now has two trained astronauts, not one, suggests that the country intends this to be the beginning of a programme, not a single flight.

The training itself was long and demanding. Ali and his crewmates have trained together since April 2026 at the Astronaut Center of China, and part of that training was learning Mandarin — the working language of the Chinese space programme, and the language in which the crew will communicate aboard Tiangong. He trained in the mock-up capsule, was fitted into the white spacesuit with its blue straps, practised inside the station simulator, and learned the systems of a spacecraft built in a country whose language he did not speak seven months ago. At the press conference the day before the launch, he spoke in English, but the fact that he can now work in Mandarin is itself a measure of the effort involved — and of the depth of the cooperation between the two countries.

For SUPARCO, Pakistan's space agency, the mission is the most visible achievement in its history. The agency, founded in 1961, has launched satellites and built a modest space programme, but it has never put a person in orbit — and it could not have done so alone. The partnership with China is what made the flight possible: the rocket, the spacecraft, the station, the training, the launch site, all of it Chinese, with a Pakistani astronaut aboard. That dependence is the subject of debate in Pakistan, where some ask whether the achievement belongs to Pakistan or to China. But the answer, for the moment, is that it belongs to both — and that without the partnership, there would have been no flight at all.

What happens next

The next hours and days will follow a familiar sequence. After launch, Shenzhou-24 will chase Tiangong across the sky, docking with the station later on Wednesday. The hatch will open, the new crew will float in, and the station's population will briefly rise to six — the incoming three and the outgoing three — before the Shenzhou-23 crew prepares to bring Ali home. The experiments will begin: the cyanobacteria, the biotechnology, the materials science. And in about a week, Ali will climb back into a spacecraft — Shenzhou-23, not the one he arrived in — and return to Earth, landing in the desert of Inner Mongolia, where Chinese astronauts have landed for more than twenty years.

For Pakistan, the return will be the moment the mission becomes real. A launch is a spectacle. A landing is a homecoming. When Ali steps out of the capsule, he will be the first Pakistani to have been to space and come back — and the country will have a new hero, and a new story to tell its children. For China, the mission will be judged on whether the rotation goes smoothly, whether the experiments produce results, and whether the first foreign visitor to Tiangong paves the way for a second. And for the rest of the world, Shenzhou-24 is a reminder that the space race is no longer a race between two countries. It is a crowded field, and the newest entrant is a 40-year-old air force pilot from Pakistan, who learned Mandarin, trained for seven months, and is now, as this is published, orbiting the Earth.

Wu Yi, speaking at the same press conference, put it simply: "Once we are in space, there is no distinction between Chinese and foreign astronauts. We are one crew." That is the idea at the heart of Shenzhou-24 — that a space station built by one country can belong, for a week at least, to the world. Whether that idea survives the politics of the world below remains to be seen. But for now, three astronauts are in orbit, one of them is Pakistani, and the hopes of 250 million people are travelling with him.
