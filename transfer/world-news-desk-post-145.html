TITLE: El Niño Floods Kill 34 in Ecuador and Swamp Piura — 47,000 Hit as the Strongest El Niño on Record Builds LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: El Niño floods have killed 34 in Ecuador and left 47,000 affected, while waist-high water swamped Piura in Peru. Both nations declared emergencies. ---BODY---

Piura's Plaza de Armas and cathedral, photographed before the floods. The northern Peruvian city is under waist-high water after 12 hours of El Niño rain. Photo: Edward Josué Quevedo Macalupú, via Wikimedia Commons.

Waist-high water filled the streets of Piura, in northern Peru, on Friday, after twelve hours of unbroken rain. In Ecuador, across the border, two rivers broke their banks in the port city of Manta, and residents waded through chest-deep water or floated on mattresses to escape their homes. By Friday evening, Ecuador's disaster agency counted 34 people dead and 47,000 affected. Both countries have declared national emergencies. And the scientists say the worst may still be coming: the El Niño now building in the Pacific is projected to be the strongest on record.

The deluge is the latest and most violent episode in a season of extreme weather that has already battered Latin America for months. Peru and Ecuador are among several Latin American countries that have declared national emergencies because of El Niño, the vast climate pattern that warms the surface of the central and eastern Pacific Ocean and rewrites the weather of an entire continent. In Piura, the rain that began on Thursday fell for twelve hours without stopping. In Ecuador, the rain has been falling, on and off, since August — and the toll has been climbing ever since.

Ecuador's National Secretariat for Risk Management reported on Friday that 34 people had died and 47,000 had been affected by heavy rains linked to El Niño across the country. The figures are the official count so far, and officials in both countries warn that they will rise. In Peru, the National Institute of Civil Defence said preliminary reports showed about 2,000 families affected in Piura alone. The numbers, stark as they are, do not capture what the flooding looked like on the ground: streets turned into rivers, cars submerged to their windows, and families carrying what they could above their heads as they waded out of their homes.

Piura under water

Piura is the capital of the Piura region in northern Peru, a city of more than 600,000 people on the edge of the Sechura Desert, along the Piura River. It is known as the City of Eternal Heat — a hot, dry place where heavy rain is rare. That is what makes the flooding so disorientating for its residents: a desert city, drowned.

The rain began on Thursday and fell for twelve hours. By Friday morning, waist-high water filled the streets of the city. In the Santa Margarita neighbourhood, in the Veintiséis de Octubre district, residents boated through streets that had become canals. At the home of Pierre Garavito, a refrigerator bobbed in the floodwater inside the house. Garavito told reporters he had lost everything. His story was repeated across the city: in the districts of Piura, Castilla and Veintiséis de Octubre, roads, homes, businesses, schools and hospitals were flooded. In the Ignacio Merino neighbourhood, the water reached more than a metre deep.

Peru's weather service, Senamhi, reported that 143 millimetres of rain fell on the city — a record amount for this time of year. For a city built for desert heat, 143 millimetres in a single episode is not weather. It is a disaster. The drainage systems, where they exist, were overwhelmed within hours. Streets that had never carried more than a trickle of rainwater became rivers, and the rivers carried cars, motorbikes and market stalls with them.

About 2,000 families have been affected by the rains in Piura, according to preliminary reports, the National Institute of Civil Defence chief, Luis Vásquez, told the RPP radio station. Two thousand families means, in round numbers, perhaps 8,000 people — a significant share of a city of 630,000 — whose homes were flooded, whose belongings were ruined, and whose lives were, for a night at least, turned upside down. And Piura was not the only Peruvian city hit. Several cities across the north of the country faced major flooding on Friday, and the rains cut roads and isolated three provinces, with Senamhi keeping a red alert in place through Sunday, 29 November.

The emergency response is under way. Peru's armed forces and civil defence teams have been deployed, with pumping, evacuations and the provision of food and water. But the scale of the flooding — and the fact that the rain is forecast to continue — means the response will be measured in weeks, not days. For families like the Garavitos, whose refrigerator floated in their living room, the recovery will be measured in months.

Manta: chest-deep water, mattresses for boats

Across the border in Ecuador, the picture was worse. In the western port city of Manta, two rivers broke their banks, and the water rose so fast that residents were wading through chest-deep water within hours. Reporters from the AFP news agency who were in the city witnessed residents floating on mattresses — the only flotation available — as they tried to reach higher ground. Cars were submerged. Shops were flooded. The city's streets had become canals, and the canals were full of sewage, debris and the contents of people's homes.

Ecuador's National Secretariat for Risk Management reported on Friday that 34 people had died and 47,000 had been affected by heavy rains linked to El Niño. The 47,000 includes the dead, the injured, the displaced and those whose homes or livelihoods have been damaged. It is a national figure, covering the whole country, and it reflects a season of rain that began in August rather than a single day's storm. The floods have struck across Ecuador's coastal lowlands, where the rivers that drain the Andes meet the Pacific — exactly the places where El Niño's rains fall hardest.

The government has declared a state of national emergency, unlocking emergency funds and powers for the response. But the emergency declaration is also an admission: the country's flood defences, its drainage systems and its early-warning networks were not built for rain on this scale, and they have been overwhelmed. In Manta, as in Piura, the people who suffered most were the people who could least afford it — families in low-lying neighbourhoods, in houses built without the drainage or the elevation that would have kept the water out.

The human cost is still being counted. Thirty-four dead is the official figure, and it will rise as the waters recede and the missing are found. Forty-seven thousand affected is a figure that will rise too, as the damage assessments come in from the towns and villages that have not yet been reached. Behind each number is a family that has lost a home, a business, or a loved one — and a country that is now facing the hardest months of its worst El Niño in living memory.

What is El Niño — and why is this one so strong?

El Niño is a natural climate pattern, and it has been known to science for more than a century. Every few years, the surface waters of the central and eastern equatorial Pacific Ocean warm by more than usual. That warming changes the winds, the currents and the rainfall across the entire Pacific basin — and, through a chain of atmospheric effects, across the world. In South America, El Niño typically brings heavy rain to the normally dry coast of Peru and Ecuador, and drought to the Amazon and the south. In Asia and Australia, it brings drought and fire. In North America, it brings a warmer winter in the north and storms in the south.

What makes the 2026 episode exceptional is its strength. According to the reporting on Friday's floods, the current El Niño is projected to be the strongest on record, and it has already been linked to severe weather across the continent. The warming of the Pacific surface — the engine of the whole pattern — is greater than in any previous episode measured, and the rains it is driving are heavier than the region's infrastructure was designed to withstand.

The comparison that haunts Peru is 2017, when the last great El Niño Costero — the coastal El Niño — flooded the north of the country, killed more than 100 people, and caused billions of dollars of damage. The 1997–98 El Niño, the strongest of the twentieth century, killed thousands across the region and caused damage measured in the tens of billions of dollars worldwide. The 2026 episode is now projected to surpass both. The rains in Piura and the deaths in Ecuador are, on that reading, not the climax of the event but its opening act.

That is the fear that runs through the official statements from both governments. The national emergencies that Peru and Ecuador have declared are not just a response to this week's floods. They are a preparation for what is coming: months more of rain, more flooding, more landslides, and more damage to the roads, the farms and the water systems on which millions of people depend.

A region already on its knees

The floods have hit a region that was already struggling. Ecuador has spent the last two years in a rolling crisis: a security emergency, an energy crisis, and a political transition that has left the state stretched thin. Peru has had six presidents in eight years, and a political system that has struggled to deliver the basic infrastructure — drainage, flood defences, early warning — that would protect its cities from exactly this kind of event.

The floods expose that fragility in the cruellest way. A city like Piura, with 630,000 people, should have drainage capable of handling heavy rain. It does not, because the investment was never made, and because the rain that falls in a normal year is so light that drainage never seemed urgent. A country like Ecuador should have river defences capable of holding back a flood. It does not, because the rivers that flood are the same rivers that water the farms, and the money for defences was spent elsewhere.

The result is that the poorest people pay the highest price. In Piura, it was the families of Santa Margarita and Ignacio Merino — working-class neighbourhoods on the edge of the city — whose homes filled with water. In Manta, it was the families who live along the rivers, in houses built on the cheapest land, who found themselves wading through chest-deep water. The floods are a natural disaster, but the vulnerability is man-made, and it is the product of decades of underinvestment in the places where the poorest people live.

How the strongest El Niño on record is measured

When scientists say an El Niño is the strongest on record, they are not guessing. They are measuring the temperature of the surface of the Pacific Ocean, in a region of the equatorial Pacific known as the Niño 3.4 region, and comparing it with the long-term average. When the water there is warmer than usual by half a degree Celsius or more, for several months in a row, an El Niño is declared. The bigger the warming, the stronger the event — and the greater its effects on the weather of the world.

The great El Niños of the modern era give the scale. The 1982–83 event, which was not recognised as an El Niño until it was well under way, caused flooding in Peru and Ecuador, drought in Australia and Indonesia, and damage estimated in the billions of dollars. The 1997–98 event, for years the benchmark, warmed the Pacific by more than two degrees above average, killed an estimated 23,000 people worldwide through floods, droughts, storms and disease, and caused damage measured in the tens of billions. The 2015–16 event was nearly as strong, and the 2023–24 event, the most recent, brought record heat and drought to much of the world.

The 2026 episode is now projected to surpass them all. The warming in the Pacific is greater than in 1997–98, and the rains it is driving in Peru and Ecuador are heavier than the region has seen in the era of modern measurement. That is why the floods in Piura and Manta matter beyond the borders of the two countries: they are the first visible sign of a climate event that will, over the coming months, affect the weather of every continent. In Australia and Indonesia, El Niño brings drought and bushfire. In southern Africa, it brings failed rains and hunger. In the southern United States, it brings storms. In the Amazon, it brings drought and fire. The world has seen all of this before. It has never seen it on the scale that 2026 now promises.

The science is also clear about why the events are getting stronger — or, at least, about the direction of the risk. A warmer world holds more moisture in the air, and when that moisture falls as rain, it falls harder. The rains that flooded Piura — 143 millimetres in a single episode, a record for the season — are the kind of extreme rainfall that a warming climate makes more likely: not because El Niño itself is caused by climate change, but because a warmer atmosphere turns a strong El Niño into a wetter one. The distinction matters, and the scientists are careful about it. El Niño is natural. The heat that supercharges it is not.

The wider region — and the world beyond it

Peru and Ecuador are not alone. Several Latin American countries have declared national emergencies because of El Niño, and the pattern of damage is familiar across the region: floods on the Pacific coast, where the warm ocean drives the rain, and drought inland and to the south, where the rains fail. The same event that drowns Piura can parch the highlands of Bolivia and the farmlands of Argentina. That is the cruelty of El Niño: it does not bring one disaster, but many, in different places, at the same time.

The consequences reach far beyond Latin America. Peru and Ecuador are major exporters of food — of bananas, of cocoa, of fishmeal made from the anchovies of the cold Humboldt Current, which collapses when El Niño warms the sea. When the rains flood the farms and the warm water drives the fish away, the effects show up in food prices in markets thousands of miles away. The floods in Piura are, in that sense, not only a Peruvian disaster. They are a warning to anyone who eats food that comes from the Pacific coast of South America.

The same logic applies to the world's weather as a whole. The El Niño of 2026 will shape the hurricane season, the monsoon, the winter in North America and the summer in Australia. It will affect the price of coffee, of cocoa, of wheat and of rice. It will decide whether millions of people in southern Africa have enough to eat, and whether the forests of Indonesia burn. The floods in two small cities on the Pacific coast of South America are the first chapter of a story that will be written, over the next year, in every country on earth.

That is why the response to the floods in Piura and Manta matters, and why it cannot be left to Peru and Ecuador alone. The United Nations, the Red Cross and the world's disaster relief agencies are built for exactly this kind of event: a slow-moving, continent-wide disaster, whose worst effects are still months away, and whose victims are, for the most part, people who did nothing to cause it. The aid that flows now — the pumps, the food, the water, the shelter — is the easy part. The hard part is the preparation for what comes next: the reinforcement of the riverbanks, the clearing of the drains, the relocation of the families in the lowest ground, and the long, unglamorous work of making two countries ready for the strongest El Niño ever measured.

What the floods mean for the months ahead

The immediate priority in both countries is rescue and relief: getting people out of flooded homes, providing food, water and shelter, and restoring the roads and the power. In Peru, the armed forces are involved, and the civil defence system is coordinating the response. In Ecuador, the national emergency declaration gives the government the powers and the funds to act. Both countries will need international help — and both have appealed for it, through the usual channels, as the scale of the disaster becomes clear.

The longer question is what the rest of the El Niño season will bring. If the current episode is indeed the strongest on record, then the rains of November will be followed by worse in December, January and February — the months when El Niño traditionally peaks. The floods in Piura and Manta may be remembered, in a few months' time, as the beginning of the disaster rather than its worst moment.

That is why the scientists' warnings matter, and why the governments' emergency declarations matter too. The time to prepare for the next flood is now, while the water is still high in Piura and the rivers are still running through Manta. The drainage needs to be cleared. The riverbanks need to be reinforced. The families in the lowest-lying neighbourhoods need to be moved, or protected, before the next rain comes. And the world needs to pay attention — because the strongest El Niño on record will not stop at the borders of Peru and Ecuador. Its effects will be felt, in drought and flood and fire, across the planet.

For now, the people of Piura are bailing out their homes, and the people of Manta are drying out what they could save. Thirty-four people are dead in Ecuador. Forty-seven thousand are affected. Two thousand families are homeless, or close to it, in a single Peruvian city. And the rain, the scientists say, has only just begun.
