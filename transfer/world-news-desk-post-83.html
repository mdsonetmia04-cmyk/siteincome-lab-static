TITLE: Kroger Just Started Cutting 3,000 Jobs — AI Will Replace 37% of Its Corporate Workforce LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Kroger has begun laying off corporate staff in a 3,000-job cut over three years as AI takes over office work and Walmart eats its sales ---BODY---

A Kroger Marketplace store. America's largest standalone supermarket chain has begun notifying corporate employees of layoffs that will eliminate 3,000 jobs over three years. Photo: Harrison Keely via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0.

Kroger has started cutting jobs. The largest standalone supermarket chain in the United States began notifying corporate employees of layoffs on Tuesday, November 25, 2026, marking the start of a restructuring that will eliminate approximately 3,000 corporate positions over the next three years. The company did not disclose how many employees were affected in the first round of notices — but the scale of the plan is now clear: roughly 37 percent of Kroger's 8,200-person corporate workforce will be gone by 2029, replaced, in large part, by artificial intelligence and automation.

The layoffs were first announced on October 13, when chief executive Greg Foran told employees in a memo that the company would shed 3,000 corporate roles as part of a broader effort to cut costs and compete with Walmart, Amazon and Costco. "Technology is reshaping what's possible," Foran wrote. It was a sentence that, read today, sounds less like a vision statement and more like a warning.

Kroger, based in Cincinnati, employs 409,000 people and operates more than 2,700 stores under brands including Ralphs, King Soopers, Smith's and Fred Meyer. The job cuts will fall on corporate and administrative staff — the people who work in offices, not in stores. Store clerks, logistics crews and food manufacturing workers are, the company says, unaffected. But for the thousands of office workers in Cincinnati and beyond, the restructuring is the deepest white-collar retrenchment in the grocer's 143-year history.

The goal, according to Foran, is $4 billion a year in cost savings by 2029. The method is a combination of artificial intelligence, machine learning, workflow automation and the elimination of what the company calls duplicate work and unnecessary organisational layers. The logic is simple: if a machine can do the job, a person should not be paid to do it. The human cost is the part the company does not put in its press releases.

A $12 billion warning from Walmart, Amazon and Costco

Kroger's problem is not that it is a bad business. It is that its competitors are better at being the business Kroger used to be. Over the past year, according to data from the research firm Numerator cited by Yahoo Finance, Kroger has lost more than $12 billion in consumer packaged goods spending to Walmart, Amazon and Costco. That is $12 billion in sales — in toothpaste and cereal and laundry detergent and all the ordinary things people buy every week — that used to flow through Kroger's tills and now flows through someone else's.

Walmart has become the largest challenge. Americans increasingly buy their groceries alongside the general merchandise that Walmart sells, picking up food in the same trip as electronics, clothing and household goods. Kroger, a grocer first and foremost, cannot match that breadth. And Walmart has been aggressive on price: when Kroger announced initial price reductions in May, Walmart responded the same day by expanding its rollbacks across more than 7,000 grocery items.

Amazon is a different kind of threat. Its online grocery business, its Whole Foods stores and its logistics network give it a reach that a traditional supermarket chain cannot easily replicate. Costco, with its membership model and its bulk pricing, has been taking the customers who buy in volume — the families, the small businesses, the shoppers for whom a weekly trip to a warehouse club is a better deal than a weekly trip to a supermarket.

And then there are the discounters. Aldi and Lidl, the German chains that have expanded rapidly across the United States, have been growing fast, offering lower prices on a narrower range of products. Their model — fewer choices, lower costs, smaller stores — is the opposite of Kroger's, and it is working. Kroger has felt the pressure from all directions at once: from above, from Walmart and Amazon; from below, from Aldi and Lidl; and from the side, from Costco.

The result is a company that is, by its own admission, carrying too much corporate overhead for the market it now competes in. Foran, who took charge of the Cincinnati-based grocer in February after previously leading Walmart's American operations, told staff that Kroger had accumulated more organisational layers, handoffs and corporate overhead than necessary to support modern grocery competition. The 3,000 job cuts are his answer.

The memo that changed everything

Foran's memo to employees, circulated on a Monday in October, was the moment the restructuring became real. "Technology is reshaping what's possible," he wrote — a line that has since been quoted in every report on the layoffs, and one that employees will remember long after the restructuring is over.

The memo followed a company-wide video address, and it laid out the plan in plain terms: 3,000 corporate positions eliminated over three years, a corporate workforce reduced by roughly 37 percent, and a company that would increasingly rely on technology, artificial intelligence and machine learning to improve productivity, eliminate duplicate work and streamline operations.

Kroger had outlined its AI strategy the previous month, in September, describing a plan "to improve the customer experience, make work easier for associates and produce better business outcomes." The language was corporate, but the meaning was clear: the company intended to use AI to do the work that people currently do, and to do it more cheaply.

The plan is not a single mass dismissal. The reductions will unfold across multiple departments over the coming months and years, rather than through one immediate round of cuts. That is, in one sense, humane: it gives employees time, and it gives the company time to manage the transition. In another sense, it is worse: it means three years of uncertainty, of waiting to find out whether your department, your team, your job is next.

Foran's own history is relevant here. He is a former Walmart executive, appointed chief executive in February, 11 months after the abrupt resignation of Kroger's previous chief executive. He has recruited several former Walmart colleagues to vital positions, appointing Nate Faust as e-commerce chief and Mark Ibbotson as head of store operations, and filling newly created roles for people management and artificial intelligence. The message is unmistakable: Kroger is being remade in the image of its biggest competitor, by people who used to work for that competitor.

3,000 jobs — and the 1,000 that came before them

The 3,000 job cuts announced in October are not the first. Kroger carried out an earlier reduction of nearly 1,000 corporate roles last year, as management sought initial cost reductions. That round of cuts was, in retrospect, a rehearsal: a smaller version of the same logic, applied to the same workforce, for the same reasons.

The company's then-chief executive, Susan Morris, alluded to further cuts during a second-quarter earnings conference call with investors, held on a Tuesday. "We did some work last year and saw a substantial reduction in headcount, but quite frankly, there's more to be had," Morris said. "And you'll see us taking aggressive action there in the next 12 to 18 months as well."

She was right. The "aggressive action" arrived in October, in the form of Foran's memo, and it began in earnest on November 25, when the first layoff notices went out. The 1,000 jobs cut last year and the 3,000 jobs to be cut over the next three years add up to 4,000 corporate positions — nearly half of the corporate workforce Kroger employed before the restructuring began.

That is a staggering number for a company that has been in business for 143 years. Kroger was founded in 1883, in Cincinnati, and it has survived wars, depressions, recessions and the rise of every competitor that has ever challenged it. It has survived by adapting — by opening new stores, by acquiring competitors, by changing its model. The current restructuring is the latest adaptation. Whether it is the right one is a question that will not be answered for years.

What is clear is that the cuts are concentrated where the company believes AI can do the work. The corporate roles being eliminated are the ones that involve data processing, reporting, planning, analysis and coordination — the white-collar work that, until recently, required a human being to do it. AI can now do much of it, faster and more cheaply. The people who did that work are being told, in effect, that their jobs have been automated.

What the money is for

Kroger says the restructuring will free up resources for investments in lower grocery prices and improvements to the customer shopping experience. That is the company's stated rationale: cut the cost of running the business, and pass the savings on to shoppers in the form of lower prices.

It is a plausible argument, and it is one that Kroger's competitors have already made. Walmart's entire business model is built on the idea that lower costs mean lower prices, and that lower prices mean more customers. Kroger is, in effect, trying to become more like Walmart — leaner, cheaper, more efficient — in order to compete with Walmart.

But the argument has a flaw. The $4 billion in annual savings that Foran is targeting will not all go to lower prices. Some of it will go to shareholders, in the form of profits. Some of it will go to investments in technology, in stores, in the supply chain. And some of it will go to the costs of the restructuring itself — severance payments, outplacement services, the expense of reorganising a company of 409,000 people.

There is also a question about whether lower prices, on their own, are enough. Kroger's competitors are not only cheaper; they are different. Walmart sells everything. Amazon delivers everything. Costco sells in bulk. Aldi sells fewer things at lower prices. Kroger, a traditional supermarket, sells groceries in a store that looks like every other supermarket. Lowering prices by a few cents on a can of beans does not change that fundamental fact.

The company knows this. Its AI strategy, its e-commerce investments, its acquisition of Giant Eagle in a deal worth $1.65 billion this summer — all of these are attempts to change what Kroger is, not just what it charges. The Giant Eagle deal, completed this summer, expanded Kroger's footprint in the northeastern United States. The AI investments are meant to make the company faster, smarter and more responsive. The layoffs are meant to pay for it all.

Whether the strategy works will depend on execution. Kroger has a history of ambitious plans that did not deliver: its attempt to merge with Albertsons, which collapsed amid regulatory opposition, is now the subject of an $8 billion breach-of-contract lawsuit from Albertsons in Delaware chancery court. That legal distraction, which has been hanging over the company for months, is a reminder that Kroger's recent history is not one of smooth, successful transformation.

The workers, and the towns they live in

Behind the numbers — 3,000 jobs, 37 percent, $4 billion, $12 billion — are people. Corporate employees in Cincinnati, where Kroger is headquartered, and in offices across the country. People who have worked for the company for years, or decades. People who have mortgages, children, plans.

Kroger has not disclosed how many employees were affected by the first round of layoff notices on November 25, and it has not provided a detailed timeline for subsequent rounds or identified all the departments that will be affected. That silence is, for the employees who remain, almost as stressful as the cuts themselves. They know that 3,000 jobs will go over three years. They do not know whether theirs is one of them, or when they will find out.

The impact extends beyond the employees themselves. Cincinnati is Kroger's hometown — the city where the company was founded in 1883, and where its headquarters still stands. A reduction of 3,000 corporate jobs, many of them in Cincinnati, is a blow to a city's economy, to its tax base, to the restaurants and shops and services that depend on the spending of Kroger's office workers. The company has not said how many of the cuts will fall in Cincinnati, but the headquarters is where the corporate workforce is concentrated, and it is where the pain will be felt most keenly.

There is also a broader question, one that goes beyond Kroger and beyond Cincinnati. The company's layoffs are part of a wave of corporate job cuts across American retail, as major retailers turn to automation, artificial intelligence and organisational changes to reduce expenses and protect profit margins. The same logic that is eliminating 3,000 jobs at Kroger is eliminating jobs at other companies, in other industries, across the country.

The workers who lose their jobs to AI do not, for the most part, find new jobs in AI. They find new jobs in other fields, or they do not find new jobs at all. The economy, in the aggregate, may benefit from the efficiency gains. The individual worker, in the particular, often does not. That is the tension at the heart of every corporate restructuring, and it is the tension at the heart of Kroger's.

How Kroger got here: the grocery wars

Kroger's troubles did not begin in October. They have been building for years, as the American grocery business — once a stable, predictable industry — turned into one of the most competitive markets in the country. The change has been driven by a handful of forces, each of which has made Kroger's traditional model harder to sustain.

The first force is scale. Walmart is now the largest grocer in the United States by a wide margin, and its scale gives it advantages that no traditional supermarket can match. It buys in larger volumes, negotiates lower prices from suppliers, and spreads its fixed costs across a far larger base of sales. When Walmart cuts a price, Kroger must either match it — and accept a thinner margin — or lose the sale. Over time, that arithmetic is brutal.

The second force is the rise of online grocery. Amazon's acquisition of Whole Foods in 2017 was a signal that the world's largest online retailer intended to compete in physical grocery, and its subsequent investments in delivery, logistics and private-label products have made it a formidable rival. For Kroger, which built its business on physical stores, the shift to online ordering and home delivery has required expensive investments in technology, in fulfilment centres and in last-mile logistics — investments that, so far, have not paid for themselves.

The third force is the discounter. Aldi, which operates more than 2,400 stores in the United States, and Lidl, which is expanding rapidly, have shown that a smaller store, a narrower range and lower prices can win customers from traditional supermarkets. Their model is the opposite of Kroger's: fewer choices, lower costs, no frills. For shoppers who are feeling the pressure of rising prices — and in 2026, that is most shoppers — the discounter's offer is hard to resist.

Kroger's response to these pressures, before the current restructuring, was to try to get bigger. Its proposed merger with Albertsons, announced in 2022, would have created a grocery giant with more than 4,000 stores. But the deal collapsed amid opposition from regulators and from state attorneys general, who argued that it would reduce competition and raise prices. Albertsons then sued Kroger for $8 billion, alleging breach of contract, in a case that is still pending in Delaware chancery court.

The failed merger left Kroger in a difficult position: too small to match Walmart's scale, too large to be as nimble as Aldi, and too traditional to compete with Amazon's technology. The restructuring announced in October is, in that sense, a second attempt at transformation — not through acquisition, but through subtraction. If Kroger cannot get bigger, it will get leaner. If it cannot match its competitors' scale, it will try to match their efficiency.

Whether that is enough is the question that will define the next three years. The grocery wars are not over. Walmart is still expanding. Amazon is still investing. Aldi is still opening stores. And Kroger, with 3,000 fewer corporate employees and $4 billion in targeted savings, is betting that it can fight them all — with fewer people, more machines, and lower prices.

A 143-year-old company, remade by AI

Kroger has been in business for 143 years. It has survived the Great Depression, the Second World War, the rise of the supermarket, the rise of the hypermarket, the rise of e-commerce and the rise of the discounter. It has survived by changing — by opening new kinds of stores, by acquiring competitors, by adopting new technologies. The current restructuring is the latest change, and it is the most radical: a company that is, in effect, replacing more than a third of its office workforce with machines.

The question is whether the change is enough. Kroger's competitors are not standing still. Walmart is investing in automation of its own. Amazon is investing in AI. Costco is expanding. Aldi is opening new stores. The grocery business, which was once a stable, predictable industry, has become a technology race — and Kroger, despite its size, is running to catch up.

Foran's bet is that a leaner, more automated Kroger can compete — that $4 billion in annual savings, reinvested in lower prices and better stores, will win back the $12 billion in sales that the company has lost to its rivals. It is a bet that depends on execution: on the AI working as promised, on the remaining employees being able to do more with less, on the customers noticing the lower prices and coming back.

It is also a bet that depends on the people who are being laid off accepting the logic of their own redundancy. That is a harder bet to win. The 3,000 employees who will lose their jobs over the next three years are not abstractions. They are the people who built the company, who ran its offices, who made its systems work. They are being told that a machine can do their job better, and cheaper, and that the company will be stronger without them.

Some of them will find new jobs. Some of them will not. Some of them will retrain, or start businesses, or retire early. All of them will remember the memo — "Technology is reshaping what's possible" — and the day the layoff notices began to arrive, on a Tuesday in November, in the week of Thanksgiving.

Kroger's story is, in the end, a story about what happens when a 143-year-old company meets the technology of 2026. The company is not dying. It is changing. It is cutting 3,000 jobs, spending billions on AI, and betting that the future of grocery shopping belongs to the companies that can do more with fewer people. Whether that bet pays off will be decided in the aisles of its 2,700 stores, in the prices on its shelves, and in the choices of the millions of Americans who buy their groceries there every week. The layoffs have begun. The restructuring will take three years. The verdict will take longer.
