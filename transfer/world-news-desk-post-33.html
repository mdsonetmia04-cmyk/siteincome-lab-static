TITLE: Dow Just Topped 50,000 While the Nasdaq Crashed 1.5% — Wall Street Split in Two on Tuesday LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The Dow closed above 50,000 at 50,018.70 while the Nasdaq fell 1.48% — Nvidia's 5.86% drop split Wall Street in two on Tuesday. ---BODY---

The New York Stock Exchange building on Wall Street. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed above 50,000 on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, while the Nasdaq fell 1.48% on the same day. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Wall Street had two completely different days on Tuesday, November 24, 2026 — at exactly the same time. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 423.39 points, or 0.85 percent, to close at 50,018.70, climbing back above the 50,000 mark for the first time in about a week and recording its highest close since November 18. On the very same trading day, the Nasdaq Composite fell 356.03 points, or 1.48 percent, to 23,733.54, and the S&P 500 slipped 36.08 points, or 0.50 percent, to 7,201.09. The Nasdaq 100, the index of the biggest technology names, fell even harder, dropping 1.99 percent to 26,214.17. One market celebrated a milestone. The other market sold off. The split was not an accident, and it was not a quirk of the data. It was the story of the day: money rotated out of the artificial intelligence trade that has powered Wall Street for two years, and into the healthcare, retail and consumer names that the AI boom had left behind. The Dow, built from thirty old-economy blue chips, rose. The Nasdaq, built from the chipmakers and software giants of the AI era, fell. And the company at the centre of it all, Nvidia, gave back a large part of the extraordinary gain it had made just twenty-four hours earlier, falling 5.86 percent to 155.38 dollars a share.

The contrast with Monday could hardly have been sharper. On Monday, November 23, the Nasdaq had surged by roughly 6.5 percent and the Nasdaq 100 by 6.86 percent, after Nvidia announced that its board had reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive. Nvidia itself had soared 31 percent in a single session, adding more than 900 billion dollars in market value in one day — one of the largest single-day gains in the history of the stock market. Tuesday was the morning after. Investors who had bought the comeback on Monday took their profits on Tuesday, and the same chip stocks that had led the rally led the retreat. The Dow's two-day recovery now stands at nearly 2,000 points, according to CNBC-TV18, but the recovery has been built on a very different foundation from the rally that preceded it. This time, it was not the chipmakers doing the lifting. It was the drugmakers, the retailers and the old industrial names — the companies that sell medicines, shoes and soap, rather than the chips that power artificial intelligence.

The scoreboard: a market split in two

The closing numbers tell the story in the starkest possible terms. The Dow Jones Industrial Average finished at 50,018.70, up 423.39 points, or 0.85 percent. The S&P 500 finished at 7,201.09, down 36.08 points, or 0.50 percent. The Nasdaq Composite finished at 23,733.54, down 356.03 points, or 1.48 percent. The Nasdaq 100 finished at 26,214.17, down 1.99 percent. The Russell 2000, the index of smaller American companies, rose 0.57 percent to 2,684.94 — a small gain, but a gain, and a sign that the selling was concentrated in the biggest technology names rather than spread across the whole market. The VIX, Wall Street's fear gauge, slipped 0.98 percent to 26.36, suggesting that the selling, sharp as it was in technology, did not turn into panic. Gold fell 2.70 percent to 3,552.80 dollars an ounce, after the CME Group raised the margin requirements for trading gold futures, and West Texas Intermediate crude oil rose 1.82 percent to 81.63 dollars a barrel, supported by the continuing tensions around the Strait of Hormuz and the tanker attacks that have rattled energy markets in recent days.

The split between the Dow and the Nasdaq was, in one sense, a matter of arithmetic. The Dow is a price-weighted index of thirty stocks, and on Tuesday its winners were the healthcare and consumer names that the Nasdaq barely holds. The Nasdaq is dominated by the technology giants, and on Tuesday the technology giants fell. But the split was also a matter of mood. For two years, the AI trade has been the only trade that mattered on Wall Street, and the chipmakers have been its kings. Tuesday was a reminder that the trade can turn, and turn fast — that a stock can gain 31 percent on Monday and lose nearly 6 percent on Tuesday, and that the companies that make the chips are not the only companies in the American economy. The Dow's rise above 50,000 was a milestone, but it was a milestone reached without the help of the stocks that have defined this market. That, more than the number itself, is what made Tuesday remarkable.

Nvidia gives back part of Monday's 900 billion dollar gain

No stock mattered more on Tuesday than Nvidia, and no stock fell harder among the giants. Nvidia closed down 5.86 percent at 155.38 dollars, after closing at 165.05 dollars on Monday. The fall came a day after the chipmaker had surged 31 percent on the news that Jensen Huang had been reinstated as chief executive, a gain that added more than 900 billion dollars to the company's market value in a single session. Tuesday's decline gave back a meaningful part of that gain, though Nvidia remains far above where it stood before the reinstatement was announced. The stock is still roughly 35 percent below the record closing high it set on October 6, according to market reports, a reminder of how far it had already fallen before the events of this week — and of how volatile the stock has become.

The questions surrounding Huang's return have not gone away, and they weighed on the stock on Tuesday. The reinstatement followed a period of extraordinary corporate drama at Nvidia, and it has drawn bipartisan criticism in Washington, where lawmakers have raised questions about the company's compliance with American export controls on advanced chips sold to China. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer has accused Huang of violating United States export control law, and the Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, set a deadline of November 25 for Nvidia to answer questions about who was responsible for its China export-control compliance. President Donald Trump has said that a pardon for Huang is always an option, and has urged the Nvidia chief to tell the truth at his congressional hearing rather than pleading the Fifth Amendment. The political cloud over the company is real, and it is not lifting quickly. On Tuesday, investors weighed that cloud against the euphoria of Monday, and the cloud won the day.

Nvidia's fall dragged the whole chip sector down with it. Micron fell 11 percent. Advanced Micro Devices fell 9 percent. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, which tracks the biggest chipmakers, was among the day's worst performers, and the broader category of semiconductors and related devices plunged 8.58 percent, according to market data. Analog Devices, which reported a record quarter, fell 17.37 percent — a collapse that stunned traders, and a reminder that in this market, even good news is not always enough. Hewlett Packard Enterprise sank 16 percent, after a lower outlook for its AI server margins overshadowed the raised forecast it had delivered on Monday evening. The AI hardware complex, which had been the engine of Monday's rally, became the anchor of Tuesday's sell-off.

The chip wreck went far beyond Nvidia

The scale of Tuesday's selling in semiconductors deserves a closer look, because it was not a gentle pullback. Analog Devices fell 17.37 percent in a single session, despite reporting a record quarter. That is the kind of move that usually follows a disaster, not a record. The company's results were strong, but investors focused on what came next rather than what had already happened, and what came next, in their judgement, was not strong enough. Micron Technology, the memory chipmaker, fell 11 percent. AMD, Nvidia's closest rival in the market for AI accelerators, fell 9 percent. The selling spread to the companies that make the equipment and the components around the chips — printed circuit boards fell 8.24 percent, and computer storage devices fell 7.99 percent, according to market data. It was, in short, a rout in the sector that has carried the market for two years.

The proximate cause was profit-taking after Monday's extraordinary rally. When a stock rises 31 percent in a day, some of the people who bought it will sell it the next day, and when the whole sector rises together, the whole sector can fall together. But there is a deeper cause, too, and it is the question that has hung over the AI trade for months: how much of the spending on AI infrastructure is real, durable demand, and how much is a bubble built on borrowed money and borrowed optimism? The chipmakers have sold tens of billions of dollars of hardware to companies building data centres, and the data centres have been financed, in part, by debt. If the returns on that investment disappoint — if the AI models do not generate the revenues that were promised — then the orders will slow, and the chipmakers will feel it first. Tuesday's selling was not a verdict on that question. But it was a reminder that the question exists, and that the market is not as certain of the answer as it was a month ago.

The great rotation: healthcare, retail and value

While technology fell, the rest of the market rose, and the rotation was broad. Amgen, Merck, Vertex and Regeneron — four of the biggest names in American pharmaceuticals — each rose about 5 percent on Tuesday, as investors moved into healthcare, the sector that had been left behind by the AI rally. Nike rose. Target rose. Salesforce, the software giant that sits in the Dow, helped lift the blue-chip index. The pattern was unmistakable: money was leaving the stocks that had gone up the most, and moving into the stocks that had gone up the least. The Russell 2000, the index of smaller companies, rose 0.57 percent, a small gain but a meaningful one on a day when the Nasdaq fell by nearly 1.5 percent. The rotation was, in the language of Wall Street, a move from growth to value — from the companies priced on their future earnings to the companies priced on their present ones.

Rotations like this one are common in markets that have been dominated by a single theme for a long time. The AI trade has been so powerful, for so long, that it has crowded out almost everything else. Healthcare stocks, consumer stocks and industrial stocks have lagged, not because their businesses are weak, but because investors have preferred to own the future rather than the present. Tuesday was a day when the present fought back. The drugmakers rose because their earnings are real, their dividends are reliable, and their valuations are modest by comparison with the chipmakers. The retailers rose because American consumers, as the confidence data showed on the same day, are feeling better about the economy than they have in nearly two years. And the Dow rose because it is, by construction, an index of the present — of the thirty companies that have been the backbone of American business for decades, rather than the handful of technology giants that have dominated the last two years.

Why the Dow could rise while tech fell

The Dow's ability to rise on a day when the Nasdaq fell by 1.5 percent is a consequence of how the index is built. The Dow Jones Industrial Average is a price-weighted index of thirty stocks, and its members are drawn from across the American economy: healthcare, industrials, consumer goods, financials and energy, as well as technology. When the healthcare and consumer names in the index rise by 5 percent, they can more than offset a fall in the technology names. The Nasdaq, by contrast, is dominated by technology. Its biggest members are the chipmakers, the software giants and the internet platforms, and when those stocks fall, the index falls with them. The S&P 500 sits in between, with technology as its largest sector but with a broader base, and on Tuesday it fell by 0.50 percent — less than the Nasdaq, more than the Dow.

The divergence is not new. The Dow and the Nasdaq have moved in opposite directions before, usually when investors are rotating between sectors rather than buying or selling the market as a whole. What made Tuesday unusual was the scale of the divergence, and the milestone that it produced. The Dow closed above 50,000, a level that would have seemed unimaginable a decade ago, and it did so on a day when the Nasdaq lost more than 350 points. The milestone matters, not because 50,000 is a magic number — it is, in the end, only a number — but because it captures the breadth of the American market. The Dow's rise was not a technology story. It was a story about the whole economy: about drugmakers and retailers, about industrials and consumer companies, about the businesses that employ millions of Americans and sell to hundreds of millions more. The Nasdaq's fall was a technology story. And on Tuesday, the whole economy beat technology.

Monday's miracle, Tuesday's morning after

To understand Tuesday, it is necessary to remember Monday. On Monday, November 23, the Nasdaq had risen by roughly 6.5 percent, and the Nasdaq 100 by 6.86 percent, in one of the sharpest single-day rallies in the history of the technology market. Nvidia had risen 31 percent. The rally was driven by a single piece of news: the reinstatement of Jensen Huang as Nvidia's chief executive. The market had spent weeks worrying about Nvidia's leadership, about its governance, and about its relationship with Washington. The board's decision to bring Huang back resolved the leadership question, at least for the moment, and the market responded with a surge of buying that lifted not only Nvidia but the whole AI complex. It was, by any measure, an extraordinary day.

Tuesday was the morning after, and the morning after a party is rarely as enjoyable as the party itself. The investors who had bought on Monday, hoping for a further rise, found that the market had other ideas. The political questions surrounding Huang had not been resolved by his reinstatement — if anything, they had been sharpened by it, as lawmakers in both parties demanded answers about export controls and about the circumstances of his return. The chip stocks that had risen on Monday fell on Tuesday, and the rally that had seemed so powerful on Monday evening looked, by Tuesday afternoon, like a one-day wonder. The Dow, however, kept rising. Its two-day recovery now stands at nearly 2,000 points, built not on the chipmakers but on the broader market. The rally, in other words, changed its character overnight — from a technology rally to a rotation rally, from a bet on the future to a bet on the present.

The consumer gave Wall Street a reason to smile

One of the reasons the broader market could rise on Tuesday was the news on the American consumer. The Conference Board's consumer confidence index jumped to 103.3 in November, its highest level in nearly two years, and far above the 92.2 that economists had forecast. The surge in confidence, which came after this month's midterm elections, reflected optimism about the labour market and the overall economy, and it gave investors a reason to buy the stocks that depend on consumer spending: the retailers, the restaurants, the travel companies and the makers of consumer goods. Target rose. Nike rose. The consumer discretionary sector, which had lagged the market for much of the year, had one of its better days.

The confidence number matters for a second reason, too. It suggests that the American economy, for all the turbulence in the stock market, remains resilient. The consumer is still spending. The labour market is still creating jobs. The economy is still growing. The stock market's gyrations — the 31 percent rise, the 6 percent fall, the rotation from growth to value — are, in the end, a reflection of investors' changing expectations about the future. The consumer confidence data is a reflection of the present, and the present, for the moment, looks healthy. That is why the Dow could rise while the Nasdaq fell: the Dow is, more than any other index, a bet on the American economy as it is, and on Tuesday the American economy, as measured by its consumers, looked strong.

Gold, oil and the fear gauge

The day's other markets told their own stories. Gold fell 2.70 percent to 3,552.80 dollars an ounce, a sharp decline that followed the CME Group's decision to raise the margin requirements for trading gold futures. Higher margins mean that traders must put up more money to hold the same position, and the change forced some traders to sell. The fall in gold was also a sign that investors were less worried about the world than they had been: gold is the asset that people buy when they are afraid, and on Tuesday, despite the selling in technology, they were not afraid enough to hold it. The VIX, the index that measures expected volatility in the S&P 500, slipped 0.98 percent to 26.36 — elevated by historical standards, but lower than it had been, and a sign that the market did not expect the selling to spiral into a panic.

Oil moved in the opposite direction. West Texas Intermediate crude rose 1.82 percent to 81.63 dollars a barrel, supported by the continuing tensions in the Strait of Hormuz, where a series of explosions on oil tankers in recent days has raised fears about the security of the world's most important oil shipping route. The tanker attacks, which have been blamed on Iran's Revolutionary Guards, have pushed oil prices higher and reminded the market that the world's energy supply remains vulnerable to conflict in the Middle East. The rise in oil was, in part, a reason for the Dow's strength: the energy companies in the index benefit from higher prices, and the industrial companies benefit from the demand that higher oil prices imply. The fall in gold and the rise in oil, taken together, were a sign of a market that was rotating, not retreating — moving money from one place to another, rather than pulling it out altogether.

What Wednesday brings: inflation, growth and a holiday

Tuesday's trading is only half the story of the week. On Wednesday, November 25, the United States will release two of the most important economic reports of the month: the October personal consumption expenditures price index, the Federal Reserve's preferred measure of inflation, and the latest estimate of gross domestic product. The PCE index is due at 8:30 in the morning Eastern Time, and economists expect the headline figure to show inflation of 3.8 percent from a year earlier, up from 3.6 percent in September. The number will be watched closely by the Federal Reserve, which has been debating whether to raise interest rates further or to hold them steady, and by investors, who have been trying to guess which way the central bank will move. A hotter-than-expected number would strengthen the case for tighter policy, and would likely hurt the technology stocks that have already fallen. A cooler number would do the opposite.

The timing of the data is complicated by the Thanksgiving holiday. American markets will be closed on Thursday for Thanksgiving, and will close early on Friday. The holiday week is traditionally a quiet one for trading, with many investors away from their desks, and the thin trading can exaggerate moves in both directions. The PCE and GDP numbers, arriving on the last full trading day before the holiday, will therefore carry more weight than they otherwise might: they will be the last major data points before the market goes quiet, and the last chance for investors to position themselves before the long weekend. The Bessent deadline for Nvidia — the Treasury Secretary's demand that the company answer questions about who was responsible for its China export-control compliance — also falls on Wednesday, adding a further layer of uncertainty to a day that is already loaded with it.

The tariff cloud over the market

Looming over the market is a second source of uncertainty: trade. The Trump administration is set to unveil new tariffs after a probe into excess factory capacity in sixteen major trading partners, according to sources familiar with the matter, and the findings of that probe could be released imminently. The tariffs, which would apply to a wide range of goods, are aimed at countering what the administration describes as excess industrial capacity abroad, and they would represent the latest escalation in a trade policy that has already roiled markets this year. Tariff worries weighed on stocks on Tuesday, according to market reports, and they are likely to weigh on them again when the findings are released.

The politics of the market have become impossible to ignore. President Trump has blamed a roughly 6 trillion dollar decline in the stock market on the Democrats' victories in this month's midterm elections, and Treasury Secretary Bessent has warned the Federal Reserve against overtightening, arguing that hawkish remarks from Fed governors risk a policy mistake. The central bank, the administration and the markets are pulling in different directions, and the tension between them is one of the reasons the market has been so volatile. Tuesday's split — the Dow up, the Nasdaq down — was, in part, a reflection of that tension. The Dow's companies are, by and large, the beneficiaries of the administration's policies: the drugmakers, the industrials, the energy companies. The Nasdaq's companies are, by and large, the ones most exposed to the risks: the chipmakers that sell to China, the technology giants that depend on global supply chains, the companies that would suffer most from a trade war. The market, on Tuesday, priced in that difference.

What it means for your money

For ordinary investors, Tuesday was a confusing day, and the confusion is understandable. The headlines said that the Dow had crossed 50,000, a milestone that sounds like a cause for celebration. The same headlines said that the Nasdaq had fallen 1.5 percent, a decline that sounds like a cause for alarm. Both headlines were true, and both described the same market on the same day. The lesson of Tuesday is not that the market is rising or that the market is falling. It is that the market is splitting — that the stocks that have led the rally for two years are no longer the only stocks that matter, and that the rotation into healthcare, retail and value may have further to run.

The history of market rotations is instructive. When a single theme has dominated for a long time, the rotation away from it can be sharp, and it can last for months. The investors who owned only the chipmakers felt Tuesday's pain most acutely. The investors who owned a broader mix of stocks — the drugmakers, the retailers, the industrials, the small companies — felt it less, or not at all. Diversification, the oldest advice in investing, proved its worth on Tuesday, as it has proved its worth on every day when the market has split in two. The AI trade is not over. Nvidia is still the most important company in the world for artificial intelligence, and its chips are still the foundation of the AI boom. But Tuesday was a reminder that the AI trade is not the only trade, and that the market is bigger than any single stock, any single sector, or any single story.

The bottom line

Tuesday, November 24, 2026, will be remembered as the day the Dow closed above 50,000 while the Nasdaq fell 1.5 percent — the day Wall Street split in two. The Dow finished at 50,018.70, up 423.39 points, or 0.85 percent. The S&P 500 finished at 7,201.09, down 0.50 percent. The Nasdaq finished at 23,733.54, down 1.48 percent. Nvidia fell 5.86 percent to 155.38 dollars, giving back part of Monday's extraordinary 31 percent gain. Analog Devices fell 17.37 percent despite a record quarter. Micron fell 11 percent, AMD fell 9 percent, and Hewlett Packard Enterprise fell 16 percent. And on the other side of the market, Amgen, Merck, Vertex and Regeneron each rose about 5 percent, Nike and Target rose, and the Russell 2000 gained 0.57 percent. The split was a rotation, not a rout — a movement of money from the stocks that had risen the most to the stocks that had risen the least, from the future to the present, from the chipmakers to the drugmakers and the retailers.

What happens next will depend, in large part, on Wednesday's data: the PCE inflation report, the GDP estimate, and the Treasury Secretary's deadline for Nvidia. It will depend, too, on the tariff findings that the administration is preparing to release, and on the political questions that continue to surround Jensen Huang and his company. The market will be closed on Thursday for Thanksgiving, and will close early on Friday, and the holiday may bring a pause in the volatility. But the questions that Tuesday raised will not go away. Is the AI trade over, or merely pausing? Is the rotation into value the beginning of a lasting shift, or a brief interruption? Is the Dow's rise above 50,000 the start of a broader rally, or a milestone reached on borrowed time? The market, as always, will answer those questions in its own time. On Tuesday, it answered only one: that Wall Street, for the moment, is two markets, moving in opposite directions, on the same day.
