TITLE: TikTok Went Down for 25,000+ Users — Videos, Search and Profiles Stopped Working Across the US and Europe LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: TikTok suffered a major outage on November 24, with 25,000+ users reporting videos, search and profiles failing to load before service returned. ---BODY---

TikTok chief executive Shou Zi Chew. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).

TikTok stopped working for tens of thousands of people on Tuesday afternoon, November 24, in a major outage that knocked out videos, search results, profiles and favourites across the United States, the United Kingdom and parts of Europe. Reports on the outage tracker Downdetector began surging at around 2 p.m. Eastern Time and climbed to roughly 25,000 complaints at the peak, with some counts putting the figure closer to 27,000. Videos refused to load in the For You feed — the recommendation engine at the heart of the app — search results came back empty, profile pages opened blank, and links to videos went dead. By about 3:45 p.m. Eastern Time, service appeared to be working normally again, and TikTok confirmed that a "brief technical issue" had been resolved. The company did not say what caused the failure, how many people were affected, or why one of the world's biggest apps went dark for close to an hour.

The outage was, by the standards of internet failures, short. But it was also a reminder of how completely a single app now dominates the daily lives of hundreds of millions of people — and how little warning there is when it stops. For the creators who earn their living on TikTok, for the small businesses that sell through it, and for the ordinary users who open it dozens of times a day, an hour of downtime is not a minor inconvenience. It is a lost shift, a lost sale, a lost audience. And because TikTok does not run a public status page, most users had no way of knowing whether the problem was their phone, their connection, or the platform itself — until they checked social media and found that everyone else was seeing the same blank screens.

What stopped working

The outage touched almost every part of the app at once. Videos failed to load in the For You feed, which is the heart of TikTok — when the recommendation feed stops serving clips, there is very little else to fall back on. Search results sometimes did not appear at all, and favourites and profile pages failed in the same way. Video links went dead, so clips shared by friends or posted on other platforms led nowhere. Opening a profile returned a blank page. The problems affected the TikTok app on both iOS and Android, as well as the website, which meant that switching devices or opening the service in a browser did not help.

Downdetector, which tracks service disruptions through user reports, showed the scale of the failure in real time. Reports began surging around 3 p.m. Eastern Time, climbing to approximately 25,000 complaints at the peak, according to the tracker. At 3 p.m. Eastern, Downdetector recorded 25,943 complaints, with 71 per cent of users reporting problems with the app itself. Other counts put the peak even higher: one tracker recorded around 27,000 reports, and another showed more than 26,000 user reports against a normal baseline of just 11 — a spike of more than two thousand times the usual level. The numbers then fell almost as fast as they had risen, dropping to around 2,000 by 3:30 p.m. Eastern, and to about 2,400 by the evening, as service recovered.

The outage was not confined to the United States. In the United Kingdom, more than 22,000 problem reports were logged in a single hour, making Britain one of the hardest-hit countries on the trackers — and, in the words of one report, the country with more reports than any other in Europe. Users across Europe reported the same failures at the same time: videos that would not play, feeds that would not refresh, profiles that would not open. The pattern — a simultaneous failure across continents, on every platform, in every feature — pointed to a problem at the centre of TikTok's infrastructure, not a local network fault. What that problem was, TikTok has not said.

An hour of silence, then an apology

For the first hour of the outage, TikTok said nothing. The company did not post about the disruption on its social media channels, did not update any public status page — because it does not have one — and did not respond to requests for comment from reporters. Users, meanwhile, did what users always do when a platform fails: they went to other platforms to check whether anyone else was having the same problem. On X, on Reddit and on rival apps, the reports piled up within minutes, confirming that the problem was widespread rather than a fault with any single phone or connection. The outage trackers, which count user reports rather than measuring the service directly, carried most of the signal while the outage was live.

By about 3:45 p.m. Eastern Time, the app appeared to be working normally again. Videos loaded. Search returned results. Profiles opened. And only then, after service was restored, did TikTok confirm the problem. "We experienced a brief technical issue that impacted access to TikTok," spokesperson Jessica Casano-Antonellis said in a statement. "The issue has been resolved. We apologize for the inconvenience and thank you for your patience." The statement was brief, and it answered none of the questions users were asking: what the technical issue was, why it happened, how many people were affected, or what TikTok is doing to stop it happening again.

The timing of the statement — after the fix, not during the failure — is now the standard pattern for major platform outages. Companies rarely confirm a problem while it is happening, partly because they are still diagnosing it, and partly because an acknowledgement can itself become a story. But for users, the silence is the most frustrating part of the experience. When an app with more than a billion users stops working, the absence of any official word leaves a vacuum that is filled by rumour, speculation and, in this case, a string of unverified claims about what had gone wrong. TikTok's two-sentence statement, when it finally came, did little to fill it.

Why it happened — and why nobody knows

As of now, there is no confirmed explanation for what caused the disruption. TikTok has not publicly explained the failure, and the outage trackers can only measure the symptoms, not the cause. The possibilities are familiar from previous outages at other platforms: a bad software deployment, a configuration error in the servers that route traffic, a failure in the databases that store videos and profiles, a problem with the content delivery network that serves clips to users, or a cascade in which one small failure triggers a chain of larger ones. Any of these can take down a service as large as TikTok in minutes, and any of them can be fixed in under an hour once engineers find the fault.

What is known is that the failure was not a single feature breaking. When videos, search, favourites, profiles and links all fail at the same time, the problem is almost always in the shared infrastructure underneath them — the systems that all of those features depend on. That is also why the recovery was so fast: once the underlying fault is fixed, everything that depends on it comes back at once. The pattern of the Downdetector numbers — a near-vertical rise to 25,000 reports, then a near-vertical fall to 2,000 within half an hour — is consistent with a central failure that was identified and fixed quickly, rather than a slow degradation.

There is a second possibility that users raised during the outage, and that TikTok has not addressed: that the failure was connected to the string of disruptions that have affected the platform over the past couple of weeks. Several reports noted that the November 24 outage followed a series of smaller, less widely reported problems with the service in recent weeks, and that this pattern has fuelled speculation about whether something deeper is wrong. Without a statement from TikTok, that speculation cannot be confirmed or denied — and that, in itself, is part of the problem. When a company does not explain its outages, users fill the gap with their own theories, and the theories are rarely generous.

The scale of what went down

To understand why a one-hour outage matters, it helps to understand the scale of TikTok. The app has more than a billion users worldwide, and it is one of the most-used apps on the planet — not just for entertainment, but for news, for shopping, for learning, for work. In the United States alone, tens of millions of people open it every day, and for many of them it is the first app they open in the morning and the last they close at night. Creators build businesses on it. Small companies find customers through it. Musicians, comedians, teachers, journalists and activists reach audiences through it that they could never reach anywhere else. When it stops, all of that stops with it.

The Downdetector figures, large as they are, almost certainly understate the number of people affected. The tracker counts only the people who take the trouble to report a problem — a small fraction of those who experience one. Most users, faced with a video that will not load, simply close the app and try again later, or switch to something else, without ever filing a report. The 25,000 reports at the peak, and the 22,930 in the United Kingdom alone, are the visible tip of a much larger number — and the true figure, which only TikTok knows, has not been disclosed. The company has not said how many users were affected, and it is unlikely to do so.

There is also a cost that does not show up in the report counts. For creators who post on a schedule, an hour of downtime means a video that does not get its first, critical hour of views — the hour in which TikTok's algorithm decides whether to show it to more people. For businesses running live shopping events, it means customers who cannot buy. For advertisers, it means impressions that never happened. None of these losses is likely to be compensated. TikTok's terms of service do not guarantee uptime, and the company has not announced any compensation for the outage. For the people who depend on the platform for income, that is the sharpest edge of the failure.

A string of outages

The November 24 outage did not happen in isolation. It followed a string of outages at other services in recent weeks, and it came after several reported disruptions affecting TikTok itself over the past couple of weeks. The pattern has become familiar enough that users now have a routine for it: check Downdetector, check social media, confirm that it is not just you, wait. The fact that a routine exists — that outages are now common enough to have a procedure — is itself a measure of how fragile the infrastructure of the modern internet has become, and how much of daily life now depends on it.

The broader context matters. In recent months, major platforms have gone down repeatedly: cloud services, messaging apps, payment systems, social networks. Each time, the cause is different — a bad update, a severed cable, a configuration error, a cyberattack — but the effect is the same: millions of people cut off from services they have come to treat as utilities. The internet was designed to be resilient, to route around damage. But the modern internet is dominated by a small number of very large platforms, and when one of those platforms fails, there is nothing to route around. TikTok's outage was a reminder of that concentration, and of the risk that comes with it.

For TikTok specifically, the outages come at a sensitive time. The company has spent the past two years under intense political scrutiny in the United States, where lawmakers have debated its ownership, its data practices and its future in the American market. An outage does not change any of that. But it does add to the sense — among users, among creators, among advertisers — that the platform is not as stable as it presents itself to be. Reliability is part of trust, and trust, once lost, is hard to rebuild. A one-hour outage will not destroy TikTok. A pattern of outages, unexplained and unacknowledged, might do more damage than the company expects.

What users can do when it happens again

When an app as large as TikTok goes down, there is very little an individual user can do to fix it. But there are a few things that help. First, check whether the problem is yours or theirs: if the app fails on both Wi-Fi and mobile data, and on more than one device, the problem is almost certainly on TikTok's side, not yours. Second, check an outage tracker such as Downdetector, or search social media for reports from other users — if thousands of people are reporting the same failure at the same time, it is a platform outage, and no amount of reinstalling the app will help. Third, do not delete and reinstall the app in a panic: you will lose your downloaded videos and your login session, and it will not fix a server-side problem.

For creators and businesses, the lesson is more structural. An outage is a reminder that a business built entirely on one platform is a business with a single point of failure. The creators who were least affected by the November 24 outage were the ones with audiences on more than one platform — the ones who could post on Instagram, on YouTube, on their own websites, while TikTok was down. That is not a realistic option for everyone, and it is not a solution to the outage itself. But it is the only insurance that exists, and the outage was a reminder of why it matters.

For TikTok, the lesson is simpler: explain what happened. The company's two-sentence statement — a brief technical issue, now resolved, sorry for the inconvenience — is the minimum that a company can say, and it is not enough. Users want to know what broke, why it broke, and what is being done to prevent it breaking again. Other platforms, after major outages, have published detailed post-mortems explaining the failure in technical terms and setting out the changes they have made. TikTok has not done that for this outage, and until it does, the questions will remain — and the next outage, whenever it comes, will be met with the same frustration, the same speculation, and the same silence.

What the trackers saw

The outage trackers captured the failure in unusual detail, and the numbers tell their own story. Downdetector, the best-known of the trackers, measures outages through user reports: when a service is working normally, it receives only a handful of complaints — in TikTok's case, a baseline of just 11 reports. On Tuesday afternoon, that number exploded to more than 26,000, a spike of more than two thousand times the normal level. At 3 p.m. Eastern Time, the tracker recorded 25,943 complaints, with 71 per cent of users reporting problems with the app itself. Another tracker, Is It Down Right Now, which also counts user reports, said the spike began at 7:21 p.m. UK time and was still going when it was checked at about 8:30 p.m. Of the reports that named a specific problem, 61 per cent were about the app, 13 per cent about the feed, and 7 per cent about comments or messages. The figures from different trackers do not match exactly, because they measure different things in different ways — but they all point the same way: a sudden, massive, simultaneous failure.

For users, the failure announced itself with a message that has become familiar to anyone who uses the app: "something went wrong." That was the error users saw when they tried to load videos, according to reports from the afternoon, and it was followed by the instruction to "try again later." Trying again later did not help, because the problem was not on the user's phone. It was on TikTok's servers, thousands of miles away. The error message is the same one users see for a dozen different problems, from a bad connection to a server failure, which is part of why the outage was so confusing in its first minutes: there was no way to tell, from the screen alone, whether the fault was yours or theirs. Only the trackers, and the flood of reports on other platforms, made the answer clear.

The outage struck at a busy hour. In the United States it was the middle of the afternoon, and in the United Kingdom it was the early evening — 7:20 p.m. — the time when millions of people settle in after work and open the app. The United Kingdom, with 22,930 reports in a single hour, was one of the hardest-hit countries on the trackers, with more reports than any other country in Europe. That is a striking detail, because the United Kingdom is not TikTok's largest market — the United States is — and it suggests that the failure was felt most acutely where the evening peak coincided with the outage window. In the United States, the reports peaked at around 25,000 to 27,000; in Britain, the hourly count was nearly as high. The outage was, in the fullest sense, a transatlantic event.

What happens next

Service has been restored, the reports have fallen back to normal levels, and for most users the outage is already forgotten — a blank screen on a Tuesday afternoon, a video that would not load, a profile that would not open, and then everything working again. That is the nature of internet outages: they are dramatic while they last, and they vanish the moment they end. But the questions they raise do not vanish with them. How many people were affected? What caused the failure? Why did it take an hour to fix? What is TikTok doing to stop it happening again? None of those questions has been answered.

The answers matter because TikTok is not just an app. It is, for hundreds of millions of people, the place where they get their news, their entertainment, their income and their community. A failure that lasts an hour is a failure that touches all of those things at once. The company has said the issue has been resolved, and for now, the app is working. But the next outage is a matter of when, not if — and when it comes, users will be asking the same questions, and hoping for better answers than the two sentences they got this time.
