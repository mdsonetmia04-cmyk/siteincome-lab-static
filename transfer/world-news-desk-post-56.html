TITLE: Bird Flu Just Killed a Dairy Worker in Washington — the First Death of Its Kind Anywhere in the World LABELS: Health SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Three dairy workers in Washington caught H5N1 bird flu and one has died, the first death anywhere linked to infected dairy cattle, the WHO says ---BODY---

Influenza A H5N1 virus particles. The virus has now killed a dairy worker in Washington state — the first death anywhere in the world linked to exposure to infected dairy cattle. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0).

A dairy worker in Washington state has died of bird flu, and the death is a first — not only for the United States, but for the world. The World Health Organization said on Tuesday, November 24, that it had been notified by the United States of three confirmed human cases of bird flu in Washington state, including one death, and that all three patients were adults who worked on dairy farms in Yakima County, where the avian influenza virus had recently been detected in cattle. The fatal case is the first death reported globally following exposure to infected dairy cattle, and the first death from avian influenza reported in the United States in 2026. The patient who died had underlying medical conditions, developed severe respiratory illness, and died on November 1. The other two patients survived — one with mild symptoms that never required hospitalisation, the other with conjunctivitis, an inflammation of the eye, who also recovered without being admitted. Genetic sequencing confirmed that all three infections were caused by the H5N1 virus, with strains closely matching those detected in dairy cattle in Washington. The WHO said there was no evidence so far of human-to-human transmission, based on the information available. But the death has changed the conversation about bird flu in America, because it shows that a virus that spent decades as a disease of birds and poultry workers can now kill a person who caught it from a cow.

The three cases unfolded over the course of a month, and the WHO's account of them, published on November 24, lays out the sequence in detail. The first case developed symptoms on October 5, 2026, including eye irritation, a runny nose, muscle aches and a headache, and sought medical care on October 12. The case was not hospitalised and was offered treatment with oseltamivir, the antiviral drug sold as Tamiflu. The second case, who had underlying medical conditions, developed respiratory symptoms during the epidemiological week ending on October 24, presented to an emergency department the following week, was hospitalised with severe illness, and died on November 1. The third case developed conjunctivitis and sought medical care but did not require hospitalisation, and during the week ending October 31, specimens collected from the case tested positive for influenza A(H5) virus at the Washington State Public Health Laboratory using the CDC influenza A(H5) assay. The United States notified the WHO of the first case on October 16, and of the two additional cases, including the fatal one, on November 4. Sequencing of the viruses from all three cases further confirmed the influenza A(H5N1) virus subtype, and the United States Department of Agriculture reported on November 4 that whole-genome sequencing of the H5N1 viruses detected in the first two affected dairy herds in Washington state identified genotype B3.13 — a strain that closely matches the virus circulating in the state's dairy herds.

All three cases were adults aged 18 years or older who worked in Yakima County, Washington state, a major agricultural and dairy-producing area, where highly pathogenic avian influenza A(H5N1) virus had recently been detected in dairy cattle. Washington confirmed its first detections of highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 in cattle at two Yakima County dairies on October 8. The outbreak expanded quickly to 33 infected herds in Yakima County, and on October 26, the Benton-Franklin Health District reported infected cows at a Benton County dairy. All three human cases in the state have occurred since mid-October, and each has been linked to exposure to infected cows. The Yakima Health District first reported the fatal case as a severe hospitalisation on October 31, and health officials are monitoring the contacts of the person who died to ensure no onward transmission occurred. The death is Washington's second fatal avian influenza infection in less than a year, and it brings the total number of human deaths from bird flu in the United States to three. "Avian influenza can cause severe illness, particularly among people who have underlying health conditions," Dave Atteberry, the health officer for the Yakima Health District, said in a statement. "While this is a serious and heartbreaking outcome, the risk to the general public remains low."

How a bird virus learned to live in cows — and what that means

The Washington cases are the latest chapter in a story that began, for American dairy cattle, in 2024. H5N1 is a subtype of influenza A virus that primarily infects wild and domestic birds, and occasionally spreads to mammals, including humans. For decades, human infections were almost always linked to poultry — to people who worked with infected birds, or who culled them, or who were exposed to their droppings. The virus's jump into dairy cattle changed that picture. Since 2024, the United States has reported 75 human cases of influenza A(H5) virus infection, including the three Washington cases, as of November 20, 2026. Of those cases, 41 involved exposure to dairy herds, 24 involved poultry farms and culling operations, three involved other animal exposures, and three had no identified source. The Washington cases are part of that pattern: dairy workers, exposed to infected cows, catching a virus that was, until 2024, a disease of birds. The genetic evidence ties the human cases directly to the cattle. The sequencing of the Washington cases showed clade 2.3.4.4b, genotype B3.13, closely matching the virus circulating in the state's dairy herds — and the USDA's whole-genome sequencing of the virus in the first two affected herds identified the same genotype, B3.13. The virus in the workers and the virus in the cows are, in genetic terms, the same virus.

That matters because of what it says about the virus's range. A pathogen that can infect birds, and then cows, and then the people who work with those cows, is a pathogen that is finding new hosts, and each new host is a new opportunity for the virus to change. The WHO's assessment, published with its November 24 report, is that there is no evidence so far of human-to-human transmission, and the public health risk to the general population remains low. But the agency also notes that the situation may change as more information becomes available, and the history of influenza is a history of viruses that changed when no one expected them to. The 1918 pandemic, the 1957 pandemic, the 1968 pandemic and the 2009 pandemic were all caused by influenza viruses that acquired, through mutation or reassortment, the ability to spread efficiently between people. H5N1 has not done that. It has infected 75 people in the United States since 2024, and in none of those cases has it spread from one person to another. But the virus is now circulating in a new host — dairy cattle, of which there are millions in the United States — and every infection is a roll of the evolutionary dice. The Washington death does not mean the virus has changed. It means the virus is in a position where change is possible, and that is why health officials are watching it as closely as they are.

The symptoms of the three Washington cases show the range of what H5N1 can do in a human body. The first case had eye irritation, a runny nose, muscle aches and a headache — the kind of illness that could be mistaken for a cold, and that resolved without hospitalisation. The third case had conjunctivitis — red, irritated eyes — and also recovered without being admitted. The second case had severe respiratory illness, required hospitalisation, and died. The difference between them is the difference that underlying medical conditions can make, and it is the reason health officials emphasise that the risk is not evenly distributed: for a healthy adult, H5N1 infection has so far usually meant a mild illness, often centred on the eyes, that resolves on its own or with antiviral treatment. For a person with underlying conditions, it can mean severe respiratory disease and death. That pattern — mild in most, devastating in a few — is familiar from other respiratory viruses, and it is the reason the Yakima Health District's statement stressed both the seriousness of the outcome and the low risk to the general public. The virus is dangerous. It is not, at present, dangerous to most people. Both of those things are true at the same time, and public health communication has to hold them both.

Yakima County — where the outbreak is, and how it is being handled

Yakima County, in central Washington, is one of the most productive agricultural regions in the United States, and dairy is a large part of it. The county's first detections of H5N1 in cattle came on October 8, at two dairies, and within weeks the virus had spread to 33 herds in the county. On October 26, the Benton-Franklin Health District reported infected cows at a dairy in Benton County, across the Columbia River from Yakima. The speed of the spread — from two herds to 33 in a matter of weeks — shows how efficiently the virus moves through a dairy herd once it arrives, and how difficult it is to contain in an environment where cows are milked daily, moved between barns, and tended by workers who move between animals. The human cases followed the cattle cases, as they have in every dairy outbreak since 2024: the workers who were exposed to infected cows were the ones who became infected, and the timing of their illnesses — symptoms beginning in early and mid-October — tracks the spread of the virus through the herds they worked with.

The public health response has followed the pattern established in the earlier dairy outbreaks. Health officials are monitoring the contacts of the person who died, to ensure no onward transmission has occurred, and the Yakima Health District has been working with the state health department, the CDC and the USDA to track the outbreak in cattle and in people. The three human cases were confirmed by laboratory testing — the first two through the WHO notification process, and the third through the Washington State Public Health Laboratory, using the CDC's influenza A(H5) assay. The genetic sequencing that tied the human cases to the cattle was done by the USDA and by state laboratories, and the genotype identified — B3.13, clade 2.3.4.4b — is the same genotype that has been circulating in American dairy cattle since the outbreak began. The monitoring of contacts, the testing of symptomatic workers, and the sequencing of the virus are the tools that public health uses to detect the moment when a virus changes — and in Washington, so far, they have found no such change. The virus in the workers is the virus in the cows. It has not acquired the ability to spread between people. That is the finding that matters most, and it is the finding that the WHO's November 24 report is built around.

The death has also drawn attention to the people who do the work. Dairy farm workers are among the most exposed to H5N1, and they are also among the least protected — often low-paid, often without easy access to healthcare, and often working in conditions where protective equipment is not routinely provided. The first Washington case was offered oseltamivir, the antiviral, and recovered without hospitalisation. The second case, who had underlying conditions, was hospitalised and died. The third case recovered without hospitalisation. The difference in outcomes is a reminder that the protection of farm workers — through testing, through antiviral treatment, through protective equipment, and through the monitoring of contacts — is the front line of the public health response to H5N1, and that the workers themselves are the sentinels: the first people to be infected, and the first sign that the virus has arrived in a new place. The 75 human cases reported in the United States since 2024 are, in that sense, a surveillance system — imperfect, but real — and the Washington cases are its latest signal.

The national picture — 75 cases, 3 deaths, and a virus that is not going away

The Washington death brings the American toll from H5N1 to three deaths, out of 75 confirmed human cases since 2024. The breakdown of those cases tells the story of how the virus has moved through the country: 41 cases involved exposure to dairy herds, 24 involved poultry farms and culling operations, three involved other animal exposures, and three had no identified source. Dairy is now the single largest source of human infection in the United States — a fact that would have been unimaginable five years ago, when H5N1 was understood as a disease of birds. The shift reflects the virus's establishment in American dairy cattle, which began in 2024 and has continued since, with outbreaks reported in herds across the country. The Washington outbreak, with 33 herds in a single county, is one of the largest concentrations yet, and it is a reminder that the virus is not a one-off event but an ongoing presence in the American food system — a pathogen that has found a new home in cattle, and that will continue to produce human cases for as long as it circulates in herds.

The wider context is a flu season that is already severe. Seasonal influenza is surging in the United States, with high flu activity reported in seven states, and the arrival of H5N1 in the news comes at a moment when hospitals are already dealing with a heavy burden of respiratory illness. The two are not the same virus, and they should not be confused: seasonal flu spreads easily between people and infects millions each year, while H5N1 has infected 75 people in the United States since 2024 and has not spread between people at all. But the coincidence of timing matters, because it means the public is hearing about bird flu at the same time as it is experiencing flu, and because it means the healthcare system is under strain from the virus it already knows, even as it watches for the virus it fears. The WHO's report on the Washington cases is careful to separate the two: the risk from H5N1 to the general public remains low, and there is no evidence of human-to-human transmission. But the report also notes that the situation may change, and that monitoring continues — a standard formulation that carries, in this case, a particular weight, because the virus that killed a dairy worker in Washington is a virus that was, two years ago, a disease of birds.

The global picture adds to the concern. H5N1 has been circulating in wild birds and poultry around the world for decades, and it has caused hundreds of human infections, mostly in Asia and the Middle East, with a high fatality rate among those infected. The American experience since 2024 has been different: 75 cases, mostly mild, mostly in dairy and poultry workers, with three deaths. The difference is partly a matter of the virus — the genotype circulating in American cattle, B3.13, appears to cause milder illness in humans than the genotypes that have infected people elsewhere — and partly a matter of exposure: American cases have mostly been in healthy working-age adults, while cases elsewhere have often been in people with heavier exposures or different health profiles. The Washington death is the first in the world to follow exposure to infected dairy cattle, and it is a reminder that the mildness of the American experience is a property of the circumstances, not a guarantee. A virus that can kill a person with underlying conditions can kill others with underlying conditions, and the population of people with underlying conditions is large.

What to watch next

The first thing to watch is the contacts of the person who died. Health officials in Yakima County are monitoring them to ensure no onward transmission has occurred, and the absence of secondary cases among those contacts will be the most important early signal that the virus has not changed. The second thing to watch is the cattle. The outbreak in Yakima County has reached 33 herds, and the Benton County detection shows that it is spreading beyond the county line. The USDA's sequencing of the virus in the herds — genotype B3.13, clade 2.3.4.4b — will continue, and any change in the genotype, or any sign that the virus is adapting to cattle in a way that makes it more transmissible to people, will be reported. The third thing to watch is the workers. The three Washington cases were all adults who worked on dairy farms, and the testing of symptomatic workers, the offering of antiviral treatment, and the monitoring of contacts are the measures that will determine whether the next case is caught early, treated promptly, and contained.

The fourth thing to watch is the flu season. Seasonal influenza is surging in seven states, and the overlap between seasonal flu and H5N1 — in symptoms, in timing, and in the population affected — means that the surveillance system will be tested in the coming weeks. A dairy worker with a fever and a cough in Yakima County could have seasonal flu, or H5N1, or both, and the only way to tell is to test. The public health system has the tools: the CDC's influenza A(H5) assay, which was used to confirm the third Washington case, can distinguish H5N1 from seasonal flu, and the state laboratories that run it are the backbone of the surveillance effort. The question is whether the testing is done, and done quickly, and whether the results are acted on. The Washington cases were notified to the WHO on October 16 and November 4, and the WHO's report was published on November 24 — a timeline that shows the system working, but also shows that it takes time.

The death of a dairy worker in Yakima County is, in the end, a story about a virus that has found a new home and a new way to reach people. H5N1 was a disease of birds. It became a disease of cattle. It has now killed a person who caught it from a cow — the first such death anywhere in the world. The WHO says there is no evidence of human-to-human transmission, and the risk to the general public remains low, and those assessments are based on the evidence available today. But the virus is circulating in 33 herds in a single county, in millions of cattle across the country, and in 75 people since 2024, and every infection is a chance for it to change. The workers who tend the cattle are the first to be exposed, and the first to be infected, and their illnesses are the earliest warning the public health system gets. The death in Washington is a warning, and it is also a test: of whether the system that detected it, sequenced it, reported it and monitored its contacts can keep doing so, for as long as the virus keeps circulating. The virus is not going away. The question is whether we are watching it closely enough.
