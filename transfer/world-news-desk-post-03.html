TITLE: Ukraine Says Its Sea Drones Sank a Russian Warship Off Libya — A 2,400-Kilometre Strike That Widens the War LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Ukraine says seven sea drones sank the Russian corvette Merkury at Benghazi naval base in Libya and hit the cargo ship MV Angara, in a strike 2,400 km from the front line. ---BODY---

Volodymyr Zelenskyy, President of Ukraine. Photo: President of Ukraine / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)

Ukraine has claimed responsibility for sinking a Russian warship at a naval base in Libya, in a sea-drone attack carried out some 2,400 kilometres from the front line of the war in Ukraine — a strike that dramatically extends the geographical reach of the conflict and raises new questions about the war's expansion into the Mediterranean.

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy confirmed on Monday, November 23, 2026, that Ukrainian forces had sunk the Russian corvette Merkury at the Benghazi naval base in eastern Libya the previous day, and had also struck the cargo vessel MV Angara and destroyed a Russian Ka-27 military helicopter. "Our warriors deployed seven Ukrainian-made naval drones," Zelenskyy wrote in a post on X. "Despite all the enemy's efforts to counter the attack, the Russian corvette Merkury was sunk."

The operation, which Ukraine's Security Service (SBU) described as "unique", was the latest in a series of daring long-range strikes by Kyiv's forces — and by far the most distant. It demonstrated that Ukraine can now project force across the Mediterranean, against Russian assets sheltering in a third country, and it exposed the vulnerability of Moscow's military presence in North Africa at a moment when Russia's position there is already under strain.

The attack on the Merkury

The attack began at dawn on Sunday, November 22, when Ukrainian naval drones struck the Merkury as it lay docked inside the naval base at Benghazi, the principal city of eastern Libya. The corvette, one of Russia's newest warships, had been commissioned in 2023 — a year after Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine — and was officially attached to the Russian Black Sea Fleet, though in recent years it had operated in the Baltic Sea.

Footage circulated on social media showed the vessel on fire, with a series of ammunition explosions following the initial strikes. Satellite imagery later showed that most of the corvette had capsized and sunk, with only its bow visible above the water. Zelenskyy posted a video that appeared to show the Merkury already burning when a naval drone carrying a camera struck it, suggesting the vessel had been hit by more than one device.

Libyan officials who were briefed on the attack told The Associated Press that at least four drones were intercepted while two hit the corvette. At least 10 people, including Libyan and Russian nationals, were wounded in the attack, according to one of the officials. A UK-based maritime security firm, Ambrey, said some of the explosions at the base were likely caused by the detonation of ammunition on board the Russian warship.

The SBU said the drones had been delivered to a designated location and launched from a so-called "mother ship" near the target — a detail that, if accurate, points to a sophisticated logistical operation conducted far from Ukrainian territory. A Ka-27 helicopter that had been stationed on board the corvette was also destroyed, the SBU said, and went down with the ship.

Zelenskyy described the attack as "exemplary" and said "the targets we set were achieved". In his post, he wrote: "Our second target was the Russian dry cargo vessel MV ANGARA, which the enemy used to transport weapons. Result: target hit. We continue our operations in designated waters. We are responding to Russia's aggression. We are developing Ukrainian weapons. Glory to Ukraine!"

The second target: MV Angara

The second vessel struck in the operation was the MV Angara, a Russian dry cargo ship that Zelenskyy said Moscow used to transport weapons. The SBU said the Angara was a sanctioned vessel used to transport Russian military equipment, and that the multipurpose corvette Merkury regularly escorted Russian naval convoys, including guarding the Angara.

Hours after the warship attack, another sea drone strike hit a Maltese-flagged commercial vessel sailing off Benghazi, according to the Libyan coast guard — a reminder that the waters off Libya's coast are crowded with shipping and that drone warfare in the area carries risks for civilian traffic.

The attack on the Angara was part of a wider pattern. A day earlier, on Saturday, a drone had struck the Russian cargo ship Sparta II in the Gulf of Finland, close to waters monitored by Finland's border guard, which said the strike was Ukrainian. Taken together, the three incidents — in the Baltic, in the Gulf of Finland and off Libya — pointed to a coordinated Ukrainian campaign against Russian shipping far beyond the Black Sea.

Why Russian warships are in Libya

Russian warships are in Libyan waters because Moscow has spent years building a military foothold in the country, and that foothold has become more important since Russia lost its sanctuary in Syria following the fall of President Bashar al-Assad in December 2024. With its Syrian bases gone or degraded, Russia moved many of its military assets to eastern Libya, where it has a cooperation agreement with the eastern-based government in Benghazi and the Libyan National Army led by Khalifa Haftar.

The Merkury had docked at Benghazi to refuel and take on supplies under that cooperation agreement. The vessel was moored in the civilian area of Benghazi seaport, close to a naval base, when it was hit, according to sources who spoke to Al Jazeera. The eastern government in Benghazi, which operates as a rival authority to the internationally recognised government in Tripoli, said its forces had destroyed four other naval drones during the attack.

Ukraine, for its part, has been operating in western Libya under what Libyan officials have described as a covert arrangement endorsed by Western governments, using the North African country's territory to strike Russian targets in the Mediterranean. Ukrainian forces, most of them drone specialists, were reported earlier this year to be operating mainly at an air base in the coastal city of Misrata, as well as at military facilities in Tripoli and the coastal town of Zawiya. In March, they used Libyan territory to strike a Russian tanker in the Mediterranean.

The arrangement, if it is as described, represents a remarkable extension of the war: Ukrainian personnel operating from a third country, with Western backing, to attack Russian military assets in a fourth country's waters. It also illustrates how thoroughly the Russia-Ukraine war has become entangled with the politics of the wider Mediterranean and North Africa.

The United Nations calls for an investigation

The United Nations Support Mission in Libya (UNSMIL) called for an independent investigation into the attacks carried out at Benghazi port and off the coast of the eastern city on Sunday. The call reflected the sensitivity of the incident: an attack by one country's forces on another country's warship, inside a third country's port, during a war that the UN has been trying to contain.

Local authorities in Benghazi said the strike on the Merkury resulted in the deaths of at least 10 Libyan and Russian crew members, according to one report — a figure that, if confirmed, would make the incident one of the deadliest single attacks on Russian naval personnel outside Ukraine since the full-scale invasion began. Other reports put the casualty figure lower, at 10 wounded. The discrepancy reflects the fog that surrounds the incident: the eastern Libyan authorities, the Russian government and the Ukrainian government each have reasons to present the outcome differently, and independent verification has been limited.

What is not in doubt is the scale of the material loss. The Merkury was one of Russia's newest surface combatants, commissioned during the war itself, and its sinking represents both a practical blow to Russia's naval capabilities in the Mediterranean and a symbolic one: a warship built to project Russian power was destroyed by drones built in Ukraine, in a port where Russia believed it was safe.

The war at home continues

While the Libya strike captured global attention, the war in Ukraine itself continued with undiminished ferocity. Russian strikes killed at least 15 people in the Kyiv region, Ukrainian officials said, and an overnight Russian attack on the city of Zaporizhzhia killed two people and wounded another 40, including four children, after striking an apartment building.

The contrast between the two stories — a daring Ukrainian success 2,400 kilometres away and continued Russian attacks on Ukrainian cities — captured the current shape of the war. Ukraine has become extraordinarily capable at long-range strikes, using domestically produced drones to hit targets deep inside Russia and now far beyond it. But it has not been able to stop Russia from striking its own cities, and the human cost of those strikes continues to mount.

The Libya operation also comes at a moment of diplomatic flux. Zelenskyy has said the United States raised a possible trade-off involving the Donbas region and NATO membership, and the broader question of how the war ends — and what Ukraine would have to give up to end it — remains unresolved. Against that backdrop, a spectacular military success serves a political purpose as well as a military one: it demonstrates to allies and adversaries alike that Ukraine remains capable of imposing costs on Russia, even far from the front line.

What the strike means

The sinking of the Merkury marks a new turn in the Ukraine war in several ways. First, it extends the conflict geographically to the southern shore of the Mediterranean — a body of water through which a significant share of world trade passes, and where the naval presence of NATO members, Russia and others has long been a source of tension. An attack on a warship in a Libyan port raises the stakes for every navy operating in those waters.

Second, it exposes the fragility of Russia's position in Libya. Moscow moved assets there after losing Syria, but Libya is a divided country with rival governments, weak institutions and a history of sudden violence. A naval base that was supposed to offer sanctuary proved to be anything but, and the eastern Libyan authorities who host the Russians now face questions about their ability — or willingness — to protect them.

Third, it demonstrates the continuing evolution of drone warfare. The operation used seven naval drones, launched from a mother ship, to sink a modern warship and damage a cargo vessel. The cost of the drones was a tiny fraction of the cost of the ships they destroyed, and the operation was conducted without putting Ukrainian personnel in direct danger. It is the latest example of a pattern that has defined this war: relatively cheap, domestically produced unmanned systems defeating vastly more expensive conventional platforms.

Fourth, it raises difficult questions about escalation. The strike took place in the territory of a third country, against the warship of a fourth, by the forces of a country at war with the warship's owner. The legal and diplomatic framework for such an operation is murky, and the UN's call for an investigation reflects the concern that the war's expansion could draw in new parties or trigger retaliation in unexpected places.

What happens next

Several developments will show how far the consequences of the Benghazi strike reach. The first is Russia's response: whether Moscow attempts to reinforce its position in Libya, withdraws assets to safer waters or retaliates against Ukrainian targets — in Libya, in Ukraine or elsewhere. Russia's options are constrained by the same factors that made the Merkury vulnerable: its Mediterranean presence is thin, its Libyan hosts are unreliable, and its naval forces are already stretched.

The second is the reaction of Libya's rival governments. The eastern authorities in Benghazi host the Russians; the western government in Tripoli has, according to reports, allowed Ukrainian operations on its territory. The strike puts both in an awkward position, and the internal politics of Libya — already volatile — could be further destabilised by the attention the incident has brought.

The third is the response of NATO and the United States. The operation was reportedly conducted with Western backing, and the question of how far that backing extends — whether to further strikes in the Mediterranean, or to operations elsewhere — will shape the next phase of the war. NATO's members have a direct interest in the security of Mediterranean shipping, and any disruption to it would affect them immediately.

And the fourth is the investigation called for by the UN mission in Libya. An independent inquiry into the attacks could establish facts — about casualties, about the involvement of third parties, about the legality of the operation — that all sides currently have an interest in obscuring. Whether such an inquiry is allowed to proceed, and what it finds, will matter for how the incident is remembered.

The Mediterranean as a new front

The Benghazi strike is best understood not as an isolated incident but as the latest step in the transformation of the Mediterranean into an active theatre of the Russia-Ukraine war. The sea has always been strategically important — a conduit for energy, for trade and for military power — but for most of the war it was a secondary concern, overshadowed by the land battle in Ukraine itself. That is changing.

Russia's Mediterranean presence has always depended on access to ports. With its Syrian bases lost or degraded after the fall of Assad, Moscow has leaned heavily on Libya — and on the eastern Libyan authorities who control Benghazi — to provide the refuelling, resupply and repair facilities its navy needs to operate in the region. The arrangement gave Russia a foothold on the southern shore of the Mediterranean, within easy reach of Europe's southern flank and of the shipping lanes that carry a large share of world trade.

Ukraine's strike challenges that foothold directly. If Russian warships cannot safely dock in Benghazi, their ability to operate in the Mediterranean is sharply reduced. They would have to sail further, refuel less often and accept greater risk — or withdraw to waters where they are safer but less useful. Either outcome would be a strategic gain for Ukraine and for the Western governments that back it.

But the strike also creates new risks for everyone who uses the Mediterranean. The sea is one of the busiest shipping routes in the world, carrying oil, gas, grain and manufactured goods between Europe, Africa, the Middle East and Asia. Drone warfare in those waters — especially warfare conducted from unmarked mother ships, against targets in civilian port areas — raises the danger of mistakes, of collateral damage and of escalation involving commercial shipping. The Maltese-flagged vessel struck hours after the Merkury is a reminder that the line between military and civilian targets in a crowded sea is thin.

NATO's southern members — Italy, Greece, Spain and France among them — have a direct interest in keeping the Mediterranean stable. They will be watching closely to see whether the Benghazi strike is the beginning of a sustained Ukrainian campaign in the region or a one-off operation. If it is the former, the alliance will face difficult questions about its own posture in the sea, about the protection of shipping and about the extent of its support for Ukrainian operations far from Ukraine.

The drone war at sea

The sinking of the Merkury is also the latest demonstration of a revolution in naval warfare that the Russia-Ukraine war has accelerated: the rise of the naval drone. Ukraine began the war with almost no navy of its own, after Russia seized or destroyed much of its fleet in 2014 and 2022. What it built instead was a force of unmanned surface vessels — small, fast, cheap and expendable — that has sunk or damaged a string of Russian warships in the Black Sea, including the flagship Moskva in 2022.

The Benghazi operation extends that logic to a new domain. Seven naval drones, launched from a mother ship, sank a modern corvette and damaged a cargo vessel at a cost that is a tiny fraction of the value of the ships destroyed. The operation required no Ukrainian sailors to be put at risk, no aircraft to be flown into defended airspace and no missiles to be fired from Ukrainian territory. It is, in the cold arithmetic of warfare, an extraordinarily efficient exchange.

The implications reach far beyond this war. Navies around the world are studying Ukraine's drone operations and drawing conclusions about the future of naval warfare: that large, expensive surface ships are increasingly vulnerable to small, cheap unmanned systems; that the advantage is shifting from the side with the bigger fleet to the side with the better drones; and that the defence of a warship against a swarm of sea drones is a problem that no navy has yet fully solved.

Russia, for its part, has been on the receiving end of this lesson repeatedly — in the Black Sea, in the Baltic and now in the Mediterranean. Its navy, built for a different era of warfare, has proven vulnerable to weapons that cost a fraction of the ships they destroy. The sinking of the Merkury is unlikely to be the last such incident, and the question for Moscow is not only how to protect its remaining ships, but whether the naval strategy it has pursued for decades still makes sense in an age of drones.

For now, the wreck of the Merkury lies on the seabed off Benghazi, its bow the only part still visible above the water. It is a monument to a war that refuses to stay within its borders, and a warning that in modern warfare, no port — however distant from the front line — can be assumed to be safe.
