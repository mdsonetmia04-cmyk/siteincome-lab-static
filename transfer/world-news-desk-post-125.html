Spain votes on Sunday, and the polls say it is about to change its government. On 29 November 2026, Spaniards will elect their Cortes Generales in a snap general election called by Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez after his government collapsed — and, if the final polls are right, the result will be a conservative victory that ends eight years of Socialist-led rule and brings the far-right Vox party into government for the first time at national level.

The numbers are stark. The final survey that can legally be published before the election, from Target Point and published on 23 November, gives Alberto Núñez Feijóo's People's Party (PP) 31.5 percent of the vote and between 127 and 131 seats. Sánchez's Spanish Socialist Workers' Party (PSOE) stands at 28.8 percent and between 114 and 118 seats. Vox, the hard-right party led by Santiago Abascal, stands at 17.4 percent and between 59 and 63 seats. Together, the PP and Vox would take 48.9 percent of the vote and between 186 and 194 deputies — comfortably above the 176 seats needed for an absolute majority in the 350-seat Congress of Deputies.

A separate forecast, from Le Millénaire and based on 41 polls and 20,000 simulations, reaches the same conclusion by a different route. According to its run of 19 November, the PP wins a median of 125 seats, ahead of the PSOE on 114, and Vox rises from 33 seats in 2023 to a median of 64. The PP and Vox together reach at least 176 seats in 76 simulations in 100, with a median total of 191 seats. The PSOE comes first in seats in only 38 simulations in 100. In the most likely scenario — 61 simulations in 100 — the PP and Vox have a majority and the PP is the largest party.

It is Spain's sixth general election in eleven years. And it may be the most consequential of them all.

How Spain got here

The election was called on 5 October, after a government that had been living on borrowed time finally ran out of it. On 2 October 2026, the seven deputies of Junts, the Catalan separatist party whose support Sánchez had relied on, voted against his housing decree. Without a budget of his own since 2023, and without the parliamentary arithmetic to pass one, the Prime Minister dissolved the Cortes and called elections for 29 November, framing the choice as one between the far right and social justice.

Spain will hold its sixth general election in eleven years on 29 November, after Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez dissolved parliament and called a snap vote following persistent domestic crises, legislative gridlock, and declining voter support. Opinion polls indicate that mainstream conservatives and the far-right Vox party are positioned to secure a combined majority, potentially ending eight years of Socialist-led governance.

The housing crisis was the trigger, but the underlying causes run deeper. Sánchez has governed since 2018, first through a vote of no confidence and then through two elections, always dependent on a patchwork of regional and left-wing parties. The 2023 election left him with a government that could only survive with the support of Catalan and Basque nationalists, and that support was always conditional. When Junts walked away over housing, the arithmetic collapsed. There was no longer a majority for anything — not a budget, not a decree, not a government.

The campaign that followed has been dominated by the same issues that brought the government down: housing, the cost of living, immigration, and the question of who governs Spain. On Thursday, 19 November, Sánchez and Feijóo squared off in the campaign's only head-to-head debate, hosted by Spain's Television Academy and moderated by María Casado: five blocks, from housing to immigration, with Feijóo opening. The debate, broadcast on RTVE's La 1 at 10 p.m. Spain time, was the set-piece of the campaign — and the polls suggest it did not change the direction of travel.

The polls: a right-wing majority, with one big exception

The striking thing about the final polls is how consistent they are. Of eighteen pollsters with recent numbers, sixteen put the PP first. The 40dB tracking poll for El País and Cadena SER, based on 2,000 people surveyed online between 12 and 16 November, has the PP on 31.8 percent and 131 seats, the PSOE on 30.6 percent and 120 seats, and Vox on 17.5 percent and 58 seats — the PP and Vox together on 189. Sigma Dos for El Mundo, based on 5,370 interviews between 3 and 13 November, has the PP on 31 percent and 126 to 132 seats, the PSOE on 25.6 percent, and Vox on 19.7 percent with 62 to 67 seats, putting the right on 188 to 199. NC Report for La Razón pushes that to 201 to 205, with Vox on 66 to 68. Data10 for OKDiario gives Vox its best number yet, 20.4 percent and 71 seats, for a combined 202. Target Point for El Debate has 189 to 197, Sondaxe for La Voz de Galicia 192, and GAD3 for ABC, in the field 3 to 5 November, had 199.

The PSOE swings between 25.5 percent (Data10) and 30.6 percent (40dB), a five-point gap between pollsters working the same days — a reminder that the polls are not a single instrument but many, each with its own methods and its own biases. But on the question that matters — whether the PP and Vox together clear 176 — the private pollsters are nearly unanimous. They do.

There are two exceptions, and they are telling. The CIS, the state-run polling outfit headed by José Félix Tezanos, released its pre-election survey on 12 November, based on 45,074 interviews: PSOE 33.3 percent and 134 to 143 seats, PP 25.5 percent and 98 to 106, Vox 18.4 percent and 57 to 65. That would leave the PP and Vox stuck on 155 to 171 — short of a majority. The catch: the interviews were done between 7 and 30 October, almost all of them before the 28 October high-speed rail crash at Urda, which killed 69 people on the Madrid-Seville line, and before Transport Minister Óscar Puente quit over it. Spain's Central Electoral Board ruled that the institute had broken electoral law, though it stopped short of opening proceedings against him.

The other outlier is SW Demoscopia, which runs a daily poll for the television channel La Séptima. Its 14 November release, on 8,000 phone interviews, has the PSOE on 31.7 percent and 133 seats, the PP on 28.3 percent and 113, and Vox on 17.1 percent and 60. That puts the right on 173 — three short of a majority. POLITICO's Poll of Polls had the PP and the PSOE tied at 30 percent on 15 November, with Vox at 18 percent.

The gap between the pollsters is, in the end, a gap between two stories. In one, the right has a comfortable majority and the only question is how large it is. In the other, the race is close, the Socialists are competitive, and three seats could decide everything. The truth, as always, will be known on Sunday night.

The 2023 warning

There is a reason Sánchez's supporters are not giving up, and it is called 2023. The polls said the same thing then. In the last election, the PP and Vox finished with 170 seats — six short of a majority — and Sánchez won a new term 116 days after the vote, after a long and tortuous negotiation that ended with an amnesty for Catalan separatists and the support of the parties the PP had counted on to stay away. The polls were wrong in 2023, and they could be wrong again.

The comparison is not exact. In 2023, Vox was smaller and the PP was larger, and the arithmetic of the right's failure was a matter of a handful of seats in a handful of constituencies. This time, the right's lead is larger in most polls — a median of 191 seats in the Le Millénaire forecast, against a majority line of 176, is a margin of 15 seats, not three. But the electoral system, which penalises mid-sized parties, can produce surprises, and the polls have been wrong before. Sánchez's campaign has been built on exactly that hope: a highly mediatised effort to bring back its abstainers, the voters who supported the PSOE in the past but stayed home in recent elections.

The Socialist campaign is relying on turnout. Target Point estimates participation at 74 percent, one point higher than in its previous survey, and El Debate reported that Sánchez's team believes a figure above 75 percent could change the result. The PSOE retains 68.6 percent of those who backed it in 2023 and loses 9.7 percent to the PP. Feijóo keeps about three in four of his voters. He is also the highest-rated leader, with an average score of 4.43, against Sánchez on 3.81, Abascal on 3.48, Colau on 3.14 and Montero on 2.69.

Both main parties have grown since Target Point's previous survey of 12 November. PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo has gained 0.9 points and Sánchez 1.8, which has cut the distance between them from 3.6 points to 2.7. The Socialist advance has not altered the balance between the blocs. The PP's gain has come at the expense of Vox, which has dropped 1.3 points during the campaign. According to the poll, 9.3 percent of Vox voters now intend to back the PP, a share that rose after the televised head-to-head between Feijóo and Sánchez on 19 November. The PSOE now attracts 31.5 percent of Sumar's former voters.

What that means is that the campaign has not been static. The Socialists have closed the gap on votes, even as the seats have barely moved. In the 40dB tracking poll of 16 November, the PSOE had risen above 30 percent for the first time since 2023 — PP 31.5 percent, PSOE 30.3 percent, Vox 18.0 percent — but the seats barely moved: PP 130, PSOE 119, Vox 62, so 192 for the right. The votes are closer than the seats. That is the nature of Spain's electoral system, and it is why the Socialists can win the argument and still lose the election.

Vox and the price of power

If the polls are right, the central question of the next government will not be whether the PP wins, but what Vox demands in return. Four main forces will sit in the new Congress, but the arithmetic is simple: Alberto Núñez Feijóo's PP needs Vox to reach a majority, but Vox, now the country's third force, could sell its support dearly. Feijóo keeps saying he wants to govern alone while refusing to rule Vox out, but on these numbers there is no government without Santiago Abascal.

Vox has risen from 33 seats in 2023 to a median of 64 in the Le Millénaire forecast, with at least 70 seats in 34 simulations in 100. In the polls, it stands between 17.1 and 20.4 percent — roughly double its 2023 vote. That is a transformation: from a party that the PP could afford to ignore, to a party without which the PP cannot govern. With 40dB's 58 seats, Feijóo negotiates from strength. With Data10's 71 seats, he negotiates from weakness. The difference between those two numbers is the difference between a coalition partner and a kingmaker.

Below 60 seats for Vox, Feijóo can govern alone with outside support. Above that, he will need a formal arrangement — and Vox will name its price. The party's demands are well known: a hard line on immigration, a rollback of the regional autonomy arrangements, a confrontation with the Catalan and Basque nationalists, and a cultural agenda that reverses much of what the Sánchez government has done on gender, memory and the environment. Whether Feijóo can accept those demands, and whether he can do so without breaking his own party or alarming the European Union, is the question that will dominate the weeks after the vote.

On the left, the picture is one of fragmentation. Frente Amplio, the new left-wing list, is forecast at 10 seats — far from Sumar's 31 seats in 2023. The three left-wing lists — the PSOE, Frente Amplio and Podemos — add up to 37 percent and between 125 and 130 seats, against the right's 186 to 194. The left has lost the arithmetic, and it has lost it decisively. Even the most optimistic Socialist scenario — the CIS poll's 134 to 143 seats for the PSOE — leaves the left far short of a majority.

What a right-wing government would change

The stakes of Sunday's vote extend well beyond Spain. A PP-Vox government would be the first time the far right has entered national government in Spain since the end of the Franco dictatorship, and it would come at a moment when the far right is already in government, or close to it, in several European countries. For the European Union, the question is what a Spanish government that includes Vox would mean for the bloc's migration policy, its climate commitments, and its support for Ukraine.

On Ukraine, the signs are mixed. The PP has been a consistent supporter of military aid, and Feijóo has said he will maintain it. Vox's position has been more ambiguous, and the party's scepticism about the European Union's foreign policy is well documented. On migration, a PP-Vox government would almost certainly take a harder line than Sánchez's — tighter border controls, faster deportations, and a confrontation with the non-governmental organisations that rescue migrants in the Mediterranean. On climate, Vox has called the green transition a fraud, and a government that includes it would be expected to slow Spain's renewable energy programme and challenge the EU's emissions targets.

On the economy, the differences are less stark. Both the PP and the PSOE are committed to the European Union's fiscal rules, and Spain's public debt — one of the highest in the eurozone — constrains any government. The housing crisis, which brought the Sánchez government down, will be the first test of the new government: the PP has promised to build more homes and to liberalise the planning system, but it has not explained how it will do so quickly, or at what cost.

For Europe, the deeper question is what Spain's election says about the direction of the continent. In 2023, Spain was the exception — the country where the far right was kept out of government by a last-minute Socialist comeback. In 2026, if the polls are right, Spain will join the countries where the far right is in government, or propping one up. That is a change with consequences that will be felt in Brussels, in Paris, in Berlin, and in every capital where the question of how to deal with the far right is still open.

The train crash that changed the campaign

One event shadows the final polls more than any other: the high-speed rail crash at Urda on 28 October, which killed 69 people on the Madrid-Seville line. The crash, one of the worst rail disasters in Spain's modern history, dominated the news for days, and Transport Minister Óscar Puente quit over it. The CIS poll, whose interviews were conducted almost entirely before the crash, is the only survey that puts the PSOE first — and the question of how much the disaster moved votes is one of the great unknowns of this election.

The crash matters for two reasons. The first is political: a disaster on the government's watch, on a flagship piece of infrastructure, is the kind of event that can shift an election — particularly when a minister resigns over it. The second is methodological: it explains why the pollsters disagree. Surveys conducted before 28 October measured a different country from surveys conducted after it, and the gap between the CIS and the private pollsters is, in part, a gap between those two moments. Whether the crash hurt the government or rallied sympathy for it will not be known until Sunday night.

Sunday night — and after

The campaign closes on Friday, 27 November. Spanish electoral law bans the publication of voting-intention polls in the five days before an election, a period that begins on 24 November, so the polls published by 23 November are the last read before the vote. From now until Sunday, the parties will campaign without new numbers — and the voters will decide without them.

On Sunday, 29 November, polls close at 8 p.m. Spain time, 2 p.m. Eastern Time, and an hour later in the Canary Islands. The first exit polls will follow within minutes, and the count — Spain counts fast — should give a clear picture within a few hours. There are five scenarios for election night, according to Le Millénaire's forecast. The most likely, in 61 simulations in 100, is a PP-Vox majority with the PP as the largest party. In 16 simulations, the PP dominates clearly. In 11, Vox is close behind. In the remaining simulations, the right falls short — and Spain faces another long negotiation, another hung parliament, and the possibility of yet another election.

Sánchez stays on as acting prime minister until Congress confirms a successor. If the PP and Vox have a majority, that process could be quick — a matter of weeks. If they do not, it could take months, as it did in 2023, when 116 days passed between the vote and the formation of a government. And if no government can be formed at all, Spain will vote again — for the seventh time in eleven years.

For Pedro Sánchez, Sunday is the end of a long road. He has been prime minister for eight years, through a pandemic, a war in Europe, a cost-of-living crisis and the slow collapse of his parliamentary majority. He has survived votes of no confidence, leadership challenges, and an election in 2023 that everyone said he would lose. On Sunday, the polls say he will lose this one. But the polls said that in 2023 too — and he is still prime minister. That is the one fact that neither side can forget.
