Elon Musk, the founder of Neuralink. The US Food and Drug Administration has cleared his company to launch LINK, the first pivotal trial of an implantable brain-computer interface in the United States, for people with severe paralysis caused by ALS. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

The brain chip is moving from experiment to exam. Elon Musk's neurotechnology company Neuralink has received approval from the United States Food and Drug Administration to launch LINK, the first pivotal clinical trial of an implantable brain-computer interface in the United States — a study that will test whether people with severe paralysis caused by amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, or ALS, can control digital devices using only their thoughts, and whether the device is safe and effective enough, over the long term, to be sold as a medical product.

"We've received FDA approval to launch LINK, the first pivotal trial for an implantable brain-computer interface in the US," Neuralink announced on X on November 25, 2026. "This builds off of our work with 30+ participants with Neuralink to date." The announcement marks the most important regulatory milestone in the company's history — and a turning point for a technology that, less than three years ago, had never been implanted in a human being.

The LINK Pivotal Study will evaluate the safety and effectiveness of Neuralink's brain-computer interface system in adults with severe paralysis due to ALS, with a focus on functional outcomes that improve independence in daily life. Participants will be tested on their ability to use the technology to control digital devices through thought — moving a cursor, typing, communicating — and the study will also monitor the long-term safety and reliability of the system across multiple clinical sites in the United States. Enrolment is now open, through the company's Patient Registry, and the company says it plans to expand access over time.

There is an important distinction to be clear about from the start. The FDA's approval covers the trial, not the sale of the device. Neuralink's implant has not been approved for general medical use, and the company continues to describe its devices as investigational, available only through authorised clinical research under an Investigational Device Exemption. A pivotal trial is, in effect, the final exam: the large, carefully controlled study whose results a company submits when it asks the regulator to let it sell its product. Passing the exam is not guaranteed. But being allowed to sit it is, for Neuralink, a very big deal.

What the trial will test

ALS is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that damages the nerve cells controlling voluntary muscle movement. It takes away, in stages, the ability to walk, to speak, to swallow and, eventually, to breathe — while leaving the mind, in most cases, intact. Around 30,000 Americans are living with the disease, and most people survive for two to five years after diagnosis. There is no cure, and the treatments that exist can only slow the decline. For people in the later stages of ALS, the loss of the ability to communicate — to speak, to type, to signal yes or no — is often described as the cruellest part of the disease: a mind that is fully present, trapped in a body that will no longer obey it.

That is the problem Neuralink's device is designed to solve. The system consists of a small, coin-sized implant — the N1 — placed in the region of the brain responsible for planning movements, and a surgical robot, the R1, that inserts 64 flexible threads, thinner than a human hair, into the brain tissue. The threads read the electrical signals produced when the person intends to move, and the implant transmits those signals wirelessly to a computer, which translates them into commands: move a cursor, click a mouse, type a word. The person does not need to move a muscle. They need only to think about moving.

The LINK study will test that promise in a way that earlier studies could not. Neuralink's previous trials were early feasibility studies — small, exploratory, designed to show that the device could be implanted safely and that it worked at all. LINK is different. It is a pivotal study, which means it is designed to produce the evidence the FDA will use to decide whether the device should be approved for the market. It will enrol adults with severe paralysis due to ALS in the United States, at multiple clinical sites, and follow them for around 24 months after implantation, measuring both whether they can control digital devices through thought and whether the device remains safe over the long term.

The study will look, in particular, at functional outcomes — the things that matter in daily life, rather than in a laboratory. Can a participant send a message to a family member? Can they browse the internet? Can they control the lights, the television, the thermostat in their own home? Can they do these things reliably, day after day, without the device failing or the signal degrading? Those are the questions a pivotal trial is designed to answer, and they are the questions that will decide whether a brain-computer interface becomes a medical product or remains a research curiosity.

Thirty people, and what they have already done

The LINK trial builds on work that began less than three years ago, with a single patient. On January 28, 2024, Noland Arbaugh, a 29-year-old who had been paralysed from the shoulders down in a diving accident in 2016, became the first person to receive a Neuralink implant, at the Barrow Neurological Institute in Phoenix, Arizona. Musk announced the surgery on X the next day. Within weeks, Arbaugh was controlling a computer cursor with his thoughts, browsing the internet, and playing chess online — a demonstration, broadcast live on X in March 2024, that turned a science-fiction premise into a visible, ordinary fact.

Arbaugh's implant was not without problems. In an interview afterwards, he said that 85 percent of the device's electrode threads had detached, because his brain had shifted roughly three times as much as Neuralink had expected. A software update largely restored the device's function, and later patients have not reported the same problem — but the episode was a reminder that putting threads into a living brain is not the same as putting them into a model of one, and that the brain moves, swells and changes in ways that are hard to predict.

The second participant, known only as Alex, was implanted in July 2024, also at Barrow, and went home the next day. The third, Brad Smith, became the first ALS patient to receive an implant, in November 2024 — and his story has become the most moving of all. Smith, who had lost the ability to speak, was able to communicate using brain signals alone, and to speak again through an AI-cloned version of his own voice. "I wrote this with my brain," he posted, in a message that was shared around the world. By October 2026, company-reported data showed that one ALS participant had reached 75 words per minute at a 5.8 percent word error rate, with a 125,000-word vocabulary, in the first week of use — a result from a single participant, not peer-reviewed, but striking all the same. A target of 140 words per minute remains.

Since then, the numbers have grown steadily. Neuralink's last official total, given in January 2026, was 21 participants enrolled in its trials worldwide. The company now says that more than 30 people have received its implant, through early feasibility studies in the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom. Independent trackers, counting publicly identified patients and hospital reports, put the figure at 35 as of November 25, 2026. The patients include people with spinal cord injuries and people with ALS, implanted at hospitals including Barrow Neurological Institute, the University of Miami Health system, UT Southwestern, Toronto Western Hospital and University College London Hospitals. Some have played video games — Counter-Strike 2, Mario Kart, even GTA VI — with their thoughts. Some have typed, browsed and worked. One, Kenny, who has ALS, speaks with his own synthesised voice.

How Neuralink got here

Neuralink was founded in 2016 by Musk, and first publicly reported in March 2017. For its first six years, it was a laboratory company, testing its devices in animals and refining the robot that would one day implant them in people. In May 2023, it received FDA clearance to begin human trials, under an investigational device exemption, and in September 2023 it opened recruitment for its first human trial — the PRIME study, for Precise Robotically Implanted Brain-Computer Interface — for people with quadriplegia from spinal cord injury or ALS. The first implant followed in January 2024.

The years since have been a blur of expansion. The PRIME study grew to multiple sites. Trials opened in Canada, at Toronto Western Hospital, which performed the country's first Neuralink implant surgeries on two patients with cervical spinal cord injuries in August and September 2025, and in the United Kingdom, at University College London Hospitals. A trial opened in the United Arab Emirates. And in September 2024, Neuralink announced Blindsight, a separate programme that aims to restore vision by stimulating the visual cortex in blind people whose visual cortex is undamaged — a different device, for a different sense, but built on the same idea that the brain can be read, and written to, by a machine.

The company has also grown as a business. Neuralink remains private, but its last reported fundraising was $650 million in June 2025, at a reported valuation of around $9 billion. It is, by the standards of medical device companies, an unusually visible one: its milestones are announced on X, its patients appear in livestreams, and its founder is the most famous entrepreneur in the world. That visibility has brought it attention, and scrutiny, in equal measure.

The competition, and the questions

Neuralink is not the only company working on brain-computer interfaces, and it is not the first to put one in a human being. Synchron, an Australian-American company, has been implanting its Stentrode device — which is delivered through a blood vessel, without open brain surgery — since 2019, and has its own FDA-authorised trial in the United States. Blackrock Neurotech, founded in 2008, has implanted its Utah array in dozens of patients over more than two decades of research. Academic laboratories at Brown, Stanford and elsewhere have been decoding brain signals since the early 2000s. What Neuralink has done differently is scale: more electrodes, a robot to implant them, a wireless system, and a pace of human testing that no other company has matched.

The LINK trial will be judged against a set of questions that apply to the whole field, not just to Neuralink. The first is safety, and not only in the short term. Brain surgery carries risks — infection, bleeding, seizures — and a device that sits in the brain for years must be shown to be safe for years, not just for months. The thread retraction in Arbaugh's case was a warning: the brain is not a stable platform, and a device that works on day one may not work on day one hundred. The 24-month follow-up in the LINK study is designed, in part, to answer that question.

The second is reliability. A brain-computer interface that works in a laboratory, with engineers standing by, is a different thing from one that works in a person's home, every day, for years. The signal must remain clear, the software must remain stable, and the device must keep working as the brain changes around it. The history of medical devices is full of technologies that worked brilliantly in trials and failed in the real world.

The third is access. If LINK succeeds, and the device is approved, who will get it, and who will pay? Brain surgery is expensive, and the device, the robot and the follow-up care will not be cheap. ALS is a disease that strikes people of all incomes, and a treatment that only the wealthy can afford would be a bitter outcome for a technology whose promise is, above all, human. The company has not disclosed a price, and it is too early to ask. But the question will come, and it will matter.

The fourth is what the technology means beyond medicine. A device that can read the brain's intentions — even in the narrow sense of intended movement — raises questions that no regulator has yet had to answer at scale. Who owns the data the device produces? What happens to it? Could it be used for purposes the patient did not consent to? Neuralink's device, for now, reads only the signals of intended movement, in a small patch of the motor cortex. It does not read thoughts, in the ordinary sense of the word. But the questions will not go away, and they will grow sharper as the devices grow more capable.

Why ALS, and why now

There is a reason the first pivotal trial is for ALS, rather than for spinal cord injury or stroke, the other conditions Neuralink has studied. ALS is, in a cruel sense, the ideal test case for a brain-computer interface. The disease destroys the motor neurons that carry signals from the brain to the muscles, but it leaves the brain itself — and the intention to move — largely intact. The signals the implant reads are still there, in the motor cortex, firing as the person imagines moving a hand that no longer responds. The implant does not need to repair the broken connection between brain and body. It needs only to listen to the brain, and to carry the message somewhere else.

That is also why the trial's focus is on functional outcomes rather than on raw technical measures. The FDA does not approve medical devices because they are clever. It approves them because they help people do things they could not otherwise do. For a person with ALS, the things that matter are ordinary and profound at the same time: sending a text message to a daughter, turning on a light, choosing what to watch on television, saying goodnight to a spouse without waiting for someone to guess what they mean. The LINK study will measure those things, or their equivalents, and its success will be judged by whether participants can do them — reliably, independently, day after day.

The timing matters, too. A pivotal trial is not a short undertaking. With around 24 months of follow-up after implantation, and time needed to enrol participants, run the study and analyse the results, the LINK trial will take years to complete. For the company, that is a long road to market: the results must be submitted to the FDA as part of a premarket approval application, the most rigorous form of device review, and the agency must be satisfied that the device is both safe and effective before it can be sold. For the patients, the road is longer still in one sense and shorter in another: longer, because approval is years away; shorter, because enrolment is open now, and the trial itself is a form of access.

There is also the question of what comes after ALS. If LINK succeeds, the same device — or a version of it — could be tested for other conditions: spinal cord injury, brainstem stroke, and the other forms of paralysis that Neuralink's earlier studies have already explored. The company's Blindsight programme, which aims to restore vision by stimulating the visual cortex, is a reminder that the technology is not limited to movement. And the speech programme, which has already produced 75 words per minute from a single ALS participant, points to a future in which a brain-computer interface does not merely replace a mouse, but replaces a voice. Each of those futures depends on the same thing: the LINK trial working, and working safely, over years rather than weeks.

What happens next

Enrolment for the LINK Pivotal Study is now open. Adults in the United States with ALS and severe paralysis can apply through Neuralink's Patient Registry, and the company says it plans to expand the study across multiple clinical sites over time. Applicants will undergo an initial assessment and neuroimaging before receiving the implant, which is performed using the R1 robot, and will then be taught to move a cursor using only their thoughts. They will be followed for around 24 months after implantation, with regular assessments of both their ability to control digital devices and the long-term safety of the system.

The company has not disclosed its target enrolment, its anticipated study duration, or its participating clinical sites. No results, enrolment target or completion date has been published. What is known is that the study is recruiting, that it is the first pivotal trial of its kind in the United States, and that its results — when they come, years from now — will decide whether a brain-computer interface becomes a product that a doctor can prescribe, or remains an experiment that a few dozen people have tried.

For the people with ALS who are applying now, the stakes are more immediate. ALS does not wait. The disease progresses, month by month, taking away the abilities it has not yet taken. A trial that takes two years to complete is, for some applicants, a race against their own decline. That is why the opening of enrolment matters as much as the FDA's approval: it means that the study is not an announcement, but a door that is actually open.

Three years ago, no human being had a Neuralink implant. Today, more than thirty do. One of them, who could not speak, speaks again in a voice that sounds like his own. Another, who could not move, plays chess. A third types at 75 words a minute, with his thoughts alone. The LINK trial is the step that will decide whether those stories are the beginning of a medical revolution, or a remarkable footnote. The exam has begun. The results will take years. And for the people waiting — the 30,000 Americans living with ALS, and the millions more around the world with paralysis of every kind — the wait has already been long enough.
