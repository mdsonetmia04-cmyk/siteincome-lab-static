TITLE: Trump Just Ordered Federal Executions to Speed Up — After Newsom Commuted All 548 of California's Death Row Sentences LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: President Trump signed a November 23 memo ordering faster federal executions and the death penalty for child murder and trafficking killings ---BODY---

President Donald J. Trump. President Donald Trump has ordered the federal government to carry out death sentences faster and to seek the death penalty in more murder cases, in the most aggressive expansion of capital punishment at the federal level in years. On Monday, November 23, 2026, at an event at the Department of Justice in Washington, Trump signed a memorandum directing Attorney General Todd Blanche to pursue the death penalty for the first-degree murder of a child, for murders committed during rape, sexual abuse, sexual exploitation of a child or sex trafficking, and for murders committed during kidnapping or human trafficking — and to make sure that federal death sentences already handed down are carried out "as expeditiously as possible". The order arrived the same month that California Governor Gavin Newsom commuted the death sentences of all 548 people on California's death row to life in prison without parole, and Trump used the signing ceremony to attack that decision in front of an audience that included the relatives of murder victims. "Once jurors have spoken, capital punishment will be swift and certain," Trump said, according to reports from the event. "The ultimate crimes deserve the ultimate punishment."

The memorandum, titled "Expeditious Enforcement of Capital Sentences and the Pursuit of Justice for Victims of Heinous Crimes", is short, direct and sweeping in its intent. It does not set deadlines, and it does not change any law. What it does is instruct the Justice Department to use every tool it already has — faster appeals, opposition to delaying tactics, requests for courts to move quickly, and the removal of what it calls unnecessary administrative delays — to shorten the time between a federal death sentence and the execution chamber at the United States Penitentiary in Terre Haute, Indiana. It also widens the list of federal murder cases in which prosecutors are told to seek death, adding three categories to the two that Trump set out in an executive order on his first day back in office. For the families of victims who spoke at the Justice Department event, the message was unmistakable: after years in which the federal death penalty was effectively frozen, the government is moving again, and it intends to move fast.

The timing is not an accident. Newsom, who is widely expected to seek the Democratic nomination for president in 2028, announced earlier in November that he was commuting the sentences of the more than 500 people on California's death row — 548 inmates, according to the reports that followed — from death to life in prison without the possibility of parole. It was the largest single commutation of death sentences in American history, and it emptied the largest death row in the country in a single stroke. Trump called the decision one of the most shocking acts of injustice in the country's history, and he used the families gathered at the Justice Department to make the contrast explicit: while California was sparing every condemned prisoner in the state, the federal government would be doing the opposite. "Every person commuted from California's death row will die in prison. Not one walks out," Newsom's office said in response, arguing that the commutations were a matter of justice, not leniency. The clash between the two men is now the sharpest fight over the death penalty in America — and it is being fought in public, in the run-up to a presidential election in which both men may be on the ballot.

What the memorandum actually orders

The memorandum has three sections, and each one does a different job. Section 1 states the policy. It says that a prolonged delay in carrying out a death sentence "is a grave offense to the justice system that denies a victim's family the closure they deserve", and that the Department of Justice "must take every step to ensure that those who commit the most heinous crimes promptly face the full consequences of their actions". The language is deliberately moral rather than legal: it frames speed not as an administrative preference but as a matter of justice for victims' families, and it sets the tone for everything that follows. The White House fact sheet published with the memorandum makes the same point in plainer terms, saying that the president signed it "to pursue capital punishment for heinous federal crimes and expedite the enforcement of capital sentences".

Section 2 is the part that widens the death penalty. It directs the Attorney General, "when appropriate and consistent with applicable law", to pursue federal jurisdiction and seek the death penalty for three categories of federal capital crime that were not on the list before: the first-degree murder of a minor; murder committed in the course of rape, sexual abuse, sexual exploitation of a child, or sex trafficking; and murder committed in the course of kidnapping or human trafficking. These are in addition to the crimes named in Section 3(b) of Executive Order 14164, which Trump signed on January 20, 2025 — his first day in office — and which covered the murder of a law-enforcement officer and capital crimes committed by a person in the country illegally. The combined list now covers five categories of federal murder in which prosecutors are instructed to seek death, and the three new ones are the most emotive: they are all crimes against children, or crimes in which a killing is bound up with sexual violence or human trafficking. The choice of categories is deliberate. They are the cases in which public support for the death penalty is strongest, and in which opposition to it is hardest to sustain, and they give the Justice Department a clear instruction: in these cases, seek death.

Section 3 is the part that speeds up executions. It directs the Attorney General to "take all lawful and appropriate measures" so that federally imposed death sentences are carried out as quickly as possible, and it lists four specific steps: giving priority to the prompt resolution of direct appeals, petitions for writs of habeas corpus, and other post-conviction proceedings; opposing what it calls "dilatory litigation tactics"; asking courts for expedited consideration wherever appropriate; and eliminating unnecessary administrative delays. None of these steps is new in itself — prosecutors have always been able to ask courts to move faster, and the Justice Department has always opposed delay — but the memorandum makes speed a formal, written priority of the department, and it gives the Attorney General a direct presidential instruction to pursue it. The memorandum applies only to federal cases. It does not mention state death sentences, California or Newsom by name, and it sets no deadlines. Its power lies in the instruction itself, and in the signal it sends to every federal prosecutor in the country.

The signing ceremony was staged to make that signal as loud as possible. Trump signed the memorandum at the close of an appearance at the Department of Justice in which multiple relatives of murder victims spoke, and in which he criticised Newsom's commutations directly. The families described the years they had waited for justice, and the president presented his order as the answer to that waiting. The event was, in effect, two announcements at once: a policy change and a political attack, delivered together, in the same room, on the same day. The White House published the full text of the memorandum and a fact sheet on November 23, and the fact sheet was published at 23:33 UTC — late in the evening in Washington, and early on Tuesday morning in London — which meant that the news landed in the American morning papers and in the European press at the same time, and that by the time the sun rose over the East Coast, the story was already everywhere.

The Newsom clash: 548 commutations against one memorandum

To understand why Trump signed this memorandum now, you have to understand what Newsom did first. California has the largest death row in the United States, and it has not carried out an execution since 2006. Newsom imposed a moratorium on executions when he took office in 2019, closing the execution chamber at San Quentin and saying that the death penalty was, in his words, inconsistent with the state's values. In November 2026, he went further, commuting the sentences of all 548 people on the state's death row to life in prison without the possibility of parole. The decision was, by any measure, the largest single act of clemency in the history of the American death penalty, and it was also a political statement: Newsom is widely expected to run for president in 2028, and the commutations put him on the record as the most prominent opponent of capital punishment in American politics.

Trump's response was equally deliberate. He did not merely criticise the commutations; he used them as the occasion for a federal policy change that moves in exactly the opposite direction. At the Justice Department event, he described Newsom as "a terrible governor", according to Al Jazeera's report of the event, and he presented the commutations as an injustice to the families of the victims whose killers had been spared. The relatives who spoke at the event reinforced the point, describing the pain of waiting for a sentence that would now never be carried out. The contrast could not have been sharper: on the same day, in the same country, one governor was sparing 548 condemned prisoners, and the president was ordering the federal government to execute its own condemned prisoners faster. The two men are now the two poles of the American debate over the death penalty, and the debate is no longer abstract. It is a contest between two named politicians, with two named policies, and it is being fought in the open, in the months before a presidential campaign begins in earnest.

The numbers behind the clash are worth setting out plainly. California's death row held 548 people when Newsom commuted their sentences, according to the reports that followed his announcement, and the figure of "more than 500" was used in the earliest accounts before the precise number was confirmed. Every one of those 548 sentences is now a sentence of life in prison without parole, and Newsom's office has said, in response to criticism, that "every person commuted from California's death row will die in prison. Not one walks out". The point of that sentence is to answer the charge of leniency: the commutations do not free anyone, and they do not shorten anyone's time in prison. They remove only the prospect of execution, and they replace it with the certainty of dying in prison. For the families of the victims, that distinction may not be enough, and Trump is betting that it is not enough for the wider public either. For Newsom, the distinction is the whole argument: that the state can punish the worst crimes with the severest penalty short of death, and that it does not need to kill to do justice.

The federal death row today: three men left after Biden's commutations

The memorandum lands on a federal death row that is, by historical standards, almost empty. When Joe Biden left office, he commuted the sentences of 37 of the 40 people on federal death row to life in prison without parole, in December 2024, leaving only three men still under a federal sentence of death: Dylann Roof, who murdered nine people at a church in Charleston, South Carolina, in 2015; Dzhokhar Tsarnaev, who carried out the Boston Marathon bombing in 2013; and Robert Bowers, who murdered 11 people at a synagogue in Pittsburgh in 2018. Those three were the exception to Biden's commutations because, in the words of his administration, their crimes were so grave that they should remain eligible for execution. Every other federal death sentence was commuted, and the federal death row that Trump inherited in January 2025 held three men.

That number matters, because it shapes what the memorandum can actually do in the short term. The order to carry out death sentences "as expeditiously as possible" applies, in the first instance, to the three men who remain on federal death row, and to any new death sentences handed down in federal cases from now on. The three men have all exhausted, or are close to exhausting, their direct appeals, and their cases have been through the federal courts for years. Roof's appeals have been rejected by the Fourth Circuit and the Supreme Court has declined to hear his case. Tsarnaev's death sentence was reinstated by the Supreme Court in 2022 after an appeals court had overturned it, and his remaining challenges are narrow. Bowers was sentenced to death in 2023, and his appeals are still moving through the courts. None of the three is scheduled to be executed, and the memorandum does not set an execution date for any of them. What it does is instruct the Justice Department to move their cases forward as fast as the law allows, and to resist the delays that have, in the past, stretched the time between sentence and execution to a decade or more.

The memorandum also matters for the future, because it changes the calculus for every federal murder case that is charged from now on. Federal prosecutors who once might have sought life in prison for a child murder, or for a murder committed during a kidnapping, are now told by the president, in writing, to seek death. That instruction will not, by itself, produce a death sentence — a jury must still find the defendant guilty, and must still vote for death, and the defendant must still exhaust his appeals — but it changes the starting point of the decision, and it changes the message that the Justice Department sends to the courts, to the victims' families, and to the public. Over time, if the instruction is followed, the federal death row will grow again, and the three men who are on it now will be joined by others. The memorandum is, in that sense, a long-term project as much as a short-term order, and its full effect will not be visible for years.

There is a second group of condemned prisoners whose fate is tied to the memorandum, and they are on military death row rather than the federal civilian one. The most prominent is Nidal Hasan, the former Army major who murdered 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009 and who is scheduled to be executed by firing squad on December 3, 2026, after seeking a stay from the courts. Hasan's case is a military case, governed by the Uniform Code of Military Justice rather than by the federal civilian system, and the memorandum does not apply to it directly. But the atmosphere it creates — a president who has made the swift enforcement of death sentences a stated priority, and an administration that has restored the death penalty to the centre of its criminal justice policy — is the atmosphere in which Hasan's execution will take place, if the courts allow it to go ahead. The Fort Hood case is a reminder that the death penalty in America is not one system but several, and that the president's influence over each of them is different.

A history of stops and starts: 13 executions, then silence

The federal death penalty has a history of long pauses and sudden bursts, and the memorandum is the latest turn in that pattern. For 17 years, from 2003 to 2020, the federal government did not carry out a single execution, while the death penalty remained on the books and the federal death row grew. Then, in the final six months of Trump's first term, the Justice Department carried out 13 federal executions — more than in the previous 67 years combined — in a burst that began in July 2020 and ended in January 2021, days before Trump left office. The executions were carried out by lethal injection, using the drug pentobarbital, at the federal execution chamber in Terre Haute, Indiana, and they were carried out over the objections of lawyers, religious leaders and the families of some of the victims, who argued that the pace was too fast and that the procedures were flawed. The last of them, Dustin Higgs, was executed on January 16, 2021, four days before Biden took office, and the federal government has not carried out an execution since.

Biden's administration imposed a moratorium on federal executions in July 2021, and the commutations of December 2024 were the culmination of that policy: a president who had campaigned on ending the federal death penalty used his final weeks in office to empty the federal death row, with the exception of the three men whose crimes he regarded as beyond commutation. The moratorium was never lifted, and the execution chamber at Terre Haute has been silent for almost six years. The memorandum of November 23, 2026, is the formal end of that silence. It does not, by itself, lift the moratorium or set an execution date, but it instructs the Attorney General to take "all lawful and appropriate measures" to carry out federal death sentences as quickly as possible, and it is, in substance, a direction to resume executions. The question is not whether the administration intends to execute again, but when, and whom, and how quickly the courts will allow it to happen.

The history matters because it shows how much of the federal death penalty depends on the president. Congress has not changed the federal death penalty statute in any significant way for decades, and the Supreme Court has not ruled on the constitutionality of the federal death penalty itself. What has changed, again and again, is the policy of the administration in power: one president halts executions, the next resumes them, the next commutes the sentences, and the next orders them to be carried out faster. The death penalty at the federal level is, in practice, a matter of presidential will, and the memorandum is the clearest expression of that will since the burst of executions in 2020 and 2021. The families who spoke at the Justice Department event were, in effect, asking the president to use that will, and the president answered them in the affirmative, in writing, on the same day.

The drug at the centre of the federal execution protocol is pentobarbital, a barbiturate that the Justice Department adopted as its single-drug method in 2019, after years of litigation over the three-drug cocktail that had been used before. The use of pentobarbital was itself challenged in the courts, and the Supreme Court declined to block its use, but the challenges did not end with the executions of 2020 and 2021. Lawyers for the condemned men argued that the drug caused excruciating pain, that the procedures were rushed, and that the men who were executed had not been given a fair chance to challenge their sentences. The courts rejected most of those arguments, and the executions went ahead, but the arguments have not gone away, and they will be made again when the next federal execution is scheduled. The memorandum's instruction to oppose "dilatory litigation tactics" is, in part, an instruction to fight those arguments harder and faster than the Justice Department has fought them before.

What happens next — and what the courts will decide

The immediate question is what the Justice Department does first. The memorandum gives the Attorney General four tasks, and the most consequential of them is the first: to give priority to the prompt resolution of direct appeals, habeas corpus petitions and other post-conviction proceedings. That is the stage at which most federal death sentences spend most of their time, and it is the stage at which delays are longest. A federal death sentence is typically followed by years of appeals — direct appeals to the circuit courts, petitions for habeas corpus in the district courts, and petitions to the Supreme Court — and each of those stages can take a year or more. The memorandum instructs the Justice Department to ask the courts to move faster at every stage, and to oppose any request for delay that it regards as tactical rather than substantive. The courts are not bound by the memorandum, and they will decide for themselves how quickly to move, but the Justice Department's position in every case will now be, explicitly, that speed is a priority.

The second question is which new cases the Justice Department will bring under the expanded list. The three new categories — the murder of a child, murder during a sexual crime or sex trafficking, and murder during kidnapping or human trafficking — are all federal crimes in the first place only when they fall within federal jurisdiction, which means, in practice, that they must involve interstate commerce, federal property, or a victim or defendant who crosses state lines. Not every child murder is a federal case, and not every killing during a kidnapping is one either. The memorandum directs the Attorney General to "pursue federal jurisdiction" in these cases, which means that federal prosecutors will be looking for cases that can be brought in federal court, and that state prosecutors may find themselves competing with the federal government for the same cases. That competition is not new — federal and state prosecutors have always shared jurisdiction over many violent crimes — but the memorandum makes it explicit that the federal government wants these cases, and wants them in federal court, where the death penalty is available.

The third question is what the courts will do when the Justice Department asks them to move faster. The federal judiciary is independent, and the judges who hear death penalty cases are not bound by a presidential memorandum. They will decide, case by case, whether a request for expedition is justified, and whether a delay is tactical or legitimate. The history of the federal death penalty suggests that the courts will move faster in some cases and slower in others, and that the overall pace of executions will depend as much on the judges as on the Justice Department. The memorandum's instruction to "seek expedited judicial consideration wherever appropriate" is, in the end, a request, and the courts will answer it as they see fit. The administration can ask, but it cannot command the courts, and the courts have, in the past, been sceptical of requests to hurry a death penalty case.

The fourth question is political, and it is the one that will dominate the coverage. The memorandum was signed in the same month that Newsom commuted 548 death sentences, and it was signed at an event at which the president attacked the governor by name. The two men are now the public faces of the two sides of the death penalty debate, and the debate is likely to intensify as the 2028 presidential campaign approaches. Newsom's commutations were, in part, a statement of his values, and Trump's memorandum is, in part, a statement of his. The voters will be asked to choose between them, and the choice will be framed, by both sides, as a choice about justice, about victims, and about what kind of country America wants to be. The memorandum is, in that sense, not only a legal document but a campaign document, and it will be read as one by everyone who follows American politics.

The wider picture: a country divided on death

The federal death penalty is a small part of the American death penalty as a whole. Most executions in the United States are carried out by the states, and most of them are carried out in a handful of states — Texas, Florida, Oklahoma, Alabama and Georgia — that have continued to execute while others have paused or abolished the practice. Twenty-three states have abolished the death penalty, and several more have imposed moratoriums. California, with the largest death row in the country, has not executed anyone since 2006, and Newsom's commutations mean that it will not execute anyone for the foreseeable future. The federal government, by contrast, has executed 13 people in the last six years, and it is now preparing to execute again. The country is divided, and the division is not only between states but between the federal government and the states, and between one president and the next.

The public, too, is divided, and the division is complicated. Polls have consistently shown that a majority of Americans support the death penalty in principle, but that support falls when people are offered the alternative of life in prison without parole, and it falls further when people are asked about specific cases, or about the risk of executing an innocent person. The categories in the memorandum — the murder of a child, murder during a sexual crime, murder during a kidnapping — are the categories in which support is highest, and in which the alternative of life in prison is least attractive to the public. Trump's choice of categories is, in that sense, a political choice as well as a legal one: it picks the ground on which the death penalty is most popular, and it asks the public to judge the policy on that ground, rather than on the ground of the death penalty in general.

The risk of executing an innocent person is the argument that opponents of the death penalty return to most often, and it is the argument that the memorandum does not address. Since 1973, more than 200 people sentenced to death in the United States have been exonerated, according to the Death Penalty Information Center, and the number grows every year. The federal system is not immune to error, and the history of federal death penalty cases includes cases in which the evidence was later shown to be flawed, or in which the defendant's lawyer was later shown to be incompetent. The memorandum's emphasis on speed — on resolving appeals promptly, on opposing delay, on eliminating administrative delays — raises, for the opponents of the death penalty, the question of whether speed and accuracy can coexist, and whether a faster system is a fairer one, or a more dangerous one. The administration's answer is that the delays are themselves an injustice, and that the families of victims have waited long enough. The opponents' answer is that a mistake, once made, cannot be undone, and that the time spent on appeals is the price of getting it right.

For the families who spoke at the Justice Department event, the argument is simpler, and it is personal. They have waited for years — in some cases for decades — for a sentence to be carried out, and they have watched the condemned men file appeal after appeal, and they have watched the years pass. The memorandum is, for them, a promise that the waiting will end, and that the sentence will be carried out. Whether that promise is kept will depend on the courts, on the lawyers, and on the pace at which the Justice Department moves. But the promise has been made, in writing, by the president, and it is now a matter of public record. The families will hold the administration to it, and the opponents of the death penalty will hold the administration to the consequences of it, and the country will watch, as it has watched before, to see which side prevails.

The memorandum of November 23, 2026, is a short document, and its language is plain, but its meaning is large. It says that the federal government will seek death in more cases, and that it will carry out death sentences faster, and that it will do so because the president believes that justice delayed is justice denied. It says, too, that the president regards the commutation of 548 death sentences in California as an act of injustice, and that his administration will move in the opposite direction. The two positions cannot both be right, and the country will have to choose between them, in the courts, in the legislatures, and, eventually, at the ballot box. For now, the memorandum stands, the instruction has been given, and the federal death penalty — silent for almost six years — is moving again.
