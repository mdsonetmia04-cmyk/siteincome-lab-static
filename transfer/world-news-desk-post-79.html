TITLE: Israel Just Killed 9 in Gaza in a 3rd Night of Strikes — Hamas Deputy Education Minister and His Wife Dead in Deir al-Balah LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Israeli strikes killed nine people in Gaza overnight, including Hamas deputy education minister Khaled Abu Nada and his wife in Deir al-Balah ---BODY---

The skyline of Deir al-Balah in the central Gaza Strip, where an Israeli air strike killed Hamas deputy education minister Khaled Abu Nada and his wife in their apartment on Thursday, November 26, 2026. Photo: Public domain (CC0) via Wikimedia Commons.

Israeli air strikes and gunfire killed at least nine Palestinians across the Gaza Strip overnight, medics said on Thursday, November 26, 2026, including a senior Hamas official and his wife — in a third consecutive night of air attacks that has thrown the territory's fragile, year-old ceasefire into deepening doubt. The dead included Khaled Abu Nada, the Hamas-appointed deputy minister of education in Gaza, who was killed with his wife, Abeer, when an Israeli strike hit their apartment in the city of Deir al-Balah in the central Gaza Strip. It was, by the Israeli military's own account, a deliberate assassination — the third senior Hamas official killed in three days, and part of what Israel's leaders describe as a widening campaign of targeted killings against the group's senior figures.

The strike on Deir al-Balah came without warning. According to Al Jazeera's correspondent in the city, Ashraf Abu Amra, Israeli Apache helicopters fired two missiles directly and in succession at the residential apartment, during dawn prayers, in an area crowded with hundreds of tents sheltering displaced families. Medics at Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital in Deir al-Balah confirmed that Abu Nada and his wife were killed in the attack. Hamas confirmed the death, saying Abu Nada was the deputy minister of education and a member of the movement's political bureau — and calling the killing a crime against civilians that breached the ceasefire agreement and violated international law.

Israel's account of who Abu Nada was, and why he was targeted, is very different. The Israeli military said Abu Nada was the head of the economic department in Hamas's political bureau, and that as part of that role he was responsible for managing the group's banks and investment network, directing funds toward militant activities and the group's military buildup, "while residents of the Gaza Strip paid the price." Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Defence Minister Israel Katz confirmed the killing in a joint statement, saying it was "part of a series of targeted killings carried out recently against members and senior officials of the organization." Netanyahu added that Israel had killed about 80 Hamas militants last month alone.

The overnight death toll extended well beyond Deir al-Balah. Palestinian media reported that three people were killed in an Israeli strike in Gaza City's coastal Shati camp, and three others were killed in the city's Tuffah neighbourhood. Together with the two deaths in Deir al-Balah, that brought the overnight toll to at least nine, according to medics cited by Reuters — although Al Jazeera reported at least eight dead, a discrepancy that reflects the chaos and confusion of casualty reporting in Gaza, where figures are compiled under fire and revised as bodies are recovered and hospitals report in. What is not in dispute is that the attacks continued for a third straight night, despite a ceasefire that has been in place for more than a year.

A third senior official in three days

Abu Nada was the third senior Hamas official Israel has killed since Tuesday, November 24, and the pattern of the killings suggests a deliberate and accelerating campaign. On Tuesday, Israeli strikes killed the director of Hamas's internal security in the northern Gaza Strip, a man who had been appointed to the post only two months earlier. Within 24 hours, his likely replacement was also killed. On Wednesday, the military killed the northern Gaza security chief, along with his family. On Thursday, it was Abu Nada — a figure who, by Israel's account, sat at the intersection of Hamas's political, economic and educational structures.

The pace and the targeting are significant. These are not the broad, area-wide bombardments that characterised the heaviest phases of the war in Gaza. They are precise, individual killings — Apache helicopters firing missiles at a specific apartment, in a specific building, in a specific city, at dawn. The Israeli military has presented them as counter-terrorism operations against senior figures who continue to direct Hamas's activities. Hamas, and Palestinian officials, have presented them as assassinations of civilian government officials — a deputy education minister, a man whose public role was running schools — and as a systematic violation of the ceasefire.

The two narratives are irreconcilable, and they turn on a question that has haunted the Gaza ceasefire since it was agreed: who, exactly, counts as a legitimate target? Israel argues that Hamas's political and economic leadership is inseparable from its military wing, and that officials who manage the group's finances are directing its capacity to fight. Hamas argues that its civilian ministries — education, health, the economy — are part of the government of Gaza, and that killing their officials is an attack on the civilian administration, not on a military target. The killing of Abu Nada, a deputy education minister, sits squarely on that fault line, and it is likely to become a central exhibit in the argument over whether the ceasefire is still alive.

What is clear is that the campaign is expanding. Reporting from Deir al-Balah on Thursday, Al Jazeera's correspondent said that Israeli assassinations in Gaza are expanding, and that the killing of civilians marks a turning point in the nature of these assassinations. Abu Nada was killed, the correspondent noted, just one street away from where he was reporting — a reminder of how close the violence is to the journalists, medics and aid workers who remain in Gaza, and how little distance there is between a targeted killing and the crowded, tent-filled neighbourhoods where displaced families are sheltering.

The strike on Deir al-Balah

Deir al-Balah is a city in the central Gaza Strip, on the Mediterranean coast, and it has become one of the main hubs for displaced Palestinians during the war. Hundreds of thousands of people have fled there from other parts of the territory, and the city is now crowded with tents, makeshift shelters and families who have been displaced multiple times. The apartment block where Abu Nada was killed sits in the western part of the city, in an area that Al Jazeera's correspondent described as crowded with hundreds of tents sheltering displaced families.

The strike itself was, by the accounts available, swift and precise. Without prior warning, Israeli Apache helicopters fired two missiles directly and in succession at the residential apartment, according to Al Jazeera's reporting from the scene. The attack came during dawn prayers — a time when the city was waking up, and when the streets and the tents around the building would have been full of people beginning their day. Medics at Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital, the main hospital in Deir al-Balah, confirmed that Abu Nada and his wife were killed. Several others were reported injured in the strike, according to Palestinian sources, although the full casualty figures were still being compiled on Thursday morning.

The Israeli military confirmed the killing on Thursday, saying it had "eliminated the terrorist Khaled Abu Nada, who served as the head of the economic department in Hamas's Political Bureau" as well as education minister. The military's statement emphasised Abu Nada's economic role: he was, it said, responsible for managing Hamas's banks and investment network, and he directed funds toward what it called terror and Hamas's military buildup. The statement did not address the circumstances of the strike — the dawn timing, the absence of warning, the crowded neighbourhood — or the death of his wife.

Hamas confirmed the death in a statement of its own, saying Abu Nada was the deputy minister of education and a member of the movement's political bureau, without addressing the Israeli accusations against him. The group said Israel's "systematic assassinations" and targeting of residential buildings breached the October 2025 ceasefire agreement and violated international law. The statement was echoed in Hamas's response to the killing, which described the assassination of a Gaza official as "another Israeli crime against civilians."

A ceasefire under strain

The ceasefire that Abu Nada's killing is said to have breached has been in place for just over a year. The agreement, brokered by the United States, entered into force in Gaza in October 2025, bringing a halt — or at least a reduction — to the large-scale fighting that had devastated the territory over the preceding two years. It was, from the start, a fragile arrangement: a ceasefire rather than a peace agreement, with the underlying political questions — the future of Gaza, the future of Hamas, the future of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict — left unresolved.

Three consecutive nights of air strikes are a serious test of that arrangement. The attacks that killed Abu Nada were the third night in a row in which Israeli aircraft struck targets in Gaza, and they followed a pattern that has become familiar in recent weeks: targeted killings of senior Hamas figures, carried out with precision weapons, and justified by Israel as counter-terrorism operations against individuals who continue to direct the group's activities. For Hamas and for Palestinian officials, the strikes are a violation of the ceasefire, plain and simple — an agreement, they argue, that does not permit one side to continue killing the other's officials.

The Israeli government has not framed the killings as a breach of the ceasefire. Netanyahu and Katz, in confirming Abu Nada's death, described it as part of a series of targeted killings against members and senior officials of Hamas — language that suggests the campaign is deliberate, ongoing and sanctioned at the highest level. Netanyahu's claim that Israel killed about 80 Hamas militants last month reinforces that impression: this is not a one-off operation, but a sustained campaign that has been running for weeks.

That raises an obvious question: if the ceasefire is still in force, how can one side be killing 80 of the other's fighters and officials in a month? The answer, from Israel's perspective, appears to be that the ceasefire does not protect Hamas officials who are rebuilding the group's military capacity. From Hamas's perspective, the answer is that it does not — and that Israel's actions are a violation. The gap between those two positions is the space in which the ceasefire is being tested, and it is not clear how much more strain it can take.

The international response, so far, has been muted. The ceasefire was brokered by the United States, and its survival depends in large part on American pressure on both sides. Whether Washington will intervene to restrain the targeted killings, or whether it will accept them as consistent with the ceasefire, is an open question — and one that will shape the coming days.

Who was Khaled Abu Nada?

Khaled Abu Nada was, by all accounts, a senior figure in Hamas's Gaza administration, but the two sides describe his role in very different terms. According to Gaza authorities and Hamas, he was the deputy minister of education — the undersecretary of the Ministry of Education — and a member of Hamas's political bureau. His public role, in that account, was in education: overseeing schools in a territory where the education system has been devastated by war, where hundreds of thousands of children have been out of school for months, and where the rebuilding of classrooms is one of the most urgent humanitarian tasks.

According to the Israeli military, Abu Nada's more significant role was economic. He was, the military said, the head of the economic department in Hamas's political bureau, and as part of that role he was responsible for managing the group's banks and investment network. The military accused him of directing funds toward militant activities and force buildup — of using Hamas's financial infrastructure to sustain its capacity to fight, while ordinary Gazans paid the price.

The two accounts are not necessarily contradictory. It is entirely possible that Abu Nada held both roles — that he was, simultaneously, the deputy education minister and the head of Hamas's economic department. Hamas's government in Gaza has always been a hybrid structure, in which the same individuals often hold positions in both the civilian administration and the movement's political and military structures. That hybridity is, in part, what makes the targeting of its officials so contentious: the same person can be, at once, a deputy minister responsible for schools and a senior figure in a movement that Israel regards as a terrorist organisation.

What is not in doubt is that Abu Nada was a senior figure, and that his death — along with the deaths of the two senior security officials killed earlier in the week — represents a significant blow to Hamas's leadership in Gaza. The group has lost three senior officials in three days, including the director of its internal security apparatus and his replacement. That is a rate of attrition that would be difficult for any organisation to sustain, and it raises questions about how Hamas will respond — and whether it can respond at all, given the constraints of the ceasefire and the state of its forces after two years of war.

The wider toll: nine dead in one night

Abu Nada and his wife were not the only people killed overnight. In Gaza City, the territory's largest city, Palestinian media reported that three people were killed in an Israeli strike in the coastal Shati camp — a densely populated refugee camp on the Mediterranean shore, home to tens of thousands of people — and three others were killed in the Tuffah neighbourhood, in the eastern part of the city. The total overnight toll, according to medics, was at least nine.

The Shati camp and Tuffah are both areas that have been heavily affected by the war. Shati, one of the oldest refugee camps in Gaza, has been struck repeatedly over the past two years. Tuffah, a residential neighbourhood in eastern Gaza City, has also seen heavy fighting. The fact that strikes are continuing in both areas — a year after the ceasefire was supposed to have brought the fighting to an end — is a measure of how far the situation on the ground has drifted from the agreement on paper.

The casualty figures from Gaza are always subject to revision, and the discrepancy between the Reuters figure of nine dead and the Al Jazeera figure of eight is a reminder of how difficult it is to establish precise numbers in the immediate aftermath of an attack. Hospitals report in as bodies arrive, medics compile figures from multiple locations, and the toll can rise as the injured die or as bodies are recovered from rubble. What matters, for the purposes of understanding the night's events, is not the difference between eight and nine, but the fact that at least eight people were killed in a single night, in three separate locations, in a territory that is supposed to be at peace.

What happens next

The immediate question is whether the ceasefire can survive a third consecutive night of strikes and the killing of a third senior Hamas official in three days. The agreement has, so far, held in the sense that large-scale fighting has not resumed. But the targeted killings, and the accumulation of deaths they represent, are eroding the ceasefire from within. Each killing is a test of the other side's willingness to absorb the blow without responding — and each killing makes the next one more likely, as the side that has been hit looks for a way to restore deterrence.

Hamas's response, so far, has been rhetorical rather than military. The group has condemned the killings, accused Israel of breaching the ceasefire and violating international law, and called the assassinations crimes against civilians. It has not, as of Thursday morning, announced a military response. That restraint may reflect the group's weakened state after two years of war, or it may reflect a calculation that a military response would give Israel a pretext for a larger operation. Either way, the restraint is unlikely to hold indefinitely if the killings continue.

For Israel, the calculation is different. The targeted killings are, from the government's perspective, a way of degrading Hamas's leadership and preventing the group from rebuilding its military capacity — without resuming the full-scale war that the ceasefire was designed to prevent. The risk is that the campaign will be seen, by Hamas and by the international community, as a violation of the ceasefire, and that it will provoke the very escalation it is intended to prevent.

The role of the United States, as the broker of the ceasefire, will be decisive. Washington has, so far, said little about the targeted killings in public. But the survival of the agreement depends on American willingness to enforce it — to tell both sides, in private if not in public, that the killing must stop. Whether the Trump administration is willing to do that, and whether either side would listen, remains to be seen.

For the people of Gaza, the consequences are immediate and personal. Abu Nada's death is a political event, but for the families in Deir al-Balah, in Shati and in Tuffah, it is something else: another night of missiles, another morning of counting the dead, another reminder that a ceasefire on paper does not always mean safety on the ground. The tents are still there. The displaced families are still there. And the helicopters, it seems, are still flying.

The human cost behind the headlines

Behind the political arguments about the ceasefire and the competing accounts of who Abu Nada was, there is a simpler reality: the people of Gaza are living through another night of air strikes, a year after they were told the war was over. The apartment block in western Deir al-Balah where Abu Nada was killed sits in a neighbourhood crowded with hundreds of tents sheltering displaced families — people who have already fled their homes, in many cases more than once, and who are now living in temporary shelters in a city that has become one of the main hubs for the displaced. When the missiles struck at dawn, during prayers, those families were waking up to another day in tents, with no warning that the building next door was about to be hit.

Al-Aqsa Martyrs Hospital in Deir al-Balah, where medics confirmed the deaths of Abu Nada and his wife, is the main hospital serving the central Gaza Strip. It has been operating under extraordinary pressure for more than two years, treating the wounded from strikes across the territory with limited supplies, limited staff and intermittent power. The fact that the hospital is still functioning at all is a testament to the medics and staff who have kept it running. But every night of strikes adds to the burden — more wounded, more dead, more families arriving at the hospital gates looking for news of their loved ones.

The Shati camp, where three people were killed overnight, is one of the most densely populated places on earth. A refugee camp on the Mediterranean shore of Gaza City, it is home to tens of thousands of people living in crowded, concrete-block housing. A strike in Shati does not hit an empty field or a remote military installation — it hits a neighbourhood where families live cheek by jowl, where children play in narrow alleys and where there is nowhere to run. The same is true of Tuffah, the eastern Gaza City neighbourhood where three more people were killed. These are residential areas, and the people who died there were, by every account available, ordinary residents of a territory that has endured two years of war and a year of a ceasefire that has not brought them safety.

That is the context in which Abu Nada's killing must be understood. Whatever his role in Hamas — deputy education minister, head of the economic department, or both — he was killed in a residential building, in a crowded neighbourhood, at dawn, without warning, alongside his wife. And the eight or nine people who died with him overnight were killed in their homes and their neighbourhoods, in a territory where the ceasefire was supposed to have ended the killing. The political debate about whether the strikes breach the agreement will continue in capitals around the world. For the families in Deir al-Balah, Shati and Tuffah, the debate is academic. They are counting their dead.
