TITLE: Hackers Just Broke Into the FBI's National Crime Database — and the Bureau Won't Say What They Took LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The FBI is investigating a hack of its National Crime Information Center, the database behind 19.3 million records and 10.7 million police queries ---BODY---

FBI Director Kash Patel. Patel confirmed on November 24 that the investigation into the breach of the FBI's National Crime Information Center is ongoing, but declined to say who was responsible or what data was taken. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (public domain, FBI official portrait).

Hackers have broken into the database that sits at the centre of American policing — the National Crime Information Center, the system that tells a police officer at a traffic stop whether the driver is wanted, whether the car is stolen, and whether a protection order is in force — and the FBI will not say what they took, how they got in, or who did it. The bureau is investigating what it calls an apparent intrusion into the NCIC, a nationwide computerized database it maintains for federal, state and local law enforcement, and after five days of questions, the only thing the FBI has confirmed is that the investigation is still going on. CNN first reported the breach on Friday, November 20, and the Associated Press confirmed it the following day through a person familiar with the matter who was not authorised to speak by name. NBC News, citing two people briefed on the matter, reported the same day that the FBI was investigating an apparent breach of the system. The scope of the problem was not immediately clear, and it still is not. No group has publicly claimed responsibility. No official accounting of what was accessed, altered or stolen has been released. And the bureau's director, Kash Patel, when pressed at a Justice Department press conference on Tuesday, November 24, would say only this: "The matter is ongoing."

That silence is what makes this story so unsettling. The NCIC is not a peripheral system. It is the shared index that American law enforcement runs a name, a licence plate or a gun purchase against — a database of warrants, fugitives, missing persons, criminal histories, protection orders and stolen property that is queried millions of times a day by officers in every state. According to the FBI's own review of fiscal year 2025, the NCIC held more than 19.3 million records — an all-time high — and handled an average of 10,729,678 queries a day, with an average response time of 0.0062 seconds and availability of 99.8 per cent. Agencies searched the system by name 2,674,486,640 times in 2025 alone, more than 7 million name searches a day. In that same year, agencies entered 498,038 missing person records into the NCIC, and 323,767 of them were children under 18. At the end of 2025, 88,093 missing person records were still open, and beside them sat 8,509 records of people whose names are unknown — 8,253 of them dead. The system serves more than 90,000 criminal justice and law enforcement agencies, and on any given day some 700,000 officers have access to it. When a database of that size and that importance is breached, the first question is what was stolen. But the former FBI official who spoke to CNN raised a worse one: whether the attackers could have changed what is in it.

The NCIC was launched on January 27, 1967, with five files and 356,784 records, and it has been growing ever since. The first transaction it ever processed came in 1967, when a New York City police officer on patrol radioed a request to run the plates of a suspicious parked car and learned in 90 seconds that the car had been stolen in Boston a month earlier — a search that had to comb through 95,000 records held in 15 state and city computers linked to the FBI. Within four years, the system had reached all 50 states. A major technological upgrade in 1999 brought near-instant results, and by 2003 the index had been modified to include files from the FBI's Terrorist Screening Center. Today the database consists of 22 files, and it is housed at the FBI's Criminal Justice Information Services Division facility in Clarksburg, West Virginia, which is also home to the bureau's fingerprint database and the National Instant Criminal Background Check System used for potential gun buyers. The system operates under what the Justice Department calls a shared management concept: the FBI serves as the custodian of the records and maintains the operational availability of the system, while the records themselves come from the FBI, other federal agencies, state and local law enforcement, and authorised courts. It is available around the clock, every day of the year, and it answers in fractions of a second — which is precisely why officers trust it without thinking, and precisely why a compromise of its contents would be so dangerous.

What the FBI has said — and what it has refused to say

The public record of the breach is, so far, a study in what has not been said. CNN reported the investigation on Friday, November 20, citing people briefed on the matter. The Associated Press confirmed it the next day through a person familiar with the matter. NBC News reported it the same day, citing two people briefed on the matter. The FBI itself declined to comment on Saturday, November 21, and has issued no public statement since. President Donald Trump addressed the reports during a telephone interview with Fox News on Saturday, saying: "We just heard about it, and I will say that it's not good." The Director of National Intelligence, Jay Clayton, acknowledged that the FBI had "an incident" and was handling it "in the ordinary course", but said he could not discuss specifics or identify those responsible. And on Tuesday, November 24, at a Justice Department press conference that was primarily aimed at highlighting progress against scammers and cybercrime since President Trump signed an executive order in March, Director Patel was pressed about the breach and limited his remarks to status updates and a commitment to transparency where legally permitted. "We've publicly released, as we always do, information we are permitted to at this time, but it's an ongoing investigation," Patel said, adding that the FBI is "working with the interagency, and as we've done in the past, we will make all the information public either through our partners at the Department of Justice or through our engagements on Capitol Hill." Beyond those lines, he declined to provide details about attribution or the scope of any data exposure.

The bureau's refusal to say whether any records were accessed leaves unanswered the most basic questions about the incident. The NCIC contains warrants, fugitives, missing persons and background-check data, and individuals whose records are maintained in the system have a direct stake in whether any such files were exposed — but the bureau has not confirmed any such exposures. There is no public confirmation that records were exfiltrated, altered or made unavailable, and there is no public evidence linking the NCIC case to any other cyber event. Officials have not linked the breach to the separate intrusion into the unclassified open-source intelligence system used by the CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence, which was discovered around the same time, and they have not linked it to the other federal incidents reported in the same week. The FBI has not disclosed the scope of the breach, and no group has publicly claimed responsibility. Patel's remarks at the press conference did not address the NCIC at all until he was asked about it directly, and his earlier public remarks in the days before the report became public — promoting an initiative called Operation Winter Shield and a round of system updates — made no mention of the intrusion.

What is known is that the investigation is active, that it involves other agencies, and that the bureau intends to disclose its findings through the Justice Department or to Congress when the investigation permits. What is not known is who was behind the breach, what NCIC records — if any — were accessed, when the intrusion began, how it was detected, and whether anything in the database was changed. Until forensic findings are released, the scale of the compromise, the data impact and the responsible actor remain unconfirmed. The central fact of this episode is stark and simple: an active investigation is under way into an intrusion into the NCIC, and the FBI, through its director, is withholding specifics while it works with other agencies. The bureau has promised to release information it is permitted to disclose, but until those disclosures arrive, the key questions remain open, and every day that passes without answers is a day in which officers across the country are querying a system whose integrity has been called into question.

The real danger is not theft — it is trust

The most consequential risk in this breach may not be what was stolen, but what may have been changed. A former FBI official told CNN that the concern was not only access to sensitive information, but whether attackers could compromise the accuracy of records on which officers rely — and the most consequential risk may concern data integrity rather than theft alone. The distinction matters, because the NCIC's value is not confidentiality. Most of what is in it is meant to be shared with 90,000 agencies. Its value is integrity. NCIC records inform decisions about whether a person is wanted, whether a vehicle or a weapon is stolen, and whether a protection order exists. At 10.7 million queries a day, a single corrupted record does not sit quietly in a database. It gets served to an officer at a traffic stop, a border checkpoint, a gun counter. A warrant that has been deleted could let a fugitive walk away from a stop. A warrant that has been added could put an innocent person in handcuffs. A protection order that has been erased could leave a victim unprotected when she needs the system most. A stolen-gun record that has been removed could let a prohibited buyer pass a background check. The database is a shared operational dependency, not merely a repository of sensitive data, and a compromise of its trustworthiness would be worse than a theft of its contents, because theft can be discovered and disclosed, while a quiet alteration can go on doing damage for as long as it goes unnoticed.

The history of the system shows how deeply it is woven into the daily work of policing. In its first year of operation, the NCIC processed approximately 2.4 million transactions, an average of 5,479 a day. By 2005 it was processing 1.6 billion transactions a year, and by 2009 it was handling almost 2.5 billion a year, with a single-day record of more than 7.9 million transactions. By the end of 2009, the NCIC contained more than 15 million active records in 19 files. By 2023, it held 22 files and more than 18 million active records, and its 50th-anniversary account put the load at an average of 14 million transactions a day. The fiscal year 2025 figures — more than 19.3 million records, 10.7 million queries a day — are the latest in a line that has risen almost without interruption for nearly six decades. One in three transactions is performed by a component of the Department of Homeland Security. Officers query it from squad cars, from squad rooms, and increasingly from handheld devices. It is, in the FBI's own words from its 40th-anniversary account, one of the unsung heroes of crime-fighting — little known to the public, but used every day by law enforcement officers nationwide to take terrorists and criminals off the street, find missing persons and property, and save lives. That is what is at stake when its integrity is in doubt, and it is why the former official's warning — that the concern is whether the attackers could compromise the accuracy of the records — is the most important sentence yet spoken about this breach.

There is also the question of who is in the database, and what exposure would mean for them. The NCIC stores names, including variations, fingerprints, mugshots, crime records, and parole and probation information on terrorists, national and international fugitives, convicted sex offenders, prisoners, missing and unidentified persons, violent gang members and immigration violators, and it holds seven categories of stolen property listings, with photographs. It holds the records of missing children, of unidentified dead, of people under protection orders, of victims of identity theft. The bureau has not confirmed that any of those records were exposed, and there is no public evidence that they were. But the public and the people referenced in NCIC records have a direct stake in the answer, and the bureau's silence leaves them with nothing but the promise that information will be released when the investigation allows. For the families of the 88,093 people whose missing person records were still open at the end of 2025, and for the 8,509 unidentified dead whose records sit in the system, the question of whether the database that holds their cases has been compromised is not an abstraction. It is personal, and it deserves an answer.

A week of breaches across the federal government

The NCIC intrusion did not happen in isolation. It arrived in the middle of a week in which federal systems seemed to be failing, one after another, and in which the Trump administration's explanations for those failures were themselves coming under scrutiny. News that the NCIC was targeted came as the country encountered a series of digital outages in the government and private sector that Trump administration officials said were caused by routine efforts to upgrade computer systems and fix vulnerabilities, rather than by cyberattacks — and there was no immediate indication that the FBI incident was related to those outages. But the list of incidents kept growing. The breach followed an apparent cyberattack at the US Department of Agriculture, where employees were informed on Thursday that one of its systems had been targeted, and officials with knowledge of the matter said a system the agency uses to pay farmers had been hit. An internal classified computer network used by the National Security Agency endured a multi-day outage after it first went down on Tuesday due to a technical problem, according to two people with knowledge of the matter; as of Friday, the outage had been resolved. And on November 25, the Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network said it was investigating a cybersecurity incident that resulted in "unauthorized access to a limited subset of its systems" and had taken some systems offline as a precaution, while its BSA E-Filing website remained operational, and the FBI and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency were working to determine the scope.

The CIA and the Office of the Director of National Intelligence discovered a separate breach of an unclassified open-source intelligence analysis network, and multiple sources said the intrusion happened the same day as the NCIC attack, according to reports. The separate intrusion into the unclassified system also hit the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency, and early indications suggested the attack may have originated in China, according to reports, with investigators reportedly suspecting that the attackers used Artex, a publicly released AI penetration testing tool developed by a Chinese engineer — although no attribution has been established. An ODNI official said there had been "no disruptions to critical capabilities or classified systems". Officials have not linked that breach to the NCIC intrusion, and the FBI still has not disclosed who was behind the NCIC attack or what data was accessed. FinCEN is the latest federal agency to disclose an intrusion in the past week, following the NCIC and CIA incidents, but officials have not linked the three. Amid the rising concerns about the outages, a White House official confirmed to NBC News on Thursday that White House National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross was leaving his role — a departure that came at the very moment the government's cyber defences were under the most scrutiny they have faced in years.

The pattern is what alarms the people who watch these things for a living. The NCIC matter is the bureau's second reported breach in recent months, following a September intrusion in which the hacking group ShinyHunters claimed it had stolen data on agents through the agency's job portal, and a March disclosure of suspicious activity involving an internal system containing surveillance and investigative information. The case should not be conflated with other cyber events affecting US institutions, and no public evidence links the NCIC case to the previous incidents. But the accumulation — a recruitment portal in September, a surveillance system in March, the national crime database in November, the CIA's unclassified network the same day, the Agriculture Department's payment system, the Treasury's financial crimes network, a multi-day outage at the NSA — is difficult to dismiss as coincidence, and it raises questions about continuity in investigations and disclosure practices that the FBI has so far declined to answer. The bureau's general posture, as Patel framed it on Tuesday, is that it has released what it is permitted to release, and that more will follow through the Justice Department or Congress. Whether that is enough, when the system in question is queried 10.7 million times a day, is a question that Congress is likely to ask, and soon.

This has happened before — and the last time, Congress was told

The NCIC has been a target before. On April 5, 2001, the director of the FBI's National Infrastructure Protection Center testified before a committee of the US House of Representatives about intrusions into government computer networks, in hearings that examined how vulnerable federal systems were to attack. The "Phone Masters" case of the late 1990s, in which the ringleaders were sentenced in 1999, showed that even in the dial-up era, the systems that held law enforcement data could be reached by determined intruders. The difference between then and now is scale. In 1967, the NCIC held 356,784 records. In 2007, it held more than 9.5 million active records and was processing 5.7 million transactions a day. Today it holds more than 19.3 million records and handles 10.7 million queries a day. The system has grown by a factor of more than fifty since it was launched, and the number of agencies that depend on it has grown with it. What was once a clearinghouse that a New York officer could use to check a suspicious car has become the backbone of American law enforcement — and the backbone is now the target.

The history of the NCIC is also a history of how completely American policing has come to depend on instant, trusted data. When the system was launched, a query took 90 seconds. By 2006, the average response time was 0.06 seconds. By fiscal year 2025, it was 0.0062 seconds. The system that once searched 95,000 records in 15 linked computers now searches more than 19 million records in a fraction of a blink. That speed is what makes the NCIC indispensable, and it is also what makes a compromise of its data so dangerous: there is no time, in 0.0062 seconds, for an officer to second-guess the answer. The system is trusted because it has earned trust over 59 years of continuous operation, and the breach of November 2026 is the first time in that history that the trust itself has been publicly called into question on this scale. The FBI's task now is not only to find out who did it and what they took, but to prove — to 90,000 agencies, to 700,000 officers, and to the public — that the answers the system gives can still be believed.

What happens next

The investigation is now in the hands of the FBI and the interagency partners Patel referred to on Tuesday, and the bureau has said it will make information public through the Justice Department or through its engagements on Capitol Hill. That means the next disclosures are likely to come in one of two forms: a public statement from the Justice Department, or a briefing to the congressional committees that oversee the FBI. Both routes take time, and both are shaped by the fact that the investigation is ongoing and that disclosing details could compromise it. But the pressure for answers is building, and it is building for a reason. Five House Republicans asked the Secretary of Homeland Security in June for the detailed deportation count behind the administration's record claims, and are still waiting for an answer. Lawmakers have already raised the alarm about the Federal Aviation Administration's decision to deploy an aviation AI tool nationally. Congress has shown, in recent weeks, that it is willing to press the administration on the systems the public depends on — and the NCIC is a system the public depends on more directly than almost any other.

For now, the officers who query the NCIC 10.7 million times a day are working with a system whose custodian has told them, in effect, that something happened and that they cannot yet be told what. The FBI has not said whether the system was taken offline, whether any records were quarantined, whether any queries were affected, or whether any agency has been warned to double-check results. The bureau has not said when the intrusion was detected, or how long it may have been under way before it was found. It has not said whether the attackers are believed to be a criminal group, a foreign intelligence service, or something else entirely. And it has not said when the public will know. What it has said is that the matter is ongoing, that it is working with other agencies, and that it will make all the information public that it is permitted to make public. That is a promise, and it is also a postponement. The 19.3 million records in the NCIC include the names of the missing, the wanted and the dead, and the people who love them, the officers who look for them and the public that depends on the system are all waiting for the same thing: an answer to the question of what happened to the database that holds them, and whether it can still be trusted.

The breach of the National Crime Information Center is, on the facts available today, an intrusion of unknown scope, unknown attribution and unknown consequence — and that is precisely what makes it so serious. A database that answers 10.7 million questions a day cannot afford to have its answers doubted, and a bureau that will not say what happened to it cannot expect the doubt to go away on its own. The FBI has promised disclosure through the Justice Department and Congress. Until that disclosure comes, the NCIC will keep answering, 10.7 million times a day, in 0.0062 seconds — and every one of those answers will carry a question that was not there a week ago: can this still be believed?
