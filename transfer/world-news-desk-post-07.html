TITLE: Russia Could Launch a Nuclear-Armed Satellite Within Weeks, Western Intelligence Warns — NATO Vows to Defend Space LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Western intelligence believes Russia's Sputnik-S satellite, designed to carry a nuclear weapon, could launch within 60 days. NATO chief Mark Rutte vowed to defend space. ---BODY---

Mark Rutte, Secretary General of NATO, at the 2025 NATO Summit in The Hague. Photo: Christopher Sheats / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)

Russia is preparing to place a nuclear weapon in Earth's orbit before the end of 2026, according to Western intelligence assessments cited in a German media investigation published on November 23 — a claim that has alarmed Germany and NATO and raised the prospect of a new and dangerous chapter in the arms race in space.

The investigation, by the German newspaper Süddeutsche Zeitung and public broadcasters NDR and WDR, reported that Western intelligence agencies believe Russia has assembled a satellite known as "Sputnik-S", designed to carry a nuclear warhead, which has been stored in the Moscow region for several years and could be launched within 60 days. Germany's military Space Command reportedly assessed that a December launch was possible.

The findings, which were said to have been shared with several NATO countries in recent days, prompted an immediate response from European leaders. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz described the reports as shocking, and NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte vowed that the alliance would defend against threats in space. The Kremlin dismissed the allegations, and Russia's embassy in Berlin called them groundless.

If the reports are accurate, the launch would mark the first time a nuclear weapon has been placed in orbit — a step that would breach the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, threaten the thousands of satellites on which modern economies depend and open a new front in the confrontation between Russia and the West.

What the intelligence reportedly says

According to the German media investigation, Western intelligence agencies believe the Sputnik-S satellite was completed several years ago and has since remained in storage in the Moscow region. Intelligence warnings shared among several NATO countries in early November indicated that the satellite, reportedly designed to carry a nuclear warhead, could be launched within 60 days.

Bundeswehr experts had briefed senior German government officials about the alleged plans in recent weeks, including Defence Minister Boris Pistorius, according to the reports. The German military's Space Command assessed that a launch in December was possible, though the reports did not say whether a specific date had been identified.

The investigation's findings rest on unnamed sources in Western intelligence agencies and the German military, and they have not been independently confirmed. The outlets that published them — Süddeutsche Zeitung, NDR and WDR — are among Germany's most respected news organisations, and the reports were taken seriously enough by the German government and NATO to draw public responses from the chancellor and the alliance's secretary general.

The reports follow an earlier assessment by the United States Defense Intelligence Agency, which warned in a report released in October that Russia is "developing a satellite designed to carry a nuclear weapon". That assessment came about two years after The Washington Post reported that the US intelligence community had concluded Russia had developed a nuclear weapon capable of being deployed in space. The latest reports suggest Moscow may now be moving closer to putting such a capability into orbit.

Merz and Rutte respond

Merz addressed the reports during a joint press conference with Rutte in Berlin on Monday. "The reports that Russia plans to deploy nuclear weapons in space are not new, but they are no less shocking. It shows the extent to which Russia is apparently prepared to escalate the situation," the chancellor said. He did not confirm or deny the media reports, but his language left no doubt about the seriousness with which Berlin regards them.

Rutte, asked about the reports while visiting Berlin, said NATO was continuously assessing its deterrence and defence capabilities across land, sea, air and space. "I can assure you, we will always make sure that we defend every inch of NATO territory," he said, adding: "We will always make sure that we defend every inch of NATO territory, and that includes making sure that we are safe when it comes to the space element." He added that the alliance had response options that were not always visible to the public.

A German government spokesman said the reports were being taken "very seriously", though he did not confirm them either and warned against causing "excessive nervousness regarding hypothetical dangers". The careful wording reflected the uncertainty surrounding the intelligence: the government regards the threat as credible enough to take seriously, but not so certain that it can be stated as fact.

Rutte had already voiced concern about Russia's nuclear plans in space in April 2025. "We are aware of reports that Russia is exploring the possibility of deploying nuclear weapons in space," he told the Sunday edition of the German newspaper Die Welt at the time. The latest reports, if accurate, would mean that exploration has moved from possibility to preparation.

Why a nuclear weapon in space matters

A nuclear weapon in orbit would be a threat of a different order from any weapon deployed on Earth. Its purpose, according to the assessments, would not be to strike targets on the ground, but to destroy or disable satellites — the thousands of spacecraft that provide communications, navigation, weather forecasting, financial transactions and military surveillance for the entire world.

A nuclear detonation in space would release an electromagnetic pulse and a burst of radiation that could damage or destroy satellites across a wide area, including those belonging to countries that had nothing to do with the conflict. Unlike a missile strike on a single satellite, which affects one spacecraft, a nuclear blast in orbit could cripple entire constellations — and the debris and radiation it created could render parts of orbit unusable for years.

That is why the head of the European Space Agency, Josef Aschbacher, described the presence of nuclear weapons in space as "the ultimate nightmare scenario" in comments to the Handelsblatt newspaper. It is also why the German Foreign Office stated that the deployment of a nuclear weapon in space would constitute "a flagrant breach of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty", the agreement that has kept weapons of mass destruction out of orbit for nearly sixty years.

The threat is not abstract. Modern economies depend on satellites to a degree that is easy to overlook until they fail: GPS navigation, mobile phone networks, banking systems, weather prediction, disaster response and military communications all rely on spacecraft that would be vulnerable to a nuclear weapon in orbit. A single detonation could disrupt services used by billions of people, with consequences that would be felt far beyond the countries directly involved in any conflict.

The Kremlin's denial

Russia has rejected the allegations. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said he had not seen the latest news stories but that frequent reports citing "unknown sources" are "hardly worth taking seriously", and questioned the credibility of intelligence reports that contained numerous unsubstantiated claims. Russia's embassy in Berlin separately dismissed the reporting as part of a propaganda campaign, maintaining that Moscow's space activities were peaceful and consistent with international obligations.

The denials follow a familiar pattern: Russia has consistently denied developing space-based nuclear weapons, even as Western intelligence agencies have grown more confident that it is doing so. The denials do not, by themselves, settle the question — but neither do the intelligence reports, which rest on sources that have not been named and assessments that have not been published in full.

What is known is that Russia has been developing anti-satellite capabilities for years, including ground-based missiles, electronic warfare systems and spacecraft capable of approaching and interfering with other satellites. The question is whether it has taken the further step of building a satellite designed to carry a nuclear warhead — and, if so, whether it intends to launch it.

The reports also come at a sensitive diplomatic moment. They emerged just four days after President Donald Trump met with Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping at a high-profile summit in Shenzhen, China, where nuclear arms control was a central topic. The three leaders issued a joint declaration affirming that nuclear war should never be fought, but stopped short of announcing binding arms-control commitments. The suggestion that Russia may be preparing to put a nuclear weapon in orbit so soon after that declaration — if true — would cast a shadow over the summit's outcome.

The wider arms race in space

The Sputnik-S reports do not exist in isolation. They come weeks after US Air Force Secretary Troy Meink announced that the United States had deployed a weapon in space, according to the German media investigation — a reminder that the militarisation of orbit is not a one-sided process, and that several countries are developing capabilities that would have been unthinkable a generation ago.

The United States, China and Russia have all invested heavily in space-based military systems, and the line between defensive and offensive capabilities in orbit has always been blurred. A satellite that can approach another satellite to inspect it can also approach it to disable it. A system designed to protect one's own spacecraft can look, to an adversary, like a system designed to attack theirs.

What makes the reported Sputnik-S different is the nuclear element. Conventional anti-satellite weapons, however dangerous, are limited in their effects: they destroy one satellite at a time. A nuclear weapon in orbit would be indiscriminate, threatening every satellite within range regardless of its owner or purpose. That is why the Outer Space Treaty banned nuclear weapons in orbit in 1967, and why the treaty's signatories — including Russia — have observed that ban for nearly six decades.

Breaking the ban would not only be a legal violation; it would be a strategic watershed. Once one country places a nuclear weapon in orbit, others will feel compelled to follow, and the result would be an orbital arms race in which the weapons themselves make the domain they occupy unusable. The "nightmare scenario" described by the head of the European Space Agency is not only the detonation of such a weapon, but the race to deploy it.

What happens next

Several developments will show whether the warnings about Sputnik-S are borne out. The first is the simplest: whether a launch takes place. Western intelligence agencies are monitoring the Moscow region and Russia's launch sites, and any movement of the satellite or preparation for a launch would be detected. A launch before the end of the year, as the reports suggest is possible, would confirm the assessments and trigger an immediate international crisis.

The second is the diplomatic response. NATO has already signalled that it is taking the reports seriously, and the alliance's members will be discussing their options — including whether to raise the issue at the United Nations, whether to impose further sanctions on Russia's space programme and whether to accelerate their own defensive capabilities in orbit. The German government's warning against "excessive nervousness" suggests a desire to respond firmly without fuelling panic, but the balance between firmness and calm will be difficult to maintain if the threat materialises.

The third is the arms-control picture. The Shenzhen summit's failure to produce binding commitments on nuclear arms control already left the field open, and a Russian orbital launch would make the need for new agreements more urgent — and harder to achieve. The Outer Space Treaty remains in force, but a violation of its central prohibition would raise questions about whether the treaty framework can survive the current era of great-power competition.

And the fourth is the reaction of the wider world. A nuclear weapon in orbit threatens every country that uses satellites — which is to say, every country. China, which participated in the Shenzhen summit and shares Russia's interest in constraining American power, would also have its own satellites at risk. Whether Beijing's attitude to the reports becomes clear in the coming weeks will be an important indicator of how the story develops.

The Outer Space Treaty and sixty years of keeping nukes out of orbit

The agreement that a Russian orbital launch would breach is one of the oldest and most successful arms-control treaties in history. The Outer Space Treaty of 1967, signed by the United States, the Soviet Union and the United Kingdom at the height of the Cold War, established the basic rules for the use of space. Its most important provision is simple: states may not place nuclear weapons or any other weapons of mass destruction in orbit around the Earth, install them on the Moon or any other celestial body, or station them in outer space in any other manner.

The treaty was a product of a particular moment. In the 1960s, both superpowers had considered placing nuclear weapons in space, and both had concluded that doing so would be destabilising — not least because a weapon in orbit would threaten the attacker's own satellites as surely as the enemy's. The treaty codified that mutual restraint, and for nearly sixty years it has held. No country has placed a nuclear weapon in orbit, and the norm against doing so has become one of the most widely observed rules in international law.

That norm has been tested before. In 2007, China destroyed one of its own satellites with a ground-launched missile, creating a cloud of debris that still threatens spacecraft in orbit today. In 2019, India conducted a similar test. In 2021, Russia destroyed a defunct satellite, Cosmos 1408, in a test that created more than 1,500 trackable pieces of debris and drew condemnation from the United States and its allies. Each of these tests demonstrated anti-satellite capability without placing a weapon in orbit — and each was criticised precisely because of the debris it created.

What the reported Sputnik-S would represent is different in kind, not merely in degree. A ground-based missile destroys one satellite; a nuclear weapon in orbit could destroy many, and could render entire orbital regions unusable. The distinction is why the treaty bans nuclear weapons in space specifically, and why a violation would be regarded as a breach of a different order from the anti-satellite tests of recent years.

The treaty has more than a hundred parties, including Russia, and its central prohibition has never been formally challenged — until now. If Russia were to launch Sputnik-S, it would not only violate a treaty it signed; it would break a norm that has held since before the first human walked on the Moon, and it would do so in a domain where the consequences of a breach are felt by every country on Earth.

How satellites became essential — and vulnerable

The stakes of a nuclear weapon in orbit are so high because satellites have become essential to almost every aspect of modern life. There are now more than ten thousand active satellites in orbit, the majority of them launched in the past few years as commercial constellations — led by SpaceX's Starlink — have transformed the economics of space. They provide the services that the modern world runs on.

Navigation is the most familiar: the GPS system, and its Russian, European and Chinese counterparts, guide everything from aircraft and ships to delivery vans and smartphones. Communications satellites carry phone calls, television broadcasts and internet traffic to places that terrestrial networks cannot reach. Weather satellites track storms and predict their paths, saving lives. Earth-observation satellites monitor crops, forests, ice caps and oceans. And financial systems depend on satellite timing signals to synchronise transactions worth trillions of dollars every day.

Militaries depend on satellites even more heavily. Surveillance satellites watch for missile launches and troop movements. Communications satellites link commanders to forces in the field. Navigation satellites guide precision weapons. The war in Ukraine has demonstrated this dependence vividly: Starlink terminals have provided Ukrainian forces with communications that Russian attacks on terrestrial networks could not disrupt, and satellite imagery has shaped the course of battles.

That dependence is also a vulnerability. Satellites are fragile, difficult to defend and impossible to replace quickly. A nuclear detonation in orbit would not need to target any particular satellite: the radiation and electromagnetic pulse it produced would damage spacecraft across a wide region, and the debris from destroyed satellites would threaten others in a cascade known as the Kessler syndrome — a chain reaction of collisions that could render an orbital band unusable for years or decades.

The irony is that the country launching such a weapon would suffer alongside its adversaries. Russia's own satellites — its navigation system, its communications network, its surveillance capabilities — would be at risk from the same blast. That is one reason many analysts doubt that Moscow would take such a step: the weapon would be as dangerous to its owner as to its enemies. But the intelligence assessments cited in the German reports suggest that Russia may have concluded otherwise — or that it values the threat itself, the leverage that comes from possessing such a capability, more than the risks of using it.

Verification, if a launch is attempted, will be almost immediate. Russia's launch sites are watched constantly by American and allied satellites, and any rocket carrying a payload the size of Sputnik-S would be detected within minutes of liftoff. Amateur satellite trackers, who monitor the skies with telescopes and radio receivers, would identify the new object within hours, and its orbit would be published openly. The world would know — and the question would shift from whether Russia has built such a weapon to what the world intends to do about it. That transparency is, in a sense, the last line of defence for the Outer Space Treaty: a violation cannot be hidden, and the violator cannot escape the judgement of the international community. Whether that judgement is enough to deter the launch in the first place is the question that will hang over the coming weeks.

For now, the Sputnik-S reports remain what they are: intelligence assessments cited by respected German news organisations, taken seriously by the German government and NATO, and denied by the Kremlin. They may prove to be accurate, or they may prove to be overstated. What is certain is that the question they raise — whether the world is about to see the first nuclear weapon in orbit — is one of the most consequential of the year, and that the answer will shape the security of space for a generation.
