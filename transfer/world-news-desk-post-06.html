TITLE: Trump and Pashinyan Launch the TRIPP Corridor — a US-Backed Route Linking Azerbaijan to Nakhchivan Through Armenia LABELS: World, Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Armenia launched the US-backed TRIPP corridor on Monday, a 43-km route linking Azerbaijan to Nakhchivan through Syunik, despite Iran's warnings. ---BODY---

Donald Trump, President of the United States. Photo: Official White House Photo by Daniel Torok / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)

Armenia has launched the Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity — known as TRIPP — a United States-backed transport and energy corridor that will connect mainland Azerbaijan with its Nakhchivan exclave through southern Armenia, in the most significant step yet toward a lasting peace between two countries that have been in conflict for decades.

Armenian Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan and US President Donald Trump announced the launch in separate video messages on Monday, November 23, 2026, confirming that preparatory construction work had begun in Armenia's southern Syunik province. Pashinyan described the project as the largest economic undertaking in his country's history since independence, and Trump announced the creation of the TRIPP Development Company to oversee it.

The roughly 43-kilometre route — about 26 miles — will run through the Meghri area of Syunik, near Armenia's border with Iran, carrying a railway, a highway, electricity transmission lines and fibre-optic cables. It will link mainland Azerbaijan to Nakhchivan, an Azerbaijani exclave separated from the rest of the country by Armenian territory, and provide an onward connection to Türkiye — creating a new trade artery between Central Asia and Europe.

The launch took place despite a warning from Iran, whose northern border runs alongside the corridor. Ali Akbar Velayati, a senior foreign policy adviser to Iranian Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, had accused Armenia of disregarding Tehran's security concerns over the proposed route, and Iranian officials warned that Tehran would oppose any development it believes could threaten its security or regional influence.

What TRIPP is

TRIPP — the Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity — is the infrastructure centrepiece of a US-brokered peace initiative between Armenia and Azerbaijan. The project emerged from a joint declaration signed by Pashinyan and Azerbaijani President Ilham Aliyev at the White House on August 8, 2025, in Trump's presence, which was intended to advance peace, normalise relations and reopen regional transportation links after decades of conflict over Nagorno-Karabakh.

Nagorno-Karabakh is an internationally recognised part of Azerbaijan that was controlled for years by ethnic Armenian separatists before Azerbaijani forces retook the territory in 2023. The war and its aftermath displaced tens of thousands of people and left the two countries technically at war, with closed borders and no diplomatic relations. The 2025 declaration was the first serious attempt to turn the page.

Under the framework agreed since then, the TRIPP Development Company will oversee the corridor's construction and operation. A US-controlled entity will hold a 74 percent stake in the company, with Armenia retaining 26 percent, under an implementation framework announced by US Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Armenian Foreign Minister Ararat Mirzoyan in January. The company will receive development rights for the corridor, giving Washington a central and long-term role in the region's infrastructure.

Armenia's parliament ratified the framework agreement on strategic cooperation concerning TRIPP on October 28, by 63 votes to 32. President Vahagn Khachaturyan signed the ratification law on November 12. In November, Armenia, the United States and Azerbaijan announced the launch of trilateral technical planning groups for the initiative — the bureaucratic machinery that turns a political agreement into concrete, steel and copper.

The launch

Pashinyan confirmed the launch during a public discussion at the DigiTec 2026 technology conference in Yerevan, where he appeared alongside Rev Lebaredian, a vice-president of Nvidia, to discuss Armenia's opportunities in artificial intelligence. "Tomorrow we will officially announce the launch of the TRIPP program, and energy and electricity transit will be an important component of it," he said.

He initially said the launch would take place on November 22, then corrected himself hours later, saying the launch event would be held on November 23. The correction was a small detail, but it illustrated the pace at which the project is moving — and the political sensitivity of getting the announcement right.

In his video message on Monday, Pashinyan said preparatory construction work had begun in Syunik, describing the initiative as his country's largest economic project since independence. "We are launching work on the largest economic project in Armenia since independence," he said. Syunik Governor Robert Ghukasyan confirmed that work had begun at several locations in the Meghri area, adding that major construction would follow the preparatory phase.

Trump, in his own video message, congratulated Pashinyan on the project's launch and praised his efforts toward peace with Azerbaijan. He announced the launch of the TRIPP Development Company, saying the US-Armenia partnership would establish new trade routes for goods, energy and travel across the region. He thanked both Pashinyan and Aliyev for their cooperation and invited the Armenian premier to visit the White House next year.

The energy dimension

Energy is a central part of TRIPP, and in some ways the most immediately consequential. Pashinyan said Armenia would connect its electricity grid to those of Türkiye and Azerbaijan as part of the project. "Energy and electricity transit are a very important part of this project. We will connect our grid to the electricity grids of Türkiye and Azerbaijan," he said. Armenia is already connected to Georgia and Iran in this respect, and the government is investing to make electricity transmission more efficient.

The energy links matter because they turn a transport corridor into something larger: an integrated regional network in which electricity, like goods, can flow across borders that were closed for a generation. For Armenia, a small, landlocked country whose economy has long been constrained by its closed borders with Türkiye and Azerbaijan, the ability to trade electricity with both neighbours is a significant opportunity. For the region, it is a step toward the kind of interdependence that makes a return to war more costly for everyone.

The corridor's broader economic logic is similar. By linking Central Asia to Europe through the South Caucasus, TRIPP strengthens the so-called Middle Corridor — the trade route that runs from China through Central Asia, across the Caspian Sea and through the Caucasus to Türkiye and Europe. That route has gained importance as an alternative to shipping through Russia, and TRIPP's 43 kilometres through Armenia fill one of its last missing links.

Azerbaijan, for its part, has already been carrying out infrastructure work within its own territory, expanding transport capabilities in East Zangezur and Nakhchivan. The construction of the Horadiz–Aghband highway was completed in October 2026. The commencement of work on the Armenian section indicates that the process has now reached a practical stage on both sides of the border.

Iran's warning

The corridor runs along Iran's northern border, and Tehran has made no secret of its opposition. Velayati, the senior adviser to Supreme Leader Khamenei, accused Armenia of disregarding Tehran's security concerns, and Iranian officials have warned against any development they believe could threaten Iran's security or regional influence.

Iran's concerns are partly about the corridor itself — a US-backed route on its border, in which an American-controlled company holds a majority stake — and partly about the wider context. The TRIPP launch comes amid a US-Israeli war with Iran, a US naval blockade and an American military effort, Task Force Iron Gate, focused on Iran's borders. From Tehran's perspective, the corridor is not merely a road and a railway; it is an extension of American power to its northern frontier.

Armenia has pressed ahead despite the warnings, calculating that the economic and political benefits of the project outweigh the risks of Iranian displeasure. That calculation reflects a broader shift in Yerevan's foreign policy: a turn toward the West, and toward the United States in particular, that has accelerated since the loss of Nagorno-Karabakh and the failure of Russia — Armenia's traditional security guarantor — to prevent it.

The United States, for its part, has been explicit about the strategic value it attaches to the corridor. Beyond the trade and energy benefits, TRIPP gives Washington a physical and institutional presence in the South Caucasus — a region where Russia has long been the dominant outside power and where China has been expanding its influence. A 74 percent American stake in the company that runs the corridor is, in that sense, as much a geopolitical statement as a commercial arrangement.

What it means for the region

The launch of TRIPP matters for the South Caucasus in several ways. The first is the most obvious: it is the largest concrete step yet toward peace between Armenia and Azerbaijan. The 2025 joint declaration was a promise; the corridor is the first major piece of that promise being built. If it is completed, it will create a physical link between two countries that have been enemies for a generation, and a daily, practical reason for them to keep talking.

The second is economic. For Armenia, the corridor is — in Pashinyan's words — the largest economic project since independence, with the potential to transform Syunik, one of the country's poorest regions, and to give the whole economy access to markets that have been closed to it. For Azerbaijan, it provides the long-sought direct link to Nakhchivan and onward to Türkiye, strengthening its position as a transit hub between Central Asia and Europe. For the region as a whole, it adds capacity to a trade route whose importance is growing.

The third is geopolitical. TRIPP formalises an American role in the South Caucasus that would have been unthinkable a decade ago. Russia, which once guaranteed Armenia's security and dominated the region's politics, has been marginalised by its war in Ukraine and by its failure to protect its Armenian ally. The United States has moved into the space Russia vacated, and the corridor — with its American majority-owned operating company — is the clearest expression of that shift.

And the fourth is the message it sends to Iran. A US-backed corridor on its northern border, built over its objections, is a demonstration that Tehran's ability to veto developments in its neighbourhood has limits. Whether that demonstration leads to escalation or to accommodation will depend on how Iran chooses to respond — and on whether the United States and Armenia can manage the relationship with Tehran while the corridor is built.

What happens next

Several things will determine whether TRIPP fulfils its promise. The first is the construction itself. Preparatory work has begun, but the major construction — the railway, the highway, the power lines — is still ahead, and large infrastructure projects in difficult terrain have a habit of running late and over budget. The TRIPP Development Company's ability to deliver will be tested in the coming years.

The second is the political relationship between Armenia and Azerbaijan. The corridor depends on a peace that is still fragile: the two countries have signed a declaration, but they have not yet signed a formal peace treaty, and the issues that divided them — borders, prisoners, the fate of displaced people — are not fully resolved. If the political process stalls, the corridor could stall with it.

The third is Iran. Tehran's warnings have so far been rhetorical, but the corridor's progress may test whether they remain so. Armenia will need to manage its relationship with its southern neighbour carefully, and the United States will need to decide how much friction with Iran it is willing to accept for the sake of the project.

And the fourth is the wider region. TRIPP's success depends on the Middle Corridor working as a whole — on Central Asian states, on Caspian shipping, on Turkish connections to Europe. The corridor is one link in a chain, and its value depends on the strength of the other links.

The South Caucasus: a region remade

To understand why a 43-kilometre corridor through southern Armenia matters so much, it helps to understand the region it runs through. The South Caucasus — Armenia, Azerbaijan and Georgia — sits at the crossroads of Europe and Asia, Russia and the Middle East, and its history is one of empires, wars and shifting borders. For most of the past two centuries, Russia was the dominant outside power, and the region's politics were shaped by Moscow's interests.

The conflict between Armenia and Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh defined the region for a generation. The territory, internationally recognised as part of Azerbaijan, was controlled by ethnic Armenian separatists from the early 1990s, after a war that killed tens of thousands of people and displaced more than a million. A ceasefire held, uneasily, for more than two decades, but the underlying dispute was never resolved.

In 2020, Azerbaijan launched a war that retook much of the territory, and in 2023 its forces retook the remainder, prompting the exodus of almost the entire ethnic Armenian population of Nagorno-Karabakh. The defeat was a trauma for Armenia and a turning point for the region. Russia, which had a peacekeeping force in the area and a defence alliance with Armenia, did not intervene to prevent the loss — a failure that shattered Yerevan's faith in Moscow as a security guarantor and set Armenia on its current path toward the West.

Since then, Armenia has moved steadily away from Russia's orbit: suspending its membership of the Russian-led Collective Security Treaty Organisation, seeking closer ties with the European Union and the United States, and opening its economy to Western investment. TRIPP is the most concrete expression of that pivot — an American-backed project, run by an American-majority company, on Armenian soil, linking Armenia's economy to its neighbours and to the wider world.

For Azerbaijan, the corridor completes a long-standing strategic goal: a direct land link to Nakhchivan, the exclave that has been cut off from the rest of the country since the early 1990s, and through it to Türkiye, Azerbaijan's closest ally. President Aliyev has pursued that goal for years, through diplomacy and through war. TRIPP delivers it through infrastructure — a road and a railway that make the connection permanent and profitable.

The Middle Corridor and the new geography of trade

TRIPP's significance extends well beyond the South Caucasus because of the trade route it completes. The Middle Corridor runs from China through Central Asia, across the Caspian Sea by ferry, through Azerbaijan and Georgia, and onward through Türkiye to Europe. It is the overland alternative to the northern route through Russia and to the sea route through the Suez Canal, and its importance has grown sharply in recent years.

The northern route, which carries a large share of China-Europe rail freight, runs through Russian territory — a vulnerability that became acute after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, when Western sanctions and the war itself disrupted traffic and raised the political cost of relying on Moscow. The sea route, through the Suez Canal, is longer and has been disrupted by attacks on shipping in the Red Sea. The Middle Corridor, by contrast, avoids both Russia and the Suez — but until now it had a gap: the missing link through southern Armenia between Azerbaijan and Türkiye.

TRIPP fills that gap. With the corridor in place, goods can travel from Central Asia to Europe without passing through Russia or the Suez Canal, on a route that is shorter than the sea journey and more secure than the northern railway. For China, which is the largest user of Eurasian rail freight, that matters. For the European Union, which has been seeking to diversify its supply chains away from both Russia and China, it matters too. And for the Central Asian states — Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan — it offers a new outlet to world markets that does not depend on Moscow.

The corridor also fits into a wider pattern of infrastructure competition. China has its Belt and Road Initiative, the vast programme of ports, railways and roads that has reshaped Eurasian trade. The European Union has its Global Gateway programme, designed to offer an alternative to Chinese investment. The United States, through TRIPP, is now staking its own claim in the region's infrastructure — not with a grand continental programme, but with a single, strategically placed corridor that gives Washington a stake in the trade route's future.

Whether the Middle Corridor can live up to its promise depends on more than TRIPP. The Caspian Sea crossing remains a bottleneck, with limited ferry capacity. The railways of Central Asia and the Caucasus need investment. And the route's competitiveness depends on the tariffs and transit fees charged by each country along the way. But the corridor's completion removes the most politically sensitive obstacle, and its backers argue that the rest — the ferries, the railways, the logistics — will follow the demand.

Georgia, the third country of the South Caucasus, will also be watching closely. The Middle Corridor runs through Georgian territory on its way to the Black Sea, and Tbilisi has invested heavily in its ports and railways to capture the transit trade. A successful TRIPP strengthens Georgia's position too, by increasing the volume of goods flowing through the corridor — but it also changes the region's balance, giving Armenia and Azerbaijan a direct link that bypasses Georgian territory for some routes. The corridor's name itself carries history: the route through Syunik was long known as the Zangezur corridor, a name that recalls the region's Armenian name and the disputes over who controls it. Renaming it TRIPP — after an American president — was itself a political act, one that signals whose order the new corridor belongs to. For the people of Syunik, the abstract politics will become concrete soon enough: construction jobs, new roads, power lines and, eventually, trains carrying goods between countries that were enemies within living memory.

For now, though, the launch is a moment of genuine significance: a road and a railway through a 43-kilometre strip of southern Armenia, built by an American-Armenian company, linking two former enemies and connecting Central Asia to Europe. It is, as Pashinyan said, the largest economic project in Armenia's history since independence. Whether it becomes the foundation of a lasting peace, or merely another unfulfilled promise in a region that has seen many, will be decided in the years ahead.
