TITLE: EU Leaders Just Walked Out After 12 Hours — No Budget, No €45 Billion for Ukraine, and Only 3 Weeks Left LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: After 12+ hours in Brussels, EU leaders left without a 2028–2034 budget deal or a final €45bn Ukraine loan. The next chance is December 17–18. ---BODY---

European Council President António Costa chaired the extraordinary EU summit in Brussels, where leaders spent more than 12 hours failing to agree on the bloc's next seven-year budget and a €45 billion loan for Ukraine. Photo: European Union, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

They came to Brussels to settle the two biggest financial questions facing Europe. They left, more than 12 hours later, having settled neither. On Thursday, 26 November 2026, the leaders of the European Union's 27 member states ended an extraordinary summit a day early — cancelling the planned second day entirely — without an agreement on the bloc's next seven-year budget, and without a final decision on a €45 billion loan to keep Ukraine's government running through 2027.

The failure was not a surprise to anyone in the room. It was, by several accounts, visible before the summit even began. Italy's Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni told reporters as she arrived that the leaders were "light-years away from a solution" and that Italy was "not willing to sign off on a budget under which we would have to give more to receive less". Hungary's Viktor Orbán posted on Facebook during the talks that "hearing the completely opposing positions in the room, the chance of an agreement today is virtually zero, but a Christmas agreement does not seem likely either". He was right about the first part. The second part — whether a deal can be reached by Christmas — will now be tested in a frantic three weeks, with the next European Council meeting set for 17–18 December.

What is at stake is not abstract. The budget under discussion — the Multiannual Financial Framework for 2028 to 2034 — is the plan for how the European Union spends its money for seven years: on farming, on poorer regions, on defence, on competitiveness, on Ukraine. And the loan for Ukraine is not a distant promise. Kyiv faces an estimated €70 billion financing gap in 2027, and without the money, the Ukrainian state could struggle to pay soldiers, pensions and public services while it fights a war. The leaders in Brussels knew all of this. They still could not agree.

A €1.78 trillion budget nobody can agree on

The numbers involved are large enough to be hard to grasp. The European Commission's original proposal for the 2028–2034 budget was close to €2 trillion. European Council President António Costa, who chaired the summit, put forward a compromise proposal that cut €204 billion from the Commission's plan, bringing the total to €1.78 trillion — about $1.95 trillion. Al Jazeera, reporting from the summit, put the figure under discussion at nearly $1.76 trillion. Even at the reduced level, the package is small compared with national budgets — it is financed mostly by the 27 member countries based on the size of their economies — but it requires unanimous approval from all member states, plus the consent of the European Parliament. One veto kills it.

The disagreements centre on competing spending priorities, and they run in both directions. Some countries want increased allocations for defence, industrial competitiveness and economic development. Others want to preserve existing funding for agriculture and regional development, the traditional policies that consume large parts of the budget. Some leaders called for belts to be tightened; others defended the old spending lines. The Commission's proposed budget seeks to redirect spending towards strategic priorities, including defence and strengthening Europe's global competitiveness, but that redirection means cuts somewhere else — and every cut has a country behind it.

The negotiations also involve new sources of revenue, including proposed levies on large companies, tobacco taxation and increased revenues from the bloc's emissions trading system. Each of those is a fight of its own. A tax on large companies divides the countries that host them from the countries that do not. Tobacco taxation divides the producers from the rest. And the emissions trading system, which puts a price on carbon, is already one of the most politically sensitive instruments in the European Union.

One group of countries made its position clear before the talks even began. Several members of the so-called "frugal" camp — Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden — decided to boycott the meeting format proposed by Costa, according to Euronews. The boycott was a signal: the frugals, who pay more into the budget than they take out, were not prepared to be presented with a compromise they had not shaped. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, the most powerful of the frugals, went further. He threatened to block a budget deal unless all member states backed the separate €45 billion loan to Kyiv for 2027 — and then, late on Thursday, he announced that the leaders would not reconvene on Friday morning and would seek to reach an agreement in December instead.

Costa, for his part, called on the leaders to approach the debate with "an open mind, a constructive approach and the sense of compromise". He said an agreement was needed "by the end of this year and for this we need to reach unanimity". After the summit ended, he described December as a crucial stage in the negotiations and noted that a final agreement must be reached by the end of the year so that the budget can take effect on 1 January 2028. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen indicated that considerable work remained before a final agreement could be reached.

Ukraine: 24 countries said yes — and it still was not enough

The Ukraine question was, in some ways, the more painful failure, because the leaders were so close. Twenty-four of the 27 member states supported the European Commission's proposal to provide Ukraine with an additional €45 billion loan in 2027, worth about $49 billion. The loan would be divided into two components: up to €25 billion for defence, including arms and ammunition, and up to €20 billion to support the state budget. Bulgaria said it would participate only in the non-military portion of the aid, which is focused on regions where the Bulgarian national minority resides. But there was no agreement on how to finance the loan — in particular, on who should cover the borrowing costs — and no final decision was taken. The leaders will return to the issue in December.

To be precise about what happened: the support of 24 countries was political backing for the proposal, not money already transferred. The next step is for the EU Council to finalise the mechanism. The lack of agreement on the 2028–2034 budget does not in itself mean the loan will be cancelled, since the loan is being discussed as a separate proposal — but its final terms still need to be agreed, and the disagreement over financing is real.

The arithmetic of Ukraine's needs explains the urgency. The country faces an estimated €70 billion financing gap in 2027, after accounting for the €90 billion EU loan already agreed for 2026 and 2027. The Commission proposes that the EU lend a further €45 billion, leaving Kyiv to find roughly €25 billion from other partners. The €45 billion would cover approximately two-thirds of the gap. Without it, the gap is the whole €70 billion — and the search for the remaining €25 billion becomes a search for the full amount.

The longer budget matters too. The draft seven-year budget sets aside up to €100 billion for Ukraine over seven years, alongside about €740 billion for agriculture and poorer regions. Nearly a year ago, EU leaders endorsed a loan of €90 billion to cover most of the Ukrainian government's needs for two years. The new loan would come on top of that. And the budget puzzle is not only about Ukraine: the leaders must also decide whether the costs of servicing the new loan should be drawn from the budget, which would reduce the money available for everything else.

Merz's ultimatum — no budget without the Ukraine loan — deepened the divisions, because it linked the two questions in a way that made each harder to solve alone. The frugal countries, which want a smaller budget, were being asked to approve a large new loan at the same time. The countries that want the loan most were being asked to accept a budget they did not like. In the end, neither side moved far enough.

Macron's deadline — and the elections hanging over everything

French President Emmanuel Macron set an end-of-year deadline for an agreement after the leaders ruled out any chance of a deal on Thursday. Macron, whose country is mired in debt and faces elections in April that the hard right appears likely to win, said he would be ready to sign up to "a good agreement" when the leaders meet again on 17–18 December. His position captures the tension at the heart of the talks: France wants a deal, but it cannot afford a bad one, and it cannot afford to be seen giving in either.

The elections are not a distant concern. Leaders want a deal by year-end because there are elections next year in France, Italy, Poland, Greece, Estonia, Finland and Slovakia that could complicate negotiations. A new government in any of those countries could reopen questions that took months to settle. In France, the presidential favourite has identified 2028 — the first year of the next EU budget — as the point at which policy would change. That makes the December meeting not just the next chance, but possibly the last good chance for a while.

There are two regular European Council meetings scheduled before France elects its next president in the spring: 17–18 December and 18–19 March. Costa now has three weeks to produce a compromise. Merz remains hopeful about the budget's eventual approval, according to the Kyiv Post, but hope is not a plan — and the three weeks will be filled with the same arguments that filled the last twelve months.

The summit also took place against a grim backdrop from the war itself. On the night before the talks, Russia launched 155 drones at Ukraine, including 83 jet-powered Shahed drones; Ukrainian air defences shot down 110, but strikes hit 27 locations and one man was killed in Zaporizhzhia. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy warned that Russia was preparing a new mass strike in the coming days. And in a separate development, the Trump administration has ended its strategy of using energy and aviation deals to persuade Moscow to end the war, moving instead to a traditional land-for-peace framework — a shift that puts more weight on Europe, and on the money Europe can provide, than ever before.

Why this budget fight is different from the last ones

The European Union has argued about its budget before — every seven-year cycle produces a version of the same fight — but this one is different in three ways. First, the world has changed since the last budget was agreed. The current framework was negotiated before Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, before the return of Donald Trump to the White House, and before defence became the central question of European politics. The new budget is being asked to do things the old one never contemplated: to fund a major rearmament, to support a country at war, and to make Europe less dependent on the United States for its security. That is a different job, and it costs more money.

Second, the money really is tighter. The Commission's proposal seeks to redirect spending towards strategic priorities, but the member states that pay the most are also the ones facing the tightest public finances. Germany, the largest net contributor, is under pressure at home. France, the second-largest economy in the bloc, is mired in debt. The countries that want more defence spending are, in many cases, the same countries that want lower contributions. The arithmetic does not resolve itself, and no amount of creative accounting changes the fact that the budget must be paid for by someone.

Third, the politics have hardened. The frugal camp — Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden — has been a feature of EU budget talks for years, but the boycott of Costa's meeting format at this summit was a new escalation: not a disagreement about numbers, but a refusal to accept the process itself. On the other side, the countries that depend on agricultural and regional funding see the Commission's redirection towards defence and competitiveness as a threat to the spending that sustains their rural economies and poorer regions. The draft budget sets aside about €740 billion for agriculture and poorer regions — a huge sum — but it is less, in relative terms, than those countries are used to, and they are not prepared to give it up quietly.

There is also a structural problem that no summit can solve in a day. The EU budget requires unanimity — all 27 governments must agree — and the consent of at least 361 of the European Parliament's 720 members. That means a deal must survive two separate negotiations, in two different institutions, with two different sets of politics. A compromise that satisfies the frugals in the Council may not satisfy the Parliament, and a compromise that satisfies the Parliament may not survive the Council. The December meeting will have to thread that needle, and the thread is getting thinner.

It is worth remembering what the budget actually pays for, because the numbers can make it sound abstract. The Common Agricultural Policy supports farmers across the continent. The cohesion funds build roads, railways and broadband in the EU's poorer regions. The defence lines, still modest by the standards of national military budgets, are meant to fund joint procurement and the industrial base that makes it possible. The money for Ukraine — up to €100 billion over seven years in the draft — would keep a state functioning while it fights a war on Europe's border. None of these are luxuries. All of them have constituencies that will fight for them. That is why the talks take so long, and why they fail as often as they succeed.

What the failure costs — in money, in time, and in credibility

The most immediate cost is time. The budget only starts in 2028, so there is no cliff on 1 January — but the negotiation was supposed to be finished, or nearly finished, by now, and it is not. Every month spent arguing about the size of the budget is a month not spent on the programmes the budget is meant to fund. Defence procurement, industrial policy, agricultural support, regional development — all of it waits on a framework that does not yet exist. And the longer the talks drag on, the more they become entangled with national elections, which is exactly what the leaders wanted to avoid.

The cost for Ukraine is more direct. The €45 billion loan is meant to cover most of Kyiv's financing gap in 2027. Until it is finalised, Ukraine cannot plan with certainty. Its government must budget for a war without knowing whether its largest financial backer will deliver the money it needs. The €90 billion already agreed for 2026–2027 helps, but it was agreed nearly a year ago, and the war has not waited. The gap for 2027 — €70 billion — is the figure that matters now, and €45 billion of it was supposed to be settled in Brussels on Thursday. It was not.

There is also a cost in credibility, and it is the one the leaders may feel most keenly. The European Union has spent the last four years presenting itself as Ukraine's most reliable partner — the one that would be there when others wavered. A summit that ends a day early, without a deal on the money, does not match that presentation. It is true that 24 countries backed the loan, and true that the budget talks will resume in December. But it is also true that the leaders flew to Brussels, talked for more than 12 hours, and left with nothing signed. In Kyiv, that will be noticed. In Moscow, it will be noticed more.

The divisions on display in Brussels are not new, but they are getting harder to bridge. The frugal countries want a smaller budget and tighter conditions. The countries that receive more than they pay want the traditional spending protected. The countries that border Russia want defence spending increased. France wants a deal but cannot pay for one it does not like. Germany wants the Ukraine loan approved but will not approve a budget it considers too large. Each position is defensible on its own. Together, they add up to a stalemate — and a stalemate that requires unanimity to break.

There is one more factor, and it is the one nobody in Brussels wants to discuss openly: the money is finite, and the demands on it keep growing. The budget must cover farming, regions, defence, competitiveness, climate, migration, and Ukraine — and now, potentially, the servicing costs of the loans to Ukraine as well. Costa's proposal cut €204 billion from the Commission's plan, and that was still too much for some and too little for others. The December talks will have to find a number that 27 governments and 720 members of the European Parliament can all accept. The arithmetic is unforgiving, and the politics are worse.

What to watch in December

The next European Council meeting, on 17–18 December, is now the most important date on the European calendar. Costa has three weeks to produce a compromise, and he will spend them shuttling between capitals, testing numbers, and trying to find the combination that eluded him in Brussels. The questions he must answer are the same ones that defeated the November summit: how big should the budget be, who pays for it, how much goes to Ukraine, and who covers the cost of borrowing the money for Kyiv.

Watch for three things. First, whether Merz maintains his ultimatum — no budget without the Ukraine loan — or softens it, and whether the frugals hold together or split. Second, whether Macron, facing elections in April, moves towards a deal or holds out for terms he can defend at home. Third, whether the loan for Ukraine is separated from the budget fight and approved on its own, which would at least settle Kyiv's 2027 financing while the seven-year argument continues.

There is also the question of what happens if December fails too. The budget does not start until 2028, so there is no immediate legal crisis — but a failure in December would push the negotiation into an election year in seven countries, which is exactly the scenario the leaders were trying to avoid. Orbán's prediction — that a Christmas agreement does not seem likely either — would start to look less like pessimism and more like a forecast.

For Ukraine, the stakes are simpler and starker. The country needs €70 billion in 2027. The European Union has promised €90 billion for 2026–2027 and is discussing €45 billion more. The gap between what is promised and what is needed is the space in which a government fights a war. On Thursday, in Brussels, the leaders of Europe had a chance to close that gap. They talked for more than 12 hours. Then they went home.
