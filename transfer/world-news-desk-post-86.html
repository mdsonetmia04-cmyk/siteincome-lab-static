The numbers keep climbing, and the warnings keep getting darker. The Ebola outbreak in the Democratic Republic of the Congo has now passed 12,000 confirmed cases since it was declared in mid-May 2026, with more than 5,500 people dead and a fatality rate hovering close to 50 percent. The World Health Organization has warned that the outbreak — already the second deadliest in history — could spread beyond the region if it is not contained, and the United States has imposed new travel restrictions on people arriving from the affected country.

The latest figures, reported on Thursday, November 26, 2026, paint a picture of an epidemic that is not being brought under control but is instead gathering speed. The outbreak, caused by the rare Bundibugyo strain of the Ebola virus, has infected more than 12,000 people in the DRC and has spread to neighbouring Kenya and Uganda, where two deaths have been recorded in each country. The death toll in the DRC alone has passed 5,500, and the case fatality rate — the proportion of confirmed cases that end in death — has climbed above 50 percent, according to the country's National Institute of Public Health.

To put those numbers in context: the current outbreak is the DRC's 17th Ebola outbreak, and its largest. It is the second deadliest Ebola outbreak in history, surpassed only by the West Africa epidemic of 2014 to 2016, which killed more than 11,000 people over two years. But the current outbreak is spreading at twice the rate of that epidemic, according to reports citing health officials, which means that if it is not contained, it could overtake the West Africa toll in a fraction of the time.

The WHO has declared the outbreak a public health emergency of international concern — the highest level of alarm the organisation can sound. Dr Mohamed Janabi, the WHO's Regional Director for Africa, has warned that a global spread of Ebola is "possible" if the outbreak is not contained. And the United States has acted on that warning: since November 3, 2026, all people boarding an international flight to the United States have been required to attest that they have not been present in the DRC, a measure designed to keep the virus from reaching American soil.

A virus with no vaccine

What makes this outbreak so dangerous is not only its scale but the nature of the virus itself. The Bundibugyo strain — named after the district in Uganda where it was first identified — is one of the rarest and least understood forms of Ebola. Unlike the Zaire strain, which caused the West Africa epidemic and for which vaccines were developed and deployed, there is no approved vaccine for the Bundibugyo strain. That single fact changes everything about the response.

In the West Africa epidemic, and in the DRC's own earlier outbreaks, vaccination was the central tool of containment. Health workers used a strategy called ring vaccination: when a case was confirmed, everyone who had been in contact with the patient — and everyone who had been in contact with those contacts — was vaccinated, creating a ring of immunity around the outbreak. It was laborious, dangerous work, carried out in remote villages and conflict zones, but it worked. The vaccine turned the tide.

With the Bundibugyo strain, that tool does not exist. There is no approved vaccine, and while experimental vaccines are in development, they are not available for mass deployment. That means the response must rely on the older, harder tools of epidemic control: isolating the sick, tracing their contacts, monitoring those contacts for symptoms, ensuring safe burials, and persuading frightened communities to cooperate with health workers they may not trust. All of those things are harder in the eastern DRC than almost anywhere else on earth.

Dr Catherine Smallwood, the WHO's Ebola incident manager, has said that staff shortages are a real problem, and that the violence in the region is making the response harder. The outbreak is concentrated in Ituri and North Kivu provinces, in the east of the country, where government forces have been fighting rebel groups for years. Health workers operate in an environment where armed groups control territory, where roads are unsafe, where clinics are attacked, and where the population has good reason to distrust outsiders in uniforms. In that environment, the basic work of epidemic control — finding the sick, tracing their contacts, ensuring safe burials — becomes a matter of negotiation, courage and luck.

The situation has been made worse by a strike. Health workers in North Kivu have been on strike since November 12, 2026, according to reports from the region, in a dispute that has removed some of the most experienced responders from the front line at precisely the moment they are needed most. The reasons for the strike — pay, conditions, safety — are the same reasons that health workers strike everywhere. But in the middle of an Ebola outbreak, the consequences are measured in lives.

The toll, in numbers

The official figures tell the story of an epidemic that has grown steadily worse over six months. When the outbreak was declared in mid-May 2026, it was the DRC's 17th, but there was no indication that it would become the largest. By the end of October, the picture had changed dramatically. The National Institute of Public Health reported on October 31 that the country had recorded 10,414 confirmed infections and 5,372 deaths, giving a case fatality rate of 51.6 percent — up from 48.6 percent just two days earlier.

The jump in the fatality rate was not the result of a sudden surge in deaths, but of a retrospective adjustment. A review of records across 97 percent of the affected health zones in Ituri, the epidemic's epicentre, resulted in 314 earlier Ebola deaths being added to the province's cumulative toll. The adjustment pushed the reported fatality ratio up sharply, even as the number of confirmed infections rose by only 140. It was a reminder that the official figures, terrible as they are, may understate the true scale of the outbreak — and that the real toll, including deaths that were never recorded, may be higher still.

By early November, the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention reported that the outbreak had killed 5,376 people: 5,372 in the DRC and two each in Kenya and Uganda. The Bundibugyo strain had infected some 10,444 people across the three countries, with a death rate of more than 50 percent. By November 20, the Guardian reported that the outbreak had recorded more than 11,000 confirmed cases and nearly 5,400 reported deaths, with children accounting for more than a third of the deaths. And by November 26, the reported case count had passed 12,000, with the death toll above 5,500.

The trajectory is unmistakable: the numbers are rising, and they are rising fast. The outbreak is spreading at twice the rate of the West Africa epidemic, and it has already lasted six months. The WHO's declaration of a public health emergency of international concern reflects the judgement that this is not a local crisis but a global threat — one that requires a coordinated international response, and one that may not receive it in time.

Children are dying in disproportionate numbers

Among the most distressing aspects of the outbreak is its impact on children. According to the Africa CDC and the WHO, children account for more than a third of the deaths recorded so far. That is an unusually high proportion for an Ebola outbreak, and it reflects the particular vulnerability of children in the affected region — to malnutrition, to other diseases, to the disruption of health services caused by the conflict, and to the virus itself.

Children who contract Ebola face a higher risk of death than adults, in part because their immune systems are less developed and in part because they are less likely to receive prompt treatment. In the eastern DRC, where health facilities are sparse and often inaccessible, a sick child may not reach a treatment centre for days, if at all. By the time they arrive, they may be too ill to save. The loss of a child to Ebola is also a particular blow to a family and a community, and it deepens the mistrust that makes the response harder: parents who have watched their children die in a treatment centre may be less willing to send another child there.

The impact on children extends beyond those who contract the virus. Thousands of children have been orphaned by the outbreak, losing one or both parents to a disease that kills half of those it infects. Many of them are now in the care of relatives, or of neighbours, or of no one. The social fabric of the affected communities — already strained by years of conflict — is being torn further by the epidemic, and the consequences will last long after the outbreak itself is over.

A region at war, a system under strain

The eastern DRC is one of the most difficult places in the world to fight an epidemic. Ituri and North Kivu, the two provinces at the centre of the outbreak, have been the scene of armed conflict for decades. Government forces, rebel groups and local militias compete for control of territory, mines and trade routes, and the civilian population is caught in the middle. Health workers, aid workers and journalists operate in an environment where kidnapping, ambush and attack are constant risks.

The conflict affects the Ebola response in almost every way. It makes it harder for health workers to reach affected communities, harder to trace contacts, harder to transport patients to treatment centres, and harder to maintain the cold chain for medical supplies. It makes it harder to build trust, because the population has learned to be suspicious of anyone who arrives with authority — whether that authority wears a military uniform, a government badge or a WHO vest. And it makes it harder to sustain the response, because the money, the staff and the political attention are all divided between the epidemic and the war.

Dr Smallwood, the WHO's incident manager, has been frank about the difficulties. Staff shortages are a real problem, she has said, because of the violence. The strike by health workers in North Kivu has compounded the problem. And the sheer scale of the outbreak — 12,000 cases and counting — means that the resources available, however generous, are stretched thin.

The international response has been significant but, by most accounts, insufficient. The WHO has deployed staff, the Africa CDC has provided support, and donor governments have pledged money. But the funding has not kept pace with the scale of the crisis, and the political attention has been divided. The world is dealing with multiple crises at once — wars, economic disruption, political upheaval — and an Ebola outbreak in the eastern DRC, however deadly, struggles to command the attention it needs.

Could it spread beyond Africa?

The question that has dominated the international coverage of the outbreak is whether it could spread beyond the region — and, ultimately, beyond Africa. The WHO's warning that a global spread is "possible" was not made lightly. Dr Janabi, the WHO's Regional Director for Africa, told the Metro newspaper that the outbreak could spread worldwide if it was not contained, and the organisation's declaration of a public health emergency of international concern was a formal recognition of that risk.

The risk is real, but it is not the same as the risk posed by a respiratory virus. Ebola is not airborne. It spreads through direct contact with the bodily fluids of a person who is sick with the disease, or with the body of a person who has died from it. That means it does not spread through the air in the way that Covid-19 or influenza do, and it does not spread through casual contact. A person who is infected but not yet showing symptoms is not generally considered infectious. The virus is most dangerous to those who care for the sick, who handle the dead, or who work in healthcare settings without adequate protection.

That is why the spread so far has been limited to the DRC and its immediate neighbours, Kenya and Uganda, where two deaths have been recorded in each country. The pattern is consistent with the way Ebola has always spread: through families, through healthcare settings, through funeral practices, and across borders that are porous and poorly monitored. The risk of a case reaching Europe or North America exists, but it is lower than the risk of a case reaching another African country — and the measures taken by the United States, requiring travellers to attest that they have not been in the DRC, are designed to reduce that risk further.

But the risk is not zero, and the consequences of a single imported case in a country with no experience of Ebola would be significant. The 2014 West Africa epidemic produced a small number of imported cases in the United States and Europe, and each one triggered a major public health response, extensive media coverage and considerable public anxiety. A case imported from the current outbreak would be no different — and if the imported case were of the Bundibugyo strain, for which there is no approved vaccine, the response would be harder still.

The WHO's warning, then, is best understood as a call for action rather than a prediction of doom. It is a warning that the outbreak is not contained, that it is growing, that it has already crossed international borders, and that the conditions for further spread — conflict, displacement, weak health systems, porous borders — are all present. The organisation is not saying that Ebola will reach London or New York. It is saying that it could, and that the only way to prevent it is to stop the outbreak where it is.

A short history of Ebola — and why this one is different

Ebola was first identified in 1976, in two simultaneous outbreaks in Sudan and in what is now the DRC, near the Ebola River from which the virus takes its name. Since then, the DRC has experienced more Ebola outbreaks than any other country — 17 in total, including the current one. The virus is believed to circulate in fruit bats, which can carry it without becoming ill, and to spill over into humans when people come into contact with infected animals or their bodily fluids.

The West Africa epidemic of 2014 to 2016 was the largest and deadliest Ebola outbreak in history. It began in Guinea, spread to Liberia and Sierra Leone, and killed more than 11,000 people over two years. It overwhelmed the health systems of three countries, triggered a massive international response, and led to the development of the vaccines and treatments that are now used against the Zaire strain. It also taught the world a painful lesson: that an Ebola outbreak in a region with weak health systems and limited international attention can grow from a handful of cases into a catastrophe before the world notices.

The current outbreak is different in several important ways. It is caused by the Bundibugyo strain, not the Zaire strain, which means the vaccines that were developed after West Africa do not apply. It is taking place in a region affected by armed conflict, which makes the response harder than it was in West Africa. It is spreading faster than the West Africa epidemic did — at twice the rate, according to the reports — which means the window for containment is narrower. And it is happening at a time when the world's attention is divided, when the funding for global health is under pressure, and when the institutions that are supposed to respond are themselves stretched thin.

There is one way in which the current outbreak is similar to West Africa, and it is the most important similarity of all. In both cases, the early response was slower and smaller than it needed to be, and the outbreak grew while the world debated whether it was a crisis. By the time the WHO declared a public health emergency of international concern, the outbreak was already large, already spreading, and already difficult to contain. The declaration was necessary, but it came late — and the delay, as in West Africa, may prove costly.

What containment would take

Containing an Ebola outbreak of this scale requires a combination of things, all of which are difficult in the eastern DRC. The first is surveillance: finding every case, as quickly as possible, and tracing every person who has been in contact with that case. That requires health workers on the ground, in every affected village, with the ability to test suspected cases and to follow up on contacts for 21 days — the maximum incubation period of the virus. In a region where roads are unsafe and where communities are wary of outsiders, that work is slow and dangerous.

The second is isolation and treatment. People who are sick with Ebola need to be isolated from their families and communities, and treated in specialised centres where health workers wear full protective equipment. That is frightening for patients and their families, who may not understand why their loved one is being taken away, and who may fear that they will never see them again. Building trust — persuading communities to bring their sick to treatment centres rather than hiding them — is one of the hardest parts of the response, and it is one that cannot be achieved by force.

The third is safe burial. Ebola is highly infectious in the bodies of those who have died from it, and traditional funeral practices — which involve washing and touching the body — have been a major driver of transmission in past outbreaks. Safe burial teams, who handle the dead with protective equipment and with respect for local customs, are essential. But they are also deeply unpopular in some communities, where they are seen as interfering with sacred rituals. Negotiating that tension requires patience, cultural sensitivity and time — all of which are in short supply.

The fourth is the development and deployment of a vaccine for the Bundibugyo strain. Experimental vaccines exist, and research is ongoing, but there is no approved vaccine ready for mass deployment. The development of a vaccine for the Zaire strain took years, and it was accelerated by the urgency of the West Africa epidemic. A similar effort for the Bundibugyo strain would require the same urgency, the same funding, and the same political will — and it would still take time that the people of the eastern DRC do not have.

And the fifth is the resolution, or at least the mitigation, of the conflict that makes all of the above so difficult. As long as armed groups control territory in Ituri and North Kivu, as long as health workers are at risk of attack, as long as the population is displaced and distrustful, the Ebola response will be fighting with one hand tied behind its back. The epidemic and the war are not separate crises. They are the same crisis, seen from two different angles, and they will not be resolved separately.

What happens next

The coming weeks will determine whether the outbreak can be contained or whether it will continue to grow. If the case count continues to rise at its current rate, it will pass 15,000, then 20,000, and the death toll will climb with it. The spread to Kenya and Uganda, where two deaths have already been recorded in each country, may widen to other neighbours. The WHO's warning of global spread may become more urgent. And the international response, which has so far been significant but insufficient, will face a test of whether it can scale up fast enough to match the scale of the crisis.

If, on the other hand, the response is strengthened — if more staff are deployed, if the strike by health workers is resolved, if funding is increased, if communities are engaged and trust is built — the outbreak could be brought under control, as previous outbreaks in the DRC have been. The country has contained 16 Ebola outbreaks before, and it has the experience, the institutions and the people to contain a 17th. What it needs is the support to do so, and the time to do it in.

The most important number to watch is not the total case count, which will continue to rise for some time even if the response is working, but the rate of new cases — whether the epidemic curve is bending downwards. That is the measure that will tell the world whether the outbreak is being contained or is still out of control. It is also the measure that is hardest to assess in real time, because the data from the affected region is incomplete, delayed and subject to revision — as the retrospective addition of 314 deaths in Ituri demonstrated.

For the people of the eastern DRC, the statistics are not abstract. They are neighbours, family members, children. They are the 5,500 people who have already died, and the thousands more who are sick now. They are the health workers on strike, the families who have lost children, the communities that have watched the virus arrive and have had no vaccine to protect them. The world has a responsibility to them that goes beyond warnings and declarations. It has a responsibility to provide the people, the money and the tools to stop the outbreak — and to do so before the numbers climb higher still.

The Ebola outbreak in the DRC is the second deadliest in history. It is spreading at twice the rate of the deadliest. It has no vaccine. It is taking place in a war zone. And the world has been warned. What happens next depends on whether that warning is heeded.
