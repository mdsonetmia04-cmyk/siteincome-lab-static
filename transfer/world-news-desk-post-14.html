TITLE: Steven Spielberg Is Finally Making a Western — and Paul Thomas Anderson Wrote It LABELS: Entertainment SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Steven Spielberg will direct his first Western, The Heart in Winter, scripted by Paul Thomas Anderson and starring Cillian Murphy and Jessie Buckley. ---BODY---

Steven Spielberg at the Berlinale, 2023. Photo: Martin Kraft / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).

In a career spanning more than five decades, Steven Spielberg has made the defining blockbuster, the defining science-fiction film, the defining war film, and the defining historical drama. He has made dinosaurs, aliens, sharks, soldiers, and spies. He has won three Academy Awards and directed some of the most successful films ever made. There is one thing he has never done. He has never made a Western — the genre that, more than any other, defined American cinema. Until now. Spielberg is set to direct his first Western, an adaptation of Kevin Barry's novel "The Heart in Winter," with a script by Paul Thomas Anderson — the first screenplay Anderson has ever written for another director — and starring Cillian Murphy and Jessie Buckley. Filming is scheduled to begin in late 2027.

The announcement, which landed this week, is one of the most significant pieces of film news of the year, and not only because of who is involved. It is significant because of what it represents: the greatest living American director, at 79, taking on the genre that shaped the movies he grew up watching — and doing it with a writer and two actors who are, between them, among the most respected artists working in film today. Here is what we know about the film, why it matters, and what the rest of the entertainment world looks like this week.

The film: a Western, at last

"The Heart in Winter" is an adaptation of the novel by Kevin Barry, the acclaimed Irish writer whose work has won some of the most prestigious prizes in contemporary literature. The novel is a Western romance — a sweeping story of love and survival set against the harsh landscape of the American frontier — and it is the kind of material that has drawn the greatest filmmakers to the genre for a century: a story in which the land itself is a character, and in which human beings are tested by a world that does not care whether they live or die.

The screenplay is by Paul Thomas Anderson, and that fact alone makes the project extraordinary. Anderson — the director of "Boogie Nights," "Magnolia," "There Will Be Blood," "The Master," "Phantom Thread," and "Licorice Pizza" — is one of the most celebrated filmmakers of his generation, and he has always written his own films. "The Heart in Winter" will be the first screenplay he has written for another director. The collaboration between Anderson and Spielberg is, in other words, a meeting of two of the greatest filmmakers alive — one writing, the other directing — and the result will be watched by the entire film world.

The stars are Cillian Murphy and Jessie Buckley, two actors at the peak of their powers. Murphy, who won the Academy Award for Best Actor for his performance as J. Robert Oppenheimer in Christopher Nolan's "Oppenheimer," is one of the most intense and precise actors of his generation, and his work in "Peaky Blinders" made him a global star. Buckley, who was nominated for an Academy Award for her performance in "The Lost Daughter" and who has delivered a series of extraordinary performances in "Women Talking," "Men," and "Wicked Little Letters," is one of the most versatile and fearless actresses working today. Together, they are a pairing of rare promise — and a sign of the ambition of the project.

Filming is scheduled for late 2027, which means the film will not reach audiences until 2028 at the earliest. The long lead time is typical for a project of this scale, and it reflects the care with which Spielberg approaches his work. At 79, he is not rushing. He is choosing his projects carefully, and the fact that he has chosen a Western — the one genre he has never attempted — suggests that he regards it as a film worth the wait.

Why a Western, and why now

The question that everyone is asking is the obvious one: why has Spielberg never made a Western before, and why is he making one now? The answer to the first part is, in a sense, that he has been making Westerns all along — without the hats and horses. The Western is not really a genre about the frontier. It is a genre about a set of ideas: about the individual against the community, about violence and its consequences, about the law and the people who live outside it, about the American myth and its discontents. Those ideas run through Spielberg's work from the beginning. "Jaws" is a Western, in the sense that it is about a small community, an outsider threat, and a man who must face it alone. "Saving Private Ryan" is a Western, in the sense that it is about a mission, a code, and the cost of honoring it. "Lincoln" is a Western, in the sense that it is about a man trying to hold a country together.

But there is a difference between a film that contains the ideas of the Western and a film that is a Western — that is set on the frontier, in the landscape, with the genre's iconography and its history. Spielberg has never made that film. He has made films in every other genre that defined American cinema, and the absence of a Western from his filmography has been one of the great curiosities of his career. It is as if the greatest chef in the world had never cooked the most famous dish in his country's cuisine.

Why now? Part of the answer is the material. Kevin Barry's novel is, by all accounts, a remarkable book — a Western that is also a love story, a story of two people thrown together by circumstance and tested by a landscape that does not forgive. It is the kind of material that speaks to Spielberg's deepest interests: the outsider, the family, the journey, and the price of survival. Part of the answer is Anderson, whose screenplay is the bridge between the novel and the film, and whose involvement signals the seriousness of the project. And part of the answer is simply that Spielberg, at 79, is thinking about legacy — about the films he has not yet made, and about the one genre that remains.

There is also the state of the Western itself. The genre has been in a long renaissance, from the revisionist Westerns of the 1990s to the prestige television of the 2010s and 2020s. The Western, which was declared dead more times than any genre in film history, keeps coming back — because its themes, of land and law and violence and redemption, are the themes of America itself, and they do not go away. Spielberg's entry into the genre, at this moment, is both a recognition of that renaissance and a contribution to it. The greatest living American director is making a Western. The genre could ask for no greater endorsement.

Spielberg at 79: the career that contains multitudes

To appreciate what "The Heart in Winter" represents, it helps to remember what Spielberg has already done. Born on December 18, 1946, in Cincinnati, Ohio, he began making films as a child, and he has been making them, professionally, for more than fifty years. His filmography is a history of American cinema in the second half of the twentieth century and the first quarter of the twenty-first.

He invented the summer blockbuster with "Jaws" in 1975, a film that changed the economics of Hollywood and the expectations of audiences forever. He made the definitive science-fiction film of the 1970s with "Close Encounters of the Third Kind" in 1977, and the definitive family adventure with "E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial" in 1982. He created Indiana Jones with "Raiders of the Lost Ark" in 1981, and he redefined the dinosaur film — and the possibilities of computer-generated imagery — with "Jurassic Park" in 1993. In the same year, he made "Schindler's List," a film about the Holocaust that won seven Academy Awards, including Best Picture and Best Director, and that stands as one of the most important films ever made. He made "Saving Private Ryan" in 1998, a film that redefined the war movie and won him a second Best Director Oscar. And he has continued, into his seventies, to make films that matter — "Lincoln," "Bridge of Spies," "The Post," "West Side Story," "The Fabelmans" — each one a reminder that his ambition has not diminished with age.

What is remarkable about that career is its range. Spielberg has made films in almost every genre: the thriller, the adventure, the science-fiction epic, the war film, the historical drama, the musical, the autobiographical film. He has made films for children and films for adults, films that broke box office records and films that won Academy Awards, films that entertained millions and films that changed how people think about history. The only major genre he has not attempted is the Western. And now, at 79, he is attempting it — with a script by Paul Thomas Anderson, and with two of the finest actors of their generation in the leads.

It is tempting to see "The Heart in Winter" as a capstone — as the final piece in a career that has touched every corner of American cinema. But Spielberg has resisted that framing before, and he will resist it again. He does not make films to complete a set. He makes them because a story moves him, because a collaboration excites him, and because he still believes, after five decades, that the movies can do something that nothing else can. The Western is not the last genre on a checklist. It is the next story he wants to tell.

Anderson, Murphy, Buckley: the collaborators

The collaborators on "The Heart in Winter" are, each in their own way, as significant as Spielberg himself. Paul Thomas Anderson is 56, and he is widely regarded as the finest American filmmaker of his generation. His films — from "Boogie Nights" in 1997 to "Licorice Pizza" in 2021 — are characterized by their ambition, their intelligence, and their refusal to compromise. He writes his own screenplays, directs them himself, and has never, until now, written for another director. That he has chosen to write for Spielberg is a statement about both men: about Anderson's respect for Spielberg, and about Spielberg's willingness to be guided by a writer whose vision is as strong as his own.

Cillian Murphy is 50, and he is coming off the greatest success of his career. His performance as J. Robert Oppenheimer in "Oppenheimer" won him the Academy Award for Best Actor, and it confirmed what those who had followed his work already knew: that he is an actor of extraordinary range and intensity, capable of carrying a film on the strength of a look. His work in "Peaky Blinders," as the gangster Thomas Shelby, made him a global star, and his performances in "28 Days Later," "Inception," and "Dunkirk" established him as one of the most reliable actors of his generation. In a Western, his stillness — the quality that made Oppenheimer so compelling — will be a formidable asset.

Jessie Buckley is 36, and she is one of the most exciting actresses working in film. Her Academy Award nomination for "The Lost Daughter" announced her as a major talent, and her performances since — in "Women Talking," in "Men," in "Wicked Little Letters," and in "Fingernails" — have confirmed it. She is an actress of remarkable versatility, capable of comedy and tragedy, of stillness and fury, and she brings to every role a fearlessness that is rare in contemporary film. In a Western romance, opposite Murphy, she will have the opportunity to do some of the finest work of her career.

Together, the four of them — Spielberg, Anderson, Murphy, Buckley — represent a convergence of talent that is rare in contemporary cinema. It is the kind of project that reminds the industry of what the movies can be when the best people in the business decide to work together on a story they believe in.

The rest of the week in entertainment

The Spielberg news landed in a week that was already crowded with entertainment stories. The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, which opened to $120 million domestically in its first weekend, has now reached its final actuals: $119.3 million in North America and $224.3 million worldwide. The figures confirm it as the biggest opening of the six-film Hunger Games franchise, and they underline the enduring power of the series — and of the young-adult adaptation as a box office force — more than a decade after the first film was released.

And in the world of video games, the launch of Grand Theft Auto VI on November 19 has become the entertainment event of the year. Creators are posting vast amounts of video from the game, and the launch — after thirteen years of anticipation — is dominating social media, streaming platforms, and the conversation around gaming. The game, set in the fictional state of Leonida and the city of Vice City, is the most anticipated entertainment release in history, and its first days have lived up to the hype.

Meanwhile, "Wicked: For Good," the second part of the musical adaptation, continues its run, with $147 million domestically and approximately $223 million worldwide — the biggest opening ever for a Broadway musical adaptation. The two films, together, have made the "Wicked" franchise one of the most successful musical properties in cinema history, and they have confirmed the power of the theatrical musical on the big screen.

It is a week, in other words, in which the entertainment industry is firing on all cylinders — a blockbuster franchise, a record-breaking video game launch, a musical phenomenon, and now, the announcement that the greatest living American director is making his first Western. The movies, for all the talk of their decline, are having a very good week.

What comes next

Filming on "The Heart in Winter" is scheduled for late 2027, which means that the film will not reach audiences until 2028 at the earliest. Between now and then, there will be casting announcements, location reports, and the slow accumulation of detail that accompanies any major production. The film will be shot — most likely on location, in the landscapes that the Western demands — and it will be edited, scored, and released into a world that will be watching.

What the film will be, no one can yet say. But what it represents is already clear. It represents the greatest living American director taking on the genre that defined American cinema. It represents a collaboration between two of the finest filmmakers alive. And it represents a story — a Western romance, written by Kevin Barry, scripted by Paul Thomas Anderson, starring Cillian Murphy and Jessie Buckley — that has the potential to be one of the great films of its year.

Spielberg has spent fifty years making the movies that defined their genres. Now, at 79, he is making the one genre he has never defined. The Western has been waiting for him. And he has finally arrived.

The Westerns that shaped Spielberg

Spielberg did not grow up in a vacuum. He grew up in the golden age of the Western — in the decades when the genre was the dominant form of American popular cinema, and when its greatest directors were making the films that would define it for all time. John Ford, the director who did more than any other to create the visual language of the Western, made "Stagecoach" in 1939, "The Searchers" in 1956, and "The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance" in 1962 — films that Spielberg has cited, throughout his career, as among the most important influences on his work. Ford's compositions — the vast landscapes, the tiny figures against enormous skies, the doorways framing the desert — are visible in Spielberg's films from the beginning, from the open spaces of "Close Encounters" to the battlefields of "Saving Private Ryan."

Howard Hawks, another of the genre's masters, made "Red River" in 1948 and "Rio Bravo" in 1959 — films about men, duty, and the codes that bind them, themes that run through Spielberg's work as well. Sergio Leone, the Italian director who reinvented the Western in the 1960s with "The Good, the Bad and the Ugly," showed that the genre could be operatic, violent, and mythic all at once — and his influence, on Spielberg and on every director who followed, is incalculable. And Clint Eastwood, who starred in Leone's films and then became a director himself, made "Unforgiven" in 1992 — a film that dismantled the Western's myths even as it honored them, and that won the Academy Award for Best Picture.

The Western did not stop evolving. "Dances with Wolves" in 1990, "No Country for Old Men" in 2007, "True Grit" in 2010, "The Power of the Dog" in 2021 — each generation has produced Westerns that reimagined the genre for its own time, and each has shown that the Western's themes — of land and law, of violence and redemption, of the individual and the community — are inexhaustible. Spielberg's entry into the genre is not an arrival at a finished form. It is an entry into a living tradition, one that has been shaped by the greatest directors in the history of cinema, and that now includes him.

What Spielberg brings to that tradition is his own visual language — the language he has spent fifty years developing, from the shark in "Jaws" to the dinosaurs in "Jurassic Park" to the soldiers on Omaha Beach. It is a language of scale and intimacy, of vast landscapes and human faces, of spectacle and emotion held in the same frame. Applied to the Western — to the deserts, the mountains, the small towns, and the people who live in them — it promises a film that is both recognizably Spielberg and recognizably a Western, and that is something the genre has never seen before.

The bottom line

Steven Spielberg will direct his first Western, "The Heart in Winter," an adaptation of Kevin Barry's novel, with a screenplay by Paul Thomas Anderson — the first script Anderson has ever written for another director — and starring Cillian Murphy and Jessie Buckley. It is the most significant film announcement of the year, and it is significant for a simple reason: the greatest living American director, at 79, is taking on the genre that shaped the movies he grew up watching, and he is doing it with two of the finest actors of their generation.

But the week's biggest story is the one that will not reach screens until 2028: Spielberg, at last, is making a Western.
