With two seconds left on the clock, a 59-yard field goal hanging in the air, and 90,000 people inside AT&T Stadium holding their breath, Jake Elliott did what he has done so many times before for the Philadelphia Eagles: he split the uprights, and a game that had looked dead and buried came back to life. The Eagles beat the Dallas Cowboys 39-38 on Thanksgiving Day, November 26, 2026, completing a 21-point comeback that is the largest in the history of Thanksgiving football in the National Football League. They did it without their starting quarterback, without their star running back, and with a quarterback who used to be on the Dallas practice squad. They did it after trailing 31-10 with barely three minutes left in the third quarter, when the computers gave Dallas a 99.4 percent chance of winning. And they did it in the cruellest possible way for the home crowd: Dallas never trailed until the final kick of the game.

The numbers alone tell a story that will be retold for years. Philadelphia scored 22 points in the fourth quarter. The Eagles outscored the Cowboys 29-7 over the final 18 minutes of the game. Joe Milton, making only his third career start, finished 21 of 31 for 331 yards, three touchdowns and one interception, and added a rushing touchdown for good measure. DeVonta Smith caught passes for 200 yards and two touchdowns, including strikes of 50 and 45 yards. The Cowboys, for their part, set records of their own, and none of them are records any team would want: they became the first team in NFL history to lose twice in the same season at home with a lead of 14 or more points in the fourth quarter, and the first team in league history to average at least 30 points a game through 12 games — 31.3, to be exact — and still have a losing record. The final score, 39-38, does not begin to capture how improbable it all was.

Thanksgiving football is one of the great American traditions, and the Dallas Cowboys have been its centrepiece for six decades. The late-afternoon game in Arlington is appointment television, a fixture that stops the country in the middle of its turkey dinners. This year it delivered one of the greatest games ever played on the holiday, and it did so for a Philadelphia team that arrived in Texas with almost nothing to play for except pride. The Eagles were 3-7, coming off a six-game losing streak, and they had lost their starting quarterback, Jalen Hurts, to blood clots. Their season, by any reasonable measure, was over. What happened on Thursday night was not reasonable at all.

A game Dallas dominated — until it did not

For the first two and a half quarters, the game went exactly as the form book suggested it would. Dallas led 28-10 at half-time, and the lead was built the way Dallas has built leads all season: with an offence that scores in bunches. Dak Prescott threw for two touchdowns and ran for another, and Javonte Williams scored three touchdowns of his own, powering the Cowboys to a first-half lead that felt comfortable and, by the third quarter, felt decisive. When the Cowboys stretched the advantage to 31-10 with 24 seconds left in the third quarter, the game was, by every measure available, over. The win-probability models gave Dallas a 99.4 percent chance of winning. The Eagles had scored nothing in the first quarter. Their starting quarterback was in street clothes. Their star running back, Saquon Barkley, had left the game in the first quarter with a stinger. Tight end Dallas Goedert was out with a shoulder injury, and cornerback Cooper DeJean had left with a concussion. The team on the field was, in the words of one report, depleted almost beyond recognition.

What followed was the most extraordinary 18 minutes of the NFL season. Milton, the 26-year-old quarterback who had been signed off the Dallas practice squad in mid-October, hit DeVonta Smith for a 45-yard touchdown late in the third quarter to make it 31-17. It was the first sign that the game was not finished, but it was only a sign. The fourth quarter was where the comeback became history. Philadelphia scored 22 points in the final period, including a blocked punt returned for a touchdown, and the Cowboys, who had not trailed all night, watched a 21-point lead evaporate in front of a stunned home crowd. The Eagles scored four of the final five touchdowns in the game, and when the dust settled, the team that had trailed by three touchdowns had won by a single point.

Joe Milton: the revenge game nobody saw coming

Every great comeback needs an unlikely hero, and this one had a perfect one. Joe Milton III was, until a few weeks ago, a footnote in the NFL: a big-armed quarterback who had spent time on the Dallas practice squad and had never been given a real chance. The Cowboys kept him around as insurance for Dak Prescott and then let him go. The Eagles signed him in mid-October, and when Jalen Hurts was diagnosed with blood clots — a condition that can be career-threatening, and that has now sidelined one of the league's best quarterbacks indefinitely — Milton was the next man up. Thursday was his third career start, and it was the game of his life: 21 of 31 passing for 331 yards, three touchdowns, one interception, and a rushing touchdown on top. His 331 passing yards were a career high, and his composure in the final minutes, driving a depleted offence into field goal range with the season effectively on the line, was the performance of a quarterback playing far above his résumé.

The irony was not lost on anyone in Arlington. Milton had been Prescott's backup in Dallas, a player the Cowboys had decided they did not need. On Thanksgiving, he outplayed the man he used to back up, and he did it in the stadium where he used to practise. His 50-yard touchdown strike to DeVonta Smith in the second quarter was a throw that showcased the arm strength that has always been his calling card — a deep heave that travelled the length of the field and landed in stride. His 45-yard touchdown to the same receiver late in the third quarter started the comeback. And his work on the final drive, after Dallas had gone for it on fourth down and failed, set up the kick that won the game. The Eagles' official game recap put it simply: Milton returned to Dallas and threw for 331 yards and accounted for four touchdowns as the Eagles overcame a 21-point second-half deficit. The numbers do not capture the strangeness of it: a practice-squad castoff, returning to his old building, ending his old team's season on the biggest stage of the regular season.

The context makes the performance even more remarkable. Milton was not just filling in for Hurts. He was playing behind an offensive line that had lost its rhythm, throwing to receivers who had lost their timing with a new quarterback, and doing it all without the running game that is supposed to make a back-up quarterback's life easier. Barkley's departure in the first quarter removed the Eagles' most dangerous weapon, and the loss of Goedert removed one of Milton's most reliable targets. That he finished the night with 331 yards and four total touchdowns, against a Dallas defence that had spent the first half dominating, is a measure of how completely the game turned in the second half, and of how well Milton handled the moment when it mattered most.

The fourth quarter: a blocked punt, a missed kick and a gamble that backfired

The comeback was not a single play. It was a cascade, and each piece of it made the next one possible. The turning point came early in the fourth quarter, when the Cowboys still led by 14 and were punting from deep in their own territory. Robinson broke through and blocked Bryan Anger's punt, and Kenny Moore II picked up the bouncing ball at the Dallas 3-yard line and walked into the end zone. It was the kind of play that changes the arithmetic of a game in an instant: a two-touchdown lead became a one-touchdown lead, and a stadium that had been celebrating began to go quiet.

Dallas answered, as good teams do. Prescott, who had been excellent all night, ran for a 14-yard touchdown, vaulting over Moore on his way into the end zone, to restore the two-touchdown lead. But the Eagles were not finished. Milton led another scoring drive, and when Makai Lemon caught a 24-yard touchdown pass with 2:10 remaining, the score was 38-36. The Eagles went for a two-point conversion and did not get it, which meant they still needed a field goal to win — and they needed the ball back. That is where the game's most debated decision arrived. With 55 seconds remaining, leading by two points, facing fourth-and-1 from the Philadelphia 39-yard line, Dallas head coach Brian Schottenheimer decided to go for it rather than punt. Prescott was sacked at the Philadelphia 48, the Eagles took over, and 11 yards was all they needed to give Elliott a realistic shot at the winning kick. Schottenheimer defended the call afterwards, but the decision will be argued about in Dallas for a long time, and not only because of what happened next: on the ensuing possession, Markquese Bell was flagged for pass interference on third-and-one, gifting the Eagles a first down and the field position they needed.

There was one more twist. Before the Eagles pulled within two, Dallas kicker Brandon Aubrey — one of the most reliable kickers in the league — missed a 40-yard field goal that would have stretched the lead to five points and forced Philadelphia to score a touchdown to win. In a game decided by a single point, the miss looms as large as any play in the fourth quarter. And so the stage was set for Elliott, whose 59-yard kick with two seconds left was the longest game-winning field goal of his career and the final act of a comeback that ESPN's Adam Schefter described in a single line: the Eagles erased a 21-point deficit for the largest Thanksgiving Day comeback in NFL history, beating the Cowboys 39-38. Some reports put the kick at 60 yards; the official game recap and the Associated Press put it at 59. Either way, it was a kick that will live in Eagles history.

The records — and what they say about both teams

The record books will remember this game for a long time. The 21-point comeback is the largest in the history of Thanksgiving football, a fixture that has been played since 1920. It also ties the biggest fourth-quarter comeback ever seen on Thanksgiving: the Chicago Bears came back from 14 down to beat the Detroit Lions 23-17 in overtime in 1980, and the Eagles' rally matched that mark for fourth-quarter drama. The win probability figure — 99.4 percent for Dallas when the lead reached 21 points in the third quarter — will be quoted for years as a measure of how unlikely the result was. And the individual performances will stand on their own: Smith's 200 receiving yards and two touchdowns, Milton's 331 passing yards and four total touchdowns, and Elliott's 59-yard winner.

But the records that matter most may be the ones Dallas set, because they describe a team that has found a new and painful way to lose. The Cowboys are now the first team in NFL history to lose twice in the same season at home with a lead of 14 or more points in the fourth quarter. They are the first team in league history to average at least 30 points a game through 12 games and have a losing record. They set an NFL record with a fourth home loss this season when scoring at least 30 points. And they began November by matching a franchise record with a blown fourth-quarter lead of 14 points in a 33-30 overtime loss to Arizona — and ended the month by blowing a 21-point lead to Philadelphia. The pattern is not a coincidence. It is a team that can score with anyone and cannot hold a lead against anyone, and the Thanksgiving loss is the most vivid illustration of that failure yet.

For Philadelphia, the win changes the shape of the season without changing the arithmetic very much. The Eagles improved to 4-7, and they have now won two straight games to break a six-game losing skid, having beaten the Pittsburgh Steelers the previous weekend. They are back in the NFC East hunt, at least mathematically, and the defending division champions are not dead yet, even after losing Hurts to blood clots. The Cowboys fell to 5-7, still alive in the division race but badly in need of wins, and with the pressure on Schottenheimer sure to intensify after another squandered lead. Dallas had beaten Philadelphia 38-21 on October 26 and was trying for a rare season sweep; instead, it suffered the most humiliating defeat of its season, in front of its own fans, on its own holiday.

What Hurts' absence means — and what comes next

The most serious story to come out of the game has nothing to do with the score. Jalen Hurts, the Eagles' franchise quarterback, was diagnosed with blood clots, a condition that is treated with blood thinners and that carries risks far beyond football. The team has given no timetable for his return, and the nature of the condition means there may not be one for some time. Hurts has led the Eagles to a fourth-quarter comeback eight times since 2023, and his absence was supposed to end the team's season. Instead, his replacement produced one of the great performances in franchise history. That does not make the situation any less worrying for Hurts himself, and the Eagles will need to manage his health with a care that goes well beyond the football field. Blood clots are not a football injury. They are a medical condition, and the priority is the player, not the play-offs.

For Milton, the game changes everything. A quarterback who was on a practice squad six weeks ago has now won two consecutive starts, thrown for 331 yards on Thanksgiving, and delivered the biggest comeback in the history of the holiday. The Eagles' next games will be played with the knowledge that their back-up quarterback can win a game that looked unwinnable, and that knowledge changes the way a team prepares, the way it calls plays and the way it believes in itself. The six-game losing skid is over. The two-game winning streak is real. And the team that was supposed to be playing out the string is, suddenly, a team with a story.

For Dallas, the questions are harder. The offence is historically productive — 31.3 points a game through 12 games, the first team in history to average 30 or more and have a losing record — and the defence is historically incapable of protecting a lead. The head coach's fourth-down gamble will be second-guessed, the missed field goal will be replayed, and the blocked punt will be studied. But the deeper problem is the pattern: a team that blows a 14-point fourth-quarter lead to Arizona at the start of November and a 21-point lead to Philadelphia at the end of it has a problem that no single play can explain. The Cowboys are 5-7, the division-leading New York Giants are pulling away, and the path to the play-offs, already difficult, is now harder still. The Thanksgiving game was supposed to be a showcase for Dallas football. It became a showcase for everything that has gone wrong with it.

Why Thanksgiving games matter — and why this one will be remembered

Thanksgiving football occupies a strange and special place in American life. The games are played while families eat, while the parade is still being discussed, while the country is, for a few hours, doing the same thing at the same time. The Dallas game, played in the late afternoon, is the centrepiece, and the team has hosted it every year since 1966. The history of the fixture is full of famous games, but very few of them have produced a finish like this one. The largest Thanksgiving comeback in history, a 59-yard field goal with two seconds left, a practice-squad quarterback beating his old team, a team that lost its starting quarterback, its star running back, its tight end and its cornerback in the same game — and won anyway. It is the kind of game that will be shown in highlight packages for decades, and that will be the first thing people mention when they talk about Thanksgiving 2026.

It is also a reminder of why the NFL remains the most popular sport in America, and why a regular-season game in late November can feel like a championship. The league's structure — a short season, a single-elimination play-off, a salary cap that keeps teams close — produces games like this one with a regularity that no other sport can match. A team that was 3-7 and written off can beat a team that was supposed to sweep it, and can do it in the most dramatic way imaginable, because the gap between the best and the worst in the NFL is smaller than in any other major league. The Eagles were given a 0.6 percent chance of winning when they trailed by 21 points in the third quarter. They won. That is the whole story, and it is enough.
