An American Airlines Boeing 737-800 on final approach to Boston in June 2026 (file photo). Flight 1494, a late-night service from San Diego to Chicago, was diverted to Phoenix after a passenger tried to manipulate an exit door mid-flight. Photo: 4300streetcar via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

It was an hour into the red-eye flight from San Diego to Chicago, and most of the cabin was asleep. Then a flight attendant started screaming. "Get away from the door!" she shouted, again and again, loud enough to wake the passengers in the rows behind her. A man had left his seat, walked to the front of the aircraft, and was trying to manipulate one of the exit doors — at altitude, in the dark, with more than a hundred people on board.

What happened next, in the early hours of Wednesday, November 25, 2026, was the kind of scene that air travellers fear most and almost never see. Flight attendants and fellow passengers wrestled the man to the floor of the forward cabin, held him down with zip ties, and kept him restrained for the rest of the flight while the captain diverted the aircraft to Phoenix. The plane landed safely at Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport at about 1 a.m. local time. Police officers met it at the gate. The man was taken into custody by the Federal Bureau of Investigation. Nobody else on board was hurt.

The flight was American Airlines Flight 1494, a regularly scheduled late-night service from San Diego International Airport to Chicago O'Hare International Airport. It had departed California late on Tuesday night, November 24, on the eve of the Thanksgiving holiday — one of the busiest travel weeks of the year in the United States. The incident happened roughly an hour into the flight, while the aircraft was flying near the Arizona–California border, south of Needles, California. The flight later continued to Chicago, landing several hours late, at around 5:52 a.m. local time on Wednesday.

American Airlines confirmed the diversion in a statement, saying the flight had been rerouted because of a disruptive customer. "American Airlines flight 1494 diverted to Phoenix (PHX) due to a disruptive customer. Upon arrival, law enforcement met the aircraft and assisted with deplaning the customer before the flight continued to its destination," the company said. The Federal Aviation Administration said it is investigating the incident. The FBI said it is conducting interviews and will consult with the United States Attorney's Office for the District of Arizona to determine whether federal charges will be filed.

An hour into the flight

The flight began like any other red-eye. American Airlines Flight 1494 departed San Diego shortly before 11 p.m. local time on Tuesday, November 24, according to flight-tracking data cited by local news outlets. The aircraft was bound for Chicago O'Hare, one of the busiest airports in the world, on a route that carries business travellers, holidaymakers and connecting passengers across the country. The cabin lights were dimmed. The passengers settled in for the night. Most of them expected to wake up over the Midwest, a few hours from Chicago.

About an hour into the flight, that changed. A male passenger left his assigned seat and walked towards the front of the aircraft. Crew members told him to return to his seat. He refused — repeatedly, according to the Phoenix police, who later described the incident to local television stations. He moved to the front of the cabin, near one of the aircraft's exit doors, and attempted to manipulate it.

Sean Bennett, a passenger on the flight, was asleep when it happened. "So, about an hour into the flight, I was taking a nap because it was a redeye flight to Chicago, and I awoke to a flight attendant screaming to get away from the door," Bennett told ABC7 Chicago. He said the man continued to resist as passengers and crew members attempted to restrain him. "And then they continued to scuffle with this guy, and he continued to resist almost the entire way to the emergency landing in Phoenix," Bennett added.

Bennett's account, posted on Facebook in the hours after the flight landed, was the first public description of what had happened. "So first leg of my flight, AA1494 San Diego to Chicago someone tried to take over the plane. We are emergency landed in Phoenix. Several strong heroes took him down. We are safe," he wrote. The phrase "tried to take over the plane" was Bennett's own characterisation of what he had seen — a passenger's description, not an official finding — but it captured the fear in the cabin. To the people on board, a man fighting with the crew at the front of the aircraft, beside an exit door, looked like a man trying to seize control of the plane.

Video filmed by another passenger and shared on social media showed the struggle in the forward cabin: several passengers and crew members holding the man on the floor in the aisle near the front galley, his hands eventually secured with zip ties. The footage, which was shared with local television stations and posted online, showed a tense but controlled scene — no panic, no screaming from the passengers, just a group of people holding a man down while the aircraft flew on towards Phoenix.

The restraint, and the landing

The crew's response was, by every account, fast and professional. Flight attendants shouted for passengers to move away from the door area. Several passengers got up from their seats and helped the crew restrain the man. Together, they held him on the cabin floor and kept him there until the aircraft reached Phoenix. The Phoenix police said the passenger continued to resist while being restrained, but nobody else on board reported any injuries.

The captain, meanwhile, made the decision every pilot in that situation is trained to make: get the aircraft on the ground as soon as possible. The flight was diverted to Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport, the nearest major airport, and landed without incident at about 1 a.m. local time on Wednesday, November 25. The Federal Aviation Administration confirmed the landing in a statement to ABC7 Chicago: "American Airlines Flight 1494 landed safely at Phoenix Sky Harbor International Airport around 1 a.m. local time on Wednesday, Nov. 25, after the crew reported a passenger disturbance on board."

Law enforcement officers were waiting when the aircraft reached the gate. They met the flight on arrival and assisted airline staff in removing the passenger, who was then taken to a hospital for evaluation after sustaining what the police described as a minor injury. The man was detained and interviewed by the Phoenix police, who passed information from the crew and witnesses to the federal authorities. The FBI later confirmed that the man had been taken into custody. "One man has been taken into custody. The FBI is currently conducting interviews to gather the facts and will consult with the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Arizona to determine if federal charges will be filed," the agency said.

No damage to the aircraft was reported, and no operational emergency — such as a rapid depressurisation — occurred during the incident, according to reports from the scene. The flight was delayed while the airline addressed operational concerns, and then continued on its journey to Chicago, departing Phoenix in the early hours of the morning and landing at O'Hare at about 5:52 a.m. local time, several hours behind schedule. The passengers who had helped restrain the man continued with them. The man did not.

Could the door actually have opened?

The first question most people ask about an incident like this is the obvious one: could the man actually have opened the door? The answer, for a modern airliner at cruising altitude, is almost certainly no — and understanding why helps explain both the fear in the cabin and the calm of the crew.

The exit doors on a commercial airliner are plug-type doors. They are designed to be larger than the opening they fill, so that the pressure inside the cabin — which is much higher than the thin air outside at altitude — pushes the door firmly into its frame. The greater the difference in pressure, the harder the door is pressed shut. At a typical cruising altitude, the force holding a cabin door closed is measured in thousands of pounds. No human being can overcome it by pulling on a handle. The door cannot be opened in flight, not because of a lock or a latch, but because of physics.

That does not mean the attempt was harmless. A passenger who is trying to manipulate a door is a passenger who is not in his seat, not following crew instructions, and creating a disturbance in the most sensitive part of the aircraft. The crew cannot know, in the moment, whether the man is confused, intoxicated, suffering a mental health crisis, or attempting something worse. They cannot know whether he will stop at the door, or whether he will turn on the crew, or try to reach the flight deck. The correct response is the one the crew of Flight 1494 gave: restrain him, get the aircraft on the ground, and let law enforcement sort out what he was trying to do.

It is also worth noting that the danger of a door opening is not the only danger. A struggle in the forward cabin, beside an exit door, can injure passengers and crew. A man who is fighting with flight attendants is a man who is not allowing them to do their jobs — and their jobs include watching the cabin, monitoring the aircraft and responding to emergencies. An unruly passenger is, by definition, a safety risk, whether or not he can actually open a door. That is why the law treats interference with a flight crew as a serious offence, and why the FAA can impose fines of up to 43,658 dollars per violation on passengers who interfere with crew members while they are doing their jobs.

Who was the man, and why did he do it?

As of Thursday, the authorities had not released the man's identity, and they had not said what motivated him. That silence is normal in the early hours of a federal investigation. The FBI said it is conducting interviews to gather the facts. The Phoenix police said they had detained and interviewed the man and passed their reports to the federal authorities. The United States Attorney's Office for the District of Arizona will decide whether federal charges are filed, and if so, what those charges will be.

There are several possibilities, and it would be wrong to guess between them. He may have been suffering a mental health crisis. He may have been under the influence of alcohol or drugs. He may have been confused, or frightened, or acting on a delusion. Or he may have been deliberately attempting to disrupt the flight. The reports from the scene do not say. What they do say is that he left his seat, refused repeated instructions to return to it, attempted to manipulate an exit door, resisted restraint for almost the entire diversion to Phoenix, and sustained a minor injury in the struggle. Those are the facts. The motive is, for now, unknown.

That uncertainty is part of what makes the incident frightening. Air travel depends, to a remarkable degree, on trust: trust that the strangers sitting next to you will behave, trust that the crew can handle anything that happens, trust that the aircraft itself is designed to keep you safe even when someone does something irrational. Most of the time, that trust is justified. On Flight 1494, it was tested — and it held, because the crew and the passengers acted together.

Thanksgiving week, and the busiest skies of the year

The incident happened at the worst possible time for American aviation — or, looked at another way, at a time when the system proved it could cope. The week of Thanksgiving is the busiest travel week of the year in the United States. This year, 81.5 million Americans were expected to travel for the holiday, according to the AAA travel forecast reported by this newspaper earlier this week — a record number, and a figure that includes tens of millions of people flying. The airports were full. The flights were full. And the crews were working at the peak of their annual workload.

The pressure on the system was visible everywhere that week. On Tuesday, a radar issue delayed flights at Chicago O'Hare Airport, the very airport Flight 1494 was bound for, and the Federal Aviation Administration said it could force a halt to arriving flights. On Wednesday, the day after the Flight 1494 incident, heavy rain, wind and flooding hit much of the United States, disrupting Thanksgiving travel on both coasts. The day before, a FedEx cargo jet had run off the runway at Miami International Airport, closing runways and delaying hundreds of flights. The system was, in short, under strain — and the diversion of a single flight to Phoenix was one more problem for controllers, dispatchers and ground crews who were already dealing with a dozen others.

That the system absorbed the incident without serious harm is a credit to the people in it. The flight attendants who shouted the warning, the passengers who left their seats to help, the captain who diverted without hesitation, the controllers who cleared the way to Phoenix, the police officers who were waiting at the gate — each of them did their job, and the result was that a frightening incident ended with one minor injury and a delayed flight. That is how aviation safety is supposed to work: not by preventing every incident, which is impossible, but by making sure that when an incident happens, the response is fast, professional and effective.

It is also worth remembering that Flight 1494 was not the only aviation story of the week. The FedEx runway overrun in Miami, the O'Hare radar outage, the Thanksgiving storms — each of them was a reminder that flying, for all its safety, is a system that operates at the edge of its capacity during the holidays, and that the margin for error is maintained by people, not just by machines. The passengers who helped restrain the man on Flight 1494 were part of that system, whether they knew it or not.

The unruly passenger problem

Flight 1494 was an extreme case, but it was not an isolated one. Unruly passenger incidents have been a growing problem for American aviation since the pandemic, and the Federal Aviation Administration has responded with a zero-tolerance policy and sharply increased fines. Passengers who interfere with flight crew members can face fines of up to 43,658 dollars per violation, and the agency has referred hundreds of cases to the FBI for criminal prosecution. The message, repeated by the FAA for years now, is simple: the crew is in charge, and interfering with them is a federal offence.

The numbers tell the story. The FAA investigated thousands of unruly passenger reports in the years after the pandemic, and while the rate has fallen from its peak, it has not returned to pre-pandemic levels. The incidents range from the trivial — a passenger who refuses to wear a seat belt, or argues about a bag — to the serious: assaults on flight attendants, attempts to open doors, threats against the crew. Flight 1494 was at the serious end of that spectrum, and it is the kind of incident that the FAA's enforcement regime was designed to deter.

There is a debate, in aviation circles, about why the problem has persisted. Some blame alcohol, which is served on most flights and available in most airports. Some blame the stress of modern air travel — the crowds, the delays, the cramped seats, the feeling of being herded. Some blame mental health, and the fact that a person in crisis has nowhere to go on an aircraft. And some blame a broader coarsening of public behaviour, in which people who would never shout at a stranger on the street feel free to shout at a flight attendant. The truth is probably a combination of all of them, and the solution is probably a combination as well: better training for crews, clearer consequences for offenders, and a recognition that the cabin of an aircraft is not a place where bad behaviour can be tolerated.

What happened on Flight 1494 also raises a question about the role of passengers. The men and women who helped restrain the disruptive passenger were not trained for it. They were ordinary travellers who saw a flight attendant in trouble and got up to help. Their actions were brave, and they were effective — but they were also risky. A struggle in a crowded cabin can injure bystanders. A passenger who intervenes can be hurt, or can hurt someone else, or can make a bad situation worse. The airlines do not ask passengers to restrain unruly travellers, and they do not train them to do it. On Flight 1494, the passengers did it anyway, and the outcome was good. It is worth asking whether the system should rely on that kind of improvisation, or whether there is a better way to handle a passenger who will not stay in his seat.

What happens next

The investigation is now in the hands of the FBI and the United States Attorney's Office for the District of Arizona. The FBI said it is conducting interviews to gather the facts, and that it will consult with the Attorney's Office to determine whether federal charges will be filed. If charges are filed, they could include interference with flight crew members, a federal offence that carries significant penalties. The FAA is conducting its own investigation, and could impose civil fines of up to 43,658 dollars per violation.

American Airlines, for its part, has said little beyond confirming the diversion. The company thanked the crew, by implication, in its statement, and it has not commented on the passenger's identity or on whether he will be banned from future flights. Airlines routinely ban passengers who commit serious offences on board, and it is likely that this passenger will not be flying with American again. The company's priority, in the hours after the incident, was to get the aircraft to Chicago and the passengers to their Thanksgiving destinations — which it did, several hours late.

For the passengers on Flight 1494, the story will not end when the investigation does. They will remember the flight attendant's scream, the struggle in the forward cabin, the zip ties, the emergency landing in the dark in Phoenix, and the hours of delay that followed. Some of them will remember it as the worst flight of their lives. Others, like Sean Bennett, will remember it as the flight where strangers became heroes. "Several strong heroes took him down. We are safe," Bennett wrote on Facebook, in the early hours of the morning, from Phoenix. He was right. They were safe. And the reason they were safe was that the people around them — the crew, and the passengers who helped them — did what they were supposed to do, when it mattered most.
