Thousands of prisoners of war could be home with their families before Christmas. The United States envoy Jared Kushner has announced that Russia and Ukraine will hold the largest prisoner exchange since the war began in February 2022 — an operation expected to free more than 1,000 prisoners on each side, dwarfing every swap that has come before it. The first phase could begin within days, in late November or early December, according to Ukraine's presidential chief of staff.

"The goal of the trilateral was really to, number one, see if we could accomplish a big humanitarian prisoner exchange, which will be happening very soon before the holidays, which will be the largest yet of the war," Kushner said in an interview published on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, with Mathias Döpfner, the chief executive of the German media group Axel Springer, on the MD Meets podcast. "Thousands of people will be able to return to their families, which is very important," he added.

The announcement is the first concrete result of the most serious diplomatic effort in months — two days of United States-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11, the first direct meeting between the Russian and Ukrainian delegations in nine months. The talks produced no ceasefire, no agreement on territory, and no breakthrough on ending a war that is nearing its fifth year. But they did produce something that matters enormously to the families of the missing: an agreement in principle on a prisoner exchange on a scale neither side has attempted before.

Kyrylo Budanov, the head of the Office of the President of Ukraine, said on November 25 that the first phase of the exchange could take place in late November or early December. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said on November 13 that the two sides had discussed exchanging 1,000 prisoners each, potentially rising to 1,500 each — which would bring 3,000 people home across both countries. On the same day, Zelenskyy said he expected the final figure to be between 1,200 and 1,300 prisoners from each side. Lieutenant-General Alexander Zorin, a senior Russian military official who attended the Abu Dhabi talks, told Russian state television that the exchange would involve more than 1,000 people on each side and could take place within two or three weeks.

For the swap to count as the largest of the war, it must top the 1,000-for-1,000 exchange of May 2025, which followed three rounds of talks in Istanbul. If the figures now being discussed hold — 1,200 to 1,300, or even 1,500 on each side — it will do so by a wide margin.

The Abu Dhabi breakthrough

The talks in Abu Dhabi were the first between the two warring sides since February 2026, and only the third round hosted in the Emirati capital this year. The United States mediated the meeting, with Kushner and the fellow special envoy Steve Witkoff leading the American side. The Russian delegation was led by Vladimir Medinsky, a presidential aide, and the Ukrainian delegation by Rustem Umerov, who leads Ukraine's Foreign Intelligence Service. The Ukrainian team also included David Arakhamia and Budanov himself.

The talks lasted just under two hours on the Wednesday, after a four-hour session the previous day. When they ended, both sides confirmed the same thing: they had agreed, in principle, to a large-scale prisoner exchange. "One of the positives is we agreed that we're going to have the biggest humanitarian release of prisoners, which we're going to finalise the numbers tomorrow," Kushner said after the talks ended. Medinsky confirmed that both sides had agreed on a large-scale swap and that "mutual understanding had been achieved on some issues", while stressing that the agreement was "in principle". Umerov called the exchange "the main result" of the round, adding that the two sides were "now working to agree on the details and practical implementation".

The day after the talks, Kushner and Witkoff flew to Moscow to meet President Vladimir Putin, and then on to Dublin, Ireland, to brief President Zelenskyy. Putin confirmed that progress had been made on prisoner exchanges. Zelenskyy called the swap "a real result". On November 13, Kushner wrote on the social media platform X that Washington was "working toward what would be the largest prisoner exchange of the war, potentially reuniting thousands of people with their families".

The talks also covered the return of Ukrainian civilians held in captivity, including children taken from the country, Zelenskyy said — an issue that has haunted the war since its earliest months and that Kyiv has raised at every negotiation. Whether civilians will be included in the exchange, and in what numbers, has not been disclosed.

A channel that kept working when nothing else did

Prisoner exchanges have been the one part of the diplomacy of this war that has kept working through nearly five years of fighting. While ceasefire talks have collapsed, summits have failed, and the front line has barely moved, the two sides have continued — every two or three months, in the words of one analyst — to swap 100 to 300 prisoners at a time. It is, as the political analyst Dmitry Medvedenko told Al Jazeera, "a channel that actually works".

The numbers tell the story of a system that has grown steadily more ambitious. In March 2026, each side freed 200 prisoners of war following talks in Geneva in February. The most recent exchange took place on October 23, 2026, when 270 people returned to Ukraine — including 261 military personnel and nine civilians — and the same number were transferred to Russia. That operation was the 11th reported prisoner exchange of 2026, and it brought the cumulative number of captives exchanged through Emirati mediation to 8,537, according to the UAE Foreign Ministry, which has mediated 27 such exchanges.

The largest exchange so far came in May 2025, after three rounds of talks in Istanbul. Over three days, from May 23 to 25, each side released 1,000 people. On the first day, 390 Ukrainians returned home, including 270 military personnel and 120 civilians. Another 307 Ukrainian service members were released the following day, and the final stage, on May 25, saw 303 more Ukrainian military personnel return from Russian captivity. It was, at the time, an operation without precedent in the war — and it is the benchmark the new exchange must beat.

The scale now being discussed would beat it comfortably. At 1,200 to 1,300 prisoners on each side, the exchange would free roughly 2,400 to 2,600 people. At 1,500 each — the upper end of the range Zelenskyy mentioned — it would free 3,000. Either figure would be the largest single release of prisoners in the history of the war, and one of the largest prisoner exchanges in any conflict in decades.

What the talks did not achieve

For all its significance, the prisoner exchange is also a measure of how little else the Abu Dhabi talks achieved. The two days of negotiations ended with no major breakthrough on ending the war, no ceasefire, and no agreement on the central question that has divided the two sides since 2022: territory. Kushner himself described the meeting as "a very tough meeting but a productive one", and spoke of "good momentum" towards a solution before the end of the year. But the hard issues remain exactly where they were.

Territory, by common consent, is the hardest issue of all. Russia controls roughly a fifth of Ukraine's internationally recognised territory, including Crimea, which it annexed in 2014, and large parts of the Donbas, which it has occupied since 2022. Ukraine insists that no peace deal can be built on surrendering land. Russia insists that its territorial gains are non-negotiable. The Abu Dhabi talks made no progress on bridging that gap, and neither side has suggested that they will.

There was, however, one other area of movement. Kushner reported "significant progress" on security guarantees for Ukraine — the question of what assurances Kyiv would receive, from whom, to prevent a future Russian attack if a ceasefire were ever agreed. Security guarantees have been the subject of months of discussion between the United States, Ukraine and European governments, including talks in Oslo on October 20. But guarantees without a ceasefire are a promise without a date, and the Abu Dhabi round produced no date.

Kushner also announced that the next round of talks would be expanded to include European nations — a recognition that any lasting settlement will need European buy-in, and that the Europeans, who have supplied much of Ukraine's military and financial support, cannot be kept outside the room indefinitely. Whether that expansion happens, and when, remains to be seen.

The human arithmetic

Behind the numbers and the negotiations are the people. Every prisoner exchange is, for the families involved, the end of months or years of not knowing — not knowing whether a son, a husband, a father is alive, where he is held, or when he might come home. The Ukrainian government maintains lists of the missing that run into the thousands. The Russian side holds its own lists. Neither side publishes a full accounting of how many of its soldiers are in enemy hands, and the true numbers are a closely guarded secret on both sides.

What is known is that the exchanges have, over nearly five years, returned thousands of people to their families. The 8,537 captives exchanged through Emirati mediation alone — a figure that covers only the exchanges the UAE has brokered — is a measure of the scale of the human traffic. Add the exchanges brokered by other intermediaries, including Turkey, Saudi Arabia and the International Committee of the Red Cross, and the total runs well into five figures. Each one of those numbers is a person who went home.

The new exchange, if it happens on the scale being discussed, will be the largest single homecoming of the war. For the families of the 1,200 to 1,500 prisoners on each side, it will mean the end of a wait that, for some, has lasted since the first months of the invasion. For the prisoners themselves, it will mean the end of captivity in conditions that both sides' human rights monitors have described, in various reports, as harsh. And for the diplomats, it will mean proof that the channel still works — that even in a war with no end in sight, the two sides can still agree on something.

That proof matters, because it is the only evidence that a wider deal might one day be possible. If Russia and Ukraine can agree on the largest prisoner exchange of the war, the reasoning goes, they might one day be able to agree on a ceasefire. It is a thin reed to lean on. But in a war that has produced so little diplomacy of any kind, it is the reed that is available.

The mediators who made it possible

The exchange would not exist without the mediators, and the most important of them has been the United Arab Emirates. The UAE has brokered 27 prisoner exchanges between Russia and Ukraine, more than any other single intermediary, and the 8,537 captives freed through its mediation represent the largest share of all the swaps that have taken place. The Emirates' role is built on relationships with both sides: it has maintained open channels with Moscow and Kyiv throughout the war, it has hosted three rounds of trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi this year alone, and it has the logistical reach — aircraft, secure facilities, diplomatic cover — to move hundreds of people across a war zone and hand them over safely.

The UAE's mediation has been deliberately low-key. Its Foreign Ministry announces each exchange after it happens, with a number and a date, and says little else. That discretion is part of the method: prisoner swaps are among the most sensitive operations in any war, and publicity before the fact can kill them. The October 23 exchange, for example, was confirmed by the UAE only after the 270 prisoners on each side had already been transferred. The same pattern is likely to hold for the Christmas exchange: the world will probably learn that it has happened only when it has already happened.

Other intermediaries have played their part. Turkey brokered the Istanbul talks that produced the May 2025 exchange, and has hosted negotiations at several points in the war. Saudi Arabia has mediated exchanges of foreign fighters. The International Committee of the Red Cross has been present at transfers, verifying identities and ensuring that the handover follows the rules of the Geneva Conventions. But the Abu Dhabi format is different, because it puts the United States in the room as a mediator alongside the two warring parties — a trilateral structure that the Americans have used twice in Abu Dhabi this year, in January and in November, and that they now hope to expand to include European nations.

The expansion matters because the prisoner exchange, for all its humanitarian importance, is not the end of the diplomacy. It is, in the Americans' design, the beginning of it: a confidence-building measure that proves the two sides can agree on something, and that the trilateral format can deliver results. If the Christmas exchange succeeds, the same format — the same room, the same mediators, the same shuttle diplomacy between Moscow and Kyiv — will be used to try for something harder. A ceasefire, for example. Or an agreement on the security guarantees that Kushner says have made "significant progress". Or, one day, a settlement that ends the war. None of that is on the table now. But the prisoner exchange is the proof of concept, and its success or failure will shape everything that follows.

The road from Abu Dhabi to Christmas

What happens between now and the exchange is a matter of logistics, and logistics in this war are never simple. The two sides must agree on the final numbers, and then on the lists: who, exactly, is to be freed. Each side will have its own priorities — its own soldiers, its own civilians, its own bargaining chips. The lists must be verified, the identities confirmed, and the logistics of the transfer arranged: a location, a date, a mechanism for handing people over. In past exchanges, the transfers have taken place on the border, or in a third country, with the Red Cross present. The details of this one have not been announced.

The timing, too, is delicate. Budanov's statement that the first phase could take place in late November or early December suggests that the exchange will happen in stages, as the May 2025 exchange did, over several days rather than all at once. Zorin's estimate of two to three weeks from the Abu Dhabi talks points to a similar timetable. And Kushner's insistence that it will happen "very soon before the holidays" sets a deadline that is both a promise and a pressure: a promise to the families, and a pressure on the negotiators to deliver.

There are reasons to be cautious. The agreement is, as Medinsky stressed, "in principle". The numbers have not been finalised. Neither Kyiv nor Moscow has disclosed the exact number of people who could be included, or the precise date. Until prisoners have been transferred and their identities confirmed, the expected exchange remains a diplomatic commitment rather than a completed humanitarian operation. Previous exchanges have been delayed, renegotiated and, on occasion, cancelled at the last moment. The history of this war is full of announcements that did not survive contact with reality.

But there are also reasons to believe this one will happen. The agreement was confirmed by both sides, in public, within hours of the talks ending. The American envoys flew to Moscow and Dublin to lock it in. The Ukrainian president has put a number on it — 1,200 to 1,300 on each side — which is the kind of specificity that is hard to walk back. And the political incentives point the same way: for Putin, a large exchange is a gesture of goodwill that costs him little; for Zelenskyy, it is a tangible result he can show his people; for the Americans, it is proof that their mediation works.

Why this exchange matters beyond the numbers

The exchange matters for reasons that go beyond the count of prisoners freed. It matters because it is the first product of a new diplomatic format — the trilateral meeting, with the United States in the room as mediator, rather than the bilateral talks that produced the Istanbul exchanges. The trilateral format, first tried in Abu Dhabi in January 2026 and again in November, is the format the Americans believe can eventually produce a ceasefire. If it can produce the largest prisoner exchange of the war, the argument goes, it might one day produce something bigger.

It matters, too, because of what it says about the state of the war. Nearly five years after Russia's full-scale invasion, the fighting continues, the front line barely moves, and neither side can win outright. In that stalemate, the prisoner exchange is one of the few things that both sides still want — one of the few areas where their interests align. That alignment is narrow, and it is humanitarian rather than political. But it is real, and it has survived every setback of the last five years.

And it matters because of the timing. Christmas is the holiday that both countries, in their different ways, observe — Orthodox Christmas in Russia and Ukraine falls on January 7, but the Western Christmas of December 25 has become, in wartime Ukraine, a symbol of defiance and of belonging to Europe. A prisoner exchange before Christmas, on either calendar, is a gift to thousands of families that no other diplomacy could deliver. It is, as Kushner put it, "very important" — a phrase that, for once, does not sound like an understatement.

The coming days will show whether the promise holds. The lists must be agreed, the numbers finalised, the logistics arranged. The first phase, Budanov said, could come in late November or early December. If it does, and if the full exchange follows, it will be the largest prisoner swap of the war — and, for thousands of families, the best Christmas present they could hope for.
