TITLE: Salmonella Found in 43% of Butterball's Ground Turkey — and There Is No Recall, No Fine, Nothing LABELS: Health SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Federal inspectors found salmonella in 43% of Butterball's ground turkey — triple the USDA limit — yet no recall, no fine, no illnesses reported. ---BODY---

A male wild turkey. Federal inspection data published the day before Thanksgiving showed salmonella in just under 43% of ground and chopped turkey sampled at four Butterball plants over the past year. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

The day before Thanksgiving, with millions of Americans about to sit down to the biggest turkey dinner of the year, an investigation published on November 25, 2026, delivered a number that should stop every home cook in the country cold: federal inspectors found salmonella in just under 43 percent of the ground and chopped turkey they sampled at the slaughterhouses of Butterball, America's biggest turkey company, over the past year. At the company's largest plant, in Mount Olive, North Carolina — the biggest turkey slaughterhouse in the United States — the figure was 36 percent. The federal limit is 13.5 percent. And the consequence, so far, has been nothing at all: no recall, no fine, no product pulled from a single shelf.

The findings come from an analysis by SourceMaterial, an investigative outlet, of inspection data from the US Department of Agriculture's Food Safety and Inspection Service, known as FSIS, covering fiscal years 2025 and 2026. The data were provided by Slingshot Investigates, the investigative arm of the animal-rights group Project Slingshot, and verified by SourceMaterial. The analysis was published with the Guardian on Wednesday, November 25 — the day before Thanksgiving — and it is, by any measure, one of the most alarming food-safety stories of the year.

Here is what the numbers say. Across four Butterball slaughterhouses, just under 43 percent of ground and chopped turkey sampled over the past year contained salmonella. At Mount Olive, inspectors found salmonella in 16 of 45 ground and chopped turkey samples — 36 percent — against the USDA's 13.5 percent limit for an individual plant. Butterball's main rival, Jennie-O, ran far cleaner: 7 percent of its ground and chopped turkey samples tested positive over the same period. And the bacteria found at Butterball were not only common, they were hard to kill: 65 percent of the positive samples carried strains resistant to at least one antibiotic, against 61 percent at Jennie-O, and two-thirds of the samples taken from birds' intestines in fiscal 2026 that contained salmonella and campylobacter — another pathogen that can sicken humans — showed strains resistant to at least one antibiotic.

There is one piece of reassurance in the data, and it matters. No contamination was found in Butterball's whole birds, and none was found in its ready-to-eat, cooked products. The problem concentrates in the raw ground product — the turkey mince and chopped turkey sold for burgers, meatballs and stuffing. That distinction decides what a Thanksgiving shopper actually holds in their hands, because the bird on the table is not the product that failed the tests. But it is cold comfort for anyone who buys ground turkey, and it raises a question that goes to the heart of how America polices its food: how can a plant fail its federal standard by a factor of three, year after year, and face no penalty at all?

The standard that carries no punishment

To understand the story, you have to understand the rule — and the hole in it. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service set the current poultry standards in February 2016. For comminuted turkey — the technical term for ground or chopped turkey — the standard is no more than seven salmonella positives in 52 samples over a rolling 52-week window. Seven divided by 52 is 13.46 percent, rounded to 13.5 percent. A plant that goes over that line is placed in Category 3, the lowest performance tier, and posted on the FSIS website.

And then? Nothing. Exceeding the limit does not require a recall, does not trigger a fine, and does not force the plant to pull product from shelves. The plant must explain how it is addressing the problem, and that is, in practical terms, the whole of the consequence. As the LexBlog analysis of the findings put it in its headline: "Inspectors Found Salmonella in 43 Percent of Butterball's Ground Turkey. The Federal Standard Is 13.5 Percent, and Nothing Happens to a Plant That Fails It."

The reason lies in how American food-safety law treats salmonella in raw meat. Salmonella is not classed as an adulterant in raw poultry — that is, its mere presence does not make the product legally adulterated and subject to seizure — because raw meat is expected to be cooked, and thorough cooking kills the bacteria. The system relies, in the end, on two things: the plant's own testing and controls, and the home cook's thermometer. When the plant's controls fail, the last line of defence is the kitchen.

That is why the comparison with Jennie-O stings so much. Both companies operate under the same rules, the same standards, the same inspectors. One runs at 7 percent — comfortably inside the limit. The other runs at 43 percent — more than three times the limit, and more than six times its rival's rate. The difference is not the rule. It is the operation. Butterball's answer, through Alice Johnson, its senior vice president for food safety, is that FSIS should measure how much salmonella is in a sample, not whether it is there — an argument that the quantity of bacteria matters more than its presence. It is a serious scientific argument, and it is one that food-safety experts have debated for years. But it does not change the number on the spreadsheet, and it does not change the fact that the company's rate is three times the standard its rival meets with room to spare.

What salmonella does to the human body

Salmonella is the leading cause of foodborne illness in the United States, and its toll is enormous. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that salmonella bacteria cause about 1.35 million infections every year in America, along with about 26,500 hospitalizations and 420 deaths. Foodborne illness as a whole causes an estimated 48 million illnesses, 128,000 hospitalizations and 3,000 deaths a year, and salmonella alone accounts for more than a quarter of the hospitalizations.

The illness it causes, salmonellosis, typically begins between six hours and six days after infection. The symptoms are familiar and miserable: diarrhoea, fever, abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting and headache. For most healthy adults the illness lasts four to seven days and resolves without treatment. But for the people at highest risk — infants and young children, adults aged 65 and over, pregnant women, and people with weakened immune systems — it can be far more serious, spreading from the intestines into the bloodstream and, in rare cases, killing. The CDC's estimate of 420 deaths a year means that, on average, more than one American dies of salmonella every single day.

The antibiotic resistance in the Butterball data adds a further layer of worry. When the bacteria that cause an infection are resistant to antibiotics, the drugs doctors rely on to treat the most serious cases may not work. Two-thirds of the salmonella and campylobacter isolates taken from birds' intestines at Butterball's plants in fiscal 2026 were resistant to at least one antibiotic, and 65 percent of the positive ground-turkey samples carried resistant strains. That resistance, the data suggest, is largely a farm problem: it develops where antibiotics are used in raising the birds, and it travels with the bacteria from the farm to the slaughterhouse to the supermarket shelf. It is, in the words of one analysis of the findings, a reminder that what happens on the farm does not stay on the farm.

It is worth being precise about what the data do not show. No illnesses have been reported in connection with these findings, and no outbreak has been linked to Butterball ground turkey. Salmonella in a raw sample is not the same as salmonella in a person: the bacteria are killed by proper cooking, and the whole birds and cooked products tested clean. The story is not that people are being poisoned. The story is that the system designed to keep them safe is, at America's biggest turkey company, failing its own test — and that the failure carries no consequence.

Mount Olive: the biggest plant in the country

The Mount Olive plant, in Duplin County, North Carolina, is where the numbers are starkest. It is, according to the Guardian, the nation's biggest turkey slaughterhouse, processing 285,000 birds a week — roughly 15 million birds a year. Inspectors there found salmonella in 16 of 45 ground and chopped turkey samples, a rate of 36 percent against the USDA's 13.5 percent limit for an individual plant. Some reports of the data put the figure at 16 of 44 samples; either way, the rate is the same, and it is more than two and a half times the standard.

Mount Olive is not a small, struggling facility. It is the flagship of the biggest turkey company in America, in the heart of North Carolina's poultry country, and it supplies ground turkey to supermarkets across the country. The scale is what makes the numbers matter: a 36 percent positive rate at a plant processing 285,000 birds a week is not a marginal failure. It is a systemic one, repeated week after week, in the product that ends up in millions of American kitchens.

The four plants covered by the analysis are Butterball's slaughterhouses — the facilities where the birds are killed, processed and packed. The data cover fiscal years 2025 and 2026, and the ground and chopped turkey sampled over the past year. The picture they paint is consistent across the company's operations, not confined to a single bad plant or a single bad month. At every one of the four plants, the rate was well above the industry average, and well above the federal standard.

The day before Thanksgiving

The timing of the publication was not an accident. November 25 was the day before Thanksgiving, the day when tens of millions of Americans were buying, thawing and preparing their turkeys, and when the country's attention was, for once, fixed on the bird itself. This year, about 81.5 million Americans were travelling 50 miles or more for the holiday, and the American Farm Bureau's annual survey found that the cost of a classic Thanksgiving meal for ten was up 6 percent on last year — the first rise since 2022. The turkey, in other words, was already the story of the week. The salmonella findings landed directly on top of it.

That is also why the distinction between whole birds and ground turkey matters so much to consumers. The Thanksgiving turkey — the whole bird, roasted in the oven — is not the product that failed. The FSIS data found no contamination in Butterball's whole birds, and none in its cooked products. The risk, such as it is, sits with the ground turkey: the mince used for burgers, meatballs, sausage and stuffing, and the chopped turkey sold in trays. For the family carving a whole bird on Thursday, the findings are a worry about the system, not about the meal on the table. For the shopper who buys ground turkey every week, they are something more direct.

The advice from food-safety experts, in the meantime, is the advice that has always applied to raw poultry, and it is worth repeating because it is the only protection the system actually offers. Cook turkey — whole or ground — to an internal temperature of 165 degrees Fahrenheit, measured with a food thermometer in the thickest part. Do not wash raw turkey in the sink, because the water splashes bacteria across the kitchen. Keep raw turkey separate from ready-to-eat foods, wash hands and surfaces after handling it, and refrigerate leftovers promptly. Salmonella is killed by heat. The thermometer, not the label, is what stands between the bacteria and the dinner table.

A system built on trust — and its limits

The Butterball findings arrive in the middle of a long-running argument about how America should regulate salmonella in poultry. In 2026, FSIS rolled out a pilot project aimed at curbing salmonella in raw poultry slaughter and processing facilities, using approaches like biomapping — mapping where contamination enters the process — and validated critical control points within food safety plans. The idea is to find the places where bacteria get in and stop them there, rather than relying on end-of-line testing alone.

But the deeper question is the one the Butterball numbers force: should a plant that fails its standard face a real consequence? Consumer advocates have argued for years that salmonella should be declared an adulterant in raw poultry — that its presence, above a certain level, should make the product legally unsaleable, just as it is for some other pathogens in other foods. The industry has resisted, arguing that raw poultry is expected to be cooked, that testing for presence rather than quantity is a blunt instrument, and that declaring salmonella an adulterant would disrupt the supply of a staple food. Alice Johnson's argument — that FSIS should measure how much salmonella is in a sample, not whether it is there — sits squarely in that debate.

The counter-argument is written in the numbers themselves. Jennie-O, operating under the same rules, manages 7 percent. Butterball manages 43 percent. If the standard were meaningless, both companies would fail it at similar rates. They do not. The gap between them is the measure of what the standard can achieve when a company meets it — and of what its absence of consequences allows when a company does not.

There is also the question of transparency. The FSIS posts Category 3 plants on its website, and the data behind the Butterball findings came from the agency's own sampling programme — the routine checks that FSIS and some states conduct in commercial poultry plants. The information was there, in the agency's files, for anyone who asked. It took an animal-rights group, Project Slingshot, to obtain it, and an investigative outlet, SourceMaterial, to analyse it and publish it with the Guardian. The system did not hide the numbers. It simply did not act on them.

What happens now

As of Thursday, November 26, the FSIS recall and public health alert list showed no Butterball action, and its most recent entries were a November 22 cheeseburger recall and two November public health alerts unrelated to turkey. There is no recall to watch for, because exceeding the salmonella standard does not require one. What happens now depends, in practice, on three things: whether Butterball changes its own processes, whether FSIS tightens its approach, and whether consumers change what they buy.

Butterball has not, in the reporting so far, announced any change to its operations. Its position, through its food-safety chief, is that the measurement itself is the problem — that the agency should count bacteria rather than test for their presence. That is a debate about science, and it will continue. But the company's own rival has shown that the current standard can be met, and met comfortably, and that fact will be hard to argue away.

For FSIS, the findings are a test of a pilot programme and a standard that are both, in different ways, new. The 2026 pilot on biomapping and critical control points is designed to do what the standard alone has not: to drive contamination down at the plants where it is highest. Whether it works at a plant processing 285,000 birds a week is a question the coming year's data will answer.

For consumers, the practical advice is unchanged and unglamorous: cook the turkey properly, keep the kitchen clean, and use a thermometer. The whole bird on the Thanksgiving table tested clean, and a properly cooked turkey — whole or ground — is safe to eat. The scandal, if that is the word, is not in the kitchen. It is in the gap between a standard and a consequence: a federal limit of 13.5 percent, a company running at 43 percent, and a system that, when the numbers came in, did nothing at all. On the day before Thanksgiving, that is the story. And it is a story that will not go away, because the numbers are now public, and the next round of sampling is already under way.

The antibiotic resistance problem, explained

The most troubling number in the Butterball data may not be the 43 percent. It may be the 65 percent. That is the share of positive samples that carried salmonella strains resistant to at least one antibiotic — and it is the number that turns a food-safety story into a public-health story, because antibiotic resistance is what makes an ordinary infection dangerous.

Here is how it works. Antibiotics are the drugs doctors use to treat the most serious salmonella infections — the ones that spread beyond the gut, that strike the very young, the very old, and the immunocompromised, and that put roughly 26,500 Americans in hospital every year. When the bacteria causing an infection are resistant to those drugs, the treatment may fail, the illness may last longer, and the patient may need stronger, rarer and more expensive medicines — or may have no effective treatment at all. The CDC has warned for years that antibiotic-resistant foodborne bacteria are a growing threat, and the Butterball data show that threat sitting on supermarket shelves.

Where does the resistance come from? The data point to the farm. Two-thirds of the salmonella and campylobacter isolates taken from birds' intestines at Butterball's plants in fiscal 2026 were resistant to at least one antibiotic, and the same pattern held, at a slightly lower rate, at Jennie-O. The intestines are where the bacteria live in the bird, and the resistance they carry is, in large part, a product of how the birds were raised — including the use of antibiotics on farms, where the drugs are given to flocks to prevent disease and promote growth, and where they create exactly the conditions in which resistant bacteria thrive. The bacteria then travel with the bird from the farm to the slaughterhouse, from the slaughterhouse to the processing line, and from the processing line to the packet of ground turkey.

It is worth noting that Jennie-O's resistant share, at 61 percent, is barely lower than Butterball's 65 percent. The resistance problem is, in other words, an industry problem, not a single company's problem. What is specific to Butterball is the sheer volume of contamination: a resistant strain in 7 percent of samples is a risk; the same strain in 43 percent of samples is a risk multiplied sixfold. The combination — high prevalence and high resistance — is what makes the company's numbers stand out.

The argument over what to do about resistance is, like the argument over the standard itself, a long-running one. Public-health experts say the use of antibiotics in livestock should be sharply limited, and that the drugs most important to human medicine should be reserved for humans. The industry says antibiotics are needed to keep flocks healthy, and that restricting them would raise costs and reduce supply. In the meantime, the bacteria keep evolving, the resistance keeps spreading, and the data from Butterball's plants — two-thirds of intestinal isolates resistant to at least one drug — show how far the process has already gone.

Your checklist: what to do with the turkey you already bought

For the millions of Americans who already have a Butterball turkey in the fridge, or a packet of ground turkey in the freezer, the practical question is simpler than the policy debate: what should I do? The answer, from the USDA and from food-safety experts, is reassuringly concrete, and it starts with a distinction the data themselves draw.

First, the whole bird. The FSIS data found no salmonella in Butterball's whole birds, and none in its cooked products. If you bought a whole Butterball turkey for Thanksgiving, the findings do not say it is contaminated. Cook it properly — to an internal temperature of 165 degrees Fahrenheit, measured with a food thermometer in the thickest part of the breast, the innermost part of the thigh and the innermost part of the wing — and it is safe to eat. The same applies to the stuffing: if it is cooked inside the bird, it must also reach 165 degrees.

Second, the ground turkey. This is the product that failed the tests, and it deserves more care. If you have ground turkey in the fridge, check the FSIS recalls and public health alerts page before you use it — as of November 26, there was no Butterball recall, but the list is updated constantly and it is the first place any action would appear. When you cook it, use a food thermometer and make sure every part reaches 165 degrees; ground meat, unlike a steak, has bacteria mixed through it, so there is no rare or medium-rare. Wash your hands, the cutting board and the utensils after handling it, and keep it away from foods that will not be cooked.

Third, the kitchen itself. Do not wash raw turkey in the sink: the water does not clean the bird, it splashes bacteria up to three feet across the kitchen, onto counters, utensils and ready-to-eat foods. Thaw a frozen turkey in the refrigerator, not on the counter, allowing 24 hours for every four to five pounds. Refrigerate leftovers within two hours of cooking, and reheat them to 165 degrees. These are the rules the USDA publishes every year, and they are the rules that matter most when the product on the shelf has already failed its own test.

The broader lesson of the Butterball findings is not that Thanksgiving dinner is dangerous. It is that the safety of the food on the table depends, in the end, on the person cooking it — because the system that is supposed to catch problems before they reach the kitchen has, in this case, caught the problem, recorded it, published it, and done nothing about it. The thermometer in the drawer is the last line of defence, and this Thanksgiving, it is worth using.
