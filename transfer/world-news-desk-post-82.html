TITLE: Jannik Sinner Is Out of the Australian Open 2027 — Knee Surgery Ends His Melbourne Reign After 2 Straight Titles LABELS: Sports SEARCH DESCRIPTION: World No. 2 Jannik Sinner will miss the 2027 Australian Open after knee surgery in Finland, ending his two-year reign at Melbourne Park ---BODY---

Jannik Sinner in action at Roland Garros in 2026. The world No. 2 will miss the 2027 Australian Open in January after undergoing surgery on his right knee in Finland. Photo: Like tears in rain via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

Jannik Sinner will not defend his Australian Open title in January. The world No. 2, who has won the season-opening Grand Slam in each of the past two years, has been ruled out of the 2027 tournament as he recovers from surgery on his right knee, Australian Open officials confirmed on Thursday, November 26, 2026. It is the first time in three years that Melbourne Park will crown a men's champion who is not named Jannik Sinner — and it leaves the first Grand Slam of 2027 without its most dominant recent champion.

"We've been in regular touch with Jannik's team and they've confirmed he won't be joining us for the AO in January following his recent surgery," Tennis Australia chief executive Andrew Abdo said in a statement to the Associated Press. "Jannik loves competing here in Melbourne and is looking forward to returning for AO 2028."

The announcement, which came less than two months before the tournament is due to begin on January 17, 2027, ends weeks of uncertainty about the Italian's fitness. Sinner, 25, has not played a competitive match since winning the Wimbledon title in July. He shut down his 2026 season last month to undergo treatment for inflammation in his right knee — and on Tuesday, November 24, he revealed that the treatment had failed, and that he had undergone what he described as a "minor" operation at a clinic in Finland.

"Over the past few months, together with my team, we've tried everything to treat my injury without surgery," the five-time major winner said in announcing the operation on Tuesday. "After some very encouraging progress early on, my knee unfortunately didn't respond as we had all hoped, that's why today I underwent a minor surgery to improve the condition of my iliotibial band."

He did not give a timeline for his return to the tour. Experts have speculated that his absence could last three months — a period that would take him past the Australian Open and deep into the early hard-court season. For now, the only certainty is the one Tennis Australia has confirmed: Melbourne Park in January 2027 will go ahead without its two-time defending champion.

The knee that would not heal

Sinner's knee trouble did not begin this week. It has been building for months, through a season that, on paper, looked like a triumph. He won Wimbledon in July 2026, adding a fifth major title to a collection that already included two Australian Opens, and he did it as the world No. 2, the most consistent force in men's tennis. But behind the results, the knee was deteriorating.

When Sinner shut down his 2026 season last month, his management team issued a statement saying the 25-year-old Italian "will follow a course of treatment for inflammation in his knee." The wording was careful, and it was deliberate. It suggested a problem that could be managed — a course of treatment, a period of rest, a return in time for the new season. It did not suggest surgery. It did not suggest that the first Grand Slam of 2027 was in doubt.

For a while, the treatment seemed to be working. Sinner said on Tuesday that there had been "some very encouraging progress early on" — a phrase that suggests the conservative approach, the rest and the rehabilitation, was initially paying off. But the progress did not last. "My knee unfortunately didn't respond as we had all hoped," he said. The knee, in the end, needed a surgeon.

The operation, performed at a clinic in Finland on Tuesday, November 24, targeted the iliotibial band — a thick band of tissue that runs down the outside of the thigh, from the hip to the knee, and plays a crucial role in stabilising the joint during the explosive, twisting movements that define modern tennis. For a player whose game is built on speed, on sliding into wide forehands and on changing direction at full sprint, the iliotibial band is not a minor structure. It is, as the medical team overseeing his recovery in Finland will know, critical to the fluid, high-velocity movement that elite tennis demands.

Sinner called the operation "minor." That word, too, was careful. In surgical terms, a procedure on the iliotibial band is less invasive than a reconstruction of a ligament or a repair of a meniscus. But in tennis terms, there is no such thing as a minor knee operation — not for a player who has not played a competitive match since July, and not for a player whose season has already been ended once by the same knee.

A reign built in Melbourne

To understand what the Australian Open loses without Sinner, it helps to remember what he built there. He won the tournament in 2024, announcing himself as a Grand Slam champion and as the future of the sport. He won it again in 2025, confirming that the first title was not a one-off but the beginning of a reign. Two titles, two years, and a record at Melbourne Park that made him the player everyone else had to beat.

Melbourne Park suits Sinner. The hard courts are fast enough to reward his flat, penetrating groundstrokes and slow enough to let him construct points. The heat, which has broken so many players over the years, has never seemed to trouble him. And the tournament's place in the calendar — the first Grand Slam of the year, when every player arrives fresh and full of intent — has suited a player whose preparation is famously meticulous.

His absence in January 2027 will be felt in more than the draw. The Australian Open is, for many fans, the tournament that sets the tone for the season. The defending champion's first match, the questions about whether he can make it three in a row, the sense of a reign being tested — all of that is now gone. In its place is a vacuum, and a question: who wins a Grand Slam when the man who has owned it for two years is not there to defend it?

Sinner himself seems to have accepted the situation with the same calm that defines his public persona. He did not rail against the injury, or against the timing, or against the fact that the operation came when it did. He described the surgery, explained the reasoning, and left the timeline open. "Jannik loves competing here in Melbourne and is looking forward to returning for AO 2028," Abdo said, relaying the message from Sinner's team. The return, if it happens as planned, is more than a year away.

Five majors, and a season that stopped in July

Sinner is a five-time major winner, and at 25, he is still at the beginning of what many expected to be a long period of dominance. His 2026 season, before the knee intervened, was another chapter in that story. He won Wimbledon in July — his first title at the All England Club, and the fifth major of his career — and he did it as the world No. 2, the player against whom every other contender in the men's game is measured.

That Wimbledon title, won in July, is now the last match he has played. He has not competed since. The knee that carried him through the grass-court season, through the fortnight at the All England Club, was already failing. He shut down his season last month, missing the rest of the 2026 calendar, and he has now missed the start of the 2027 season as well.

The gap — from July 2026 to whenever he returns, perhaps April 2027 or later — is the longest absence of Sinner's career. It is also, in tennis terms, an eternity. The sport moves fast. Rankings shift. New contenders emerge. The players who were chasing Sinner in July will have had eight or nine months to close the gap, to win the titles he could not contest, and to build the confidence that comes from winning without him in the draw.

That is the risk of a long injury absence in tennis: it is not only the matches you miss, but the momentum you lose. Sinner's ranking, his seeding, his aura — all of them are built on results, and results require playing. A player who does not play does not accumulate points, does not defend titles, and does not remind his rivals why they fear him. By the time he returns, the tour will have moved on, and he will have to win his way back to the top of it.

None of this is new to Sinner. He has dealt with injuries before, and he has returned from them before. But the knee is different. It is the joint that carries every step, every slide, every change of direction. And the iliotibial band, the structure that was operated on in Finland, is the kind of injury that does not always heal cleanly, or quickly, or completely.

What the draw looks like without him

The immediate consequence of Sinner's withdrawal is a wide-open Australian Open. For two years, the tournament has been shaped by his presence: the draw, the seedings, the expectations. Without him, the path to the title runs through a field that is suddenly, dramatically more open.

Carlos Alcaraz, Sinner's great rival and the player with whom he has shared the sport's biggest stages for the past three years, becomes the clear favourite — if he is fit and in form. The two have defined the current era of men's tennis, trading Grand Slam titles and world No. 1 rankings, and their rivalry has been the sport's defining story. With Sinner absent, Alcaraz has the clearest run at a title that has eluded him in Melbourne.

But the absence of a defending champion does more than elevate the favourite. It opens the draw for everyone else: the contenders who have never won a major, the veterans chasing one last title, the young players for whom a deep run at a Grand Slam can change a career. A Grand Slam without its defending champion is a tournament in which the usual certainties do not apply, and in which a player ranked 20th or 30th in the world can look at the draw and see a path to the second week, and beyond.

That is what makes Sinner's absence so significant for the sport. It is not only that a great player is missing. It is that the tournament's entire shape changes. The storylines, the seedings, the expectations — all of them are rewritten. And the player who wins in January will know, whatever he says publicly, that he won a tournament the two-time defending champion did not contest.

Tennis Australia, for its part, has handled the announcement with the grace that the occasion requires. Abdo's statement was warm, and it was forward-looking: Sinner loves Melbourne, and he is looking forward to returning for the 2028 tournament. The message was clear: the door is open, the welcome is waiting, and the Australian Open will be there when Sinner is ready. But the 2027 tournament will go ahead without him, and the sport will have to get used to that.

The long road back

What happens next for Sinner is, by his own account, uncertain. He did not give a timeline for his return when he announced the surgery on Tuesday, and Tennis Australia did not offer one either. The experts who have speculated about a three-month absence are guessing — educated guessing, based on the nature of the procedure and the demands of the sport, but guessing nonetheless.

Three months from the operation, performed on November 24, would take Sinner to late February 2027 — past the Australian Open, past the early hard-court swing, and into the start of the clay-court season. That is the optimistic scenario. The pessimistic one is longer: a recovery that takes four months, or five, or six, and a return that comes not in the spring but in the summer, on the grass, a full year after his last match.

Either way, the road back is long, and it is not only physical. Sinner will need to rebuild his match fitness, his timing, his confidence — the things that cannot be rebuilt in a gym or on a practice court, but only in competition. His first tournament back, whenever it comes, will be a test of all of them. The knee will be tested. The movement will be tested. And the player who returns will need to prove, to himself as much as to anyone else, that he is still the player who won Wimbledon in July.

There is a precedent, of course. Tennis is full of players who have returned from long absences to win again — players whose careers were interrupted by injury and who came back stronger, or at least as strong. But there are also players who never fully returned, whose injuries changed them, whose bodies would not let them be what they were. Sinner, at 25, has time on his side. He has a team that has managed his career with care. And he has, in his own words, tried everything before choosing surgery. The operation was not a gamble. It was a decision, made after months of trying to avoid it.

The question, now, is whether the decision was the right one — and whether the knee, once operated on, will let him play the tennis that made him a five-time major champion. That question will not be answered in November, or in December, or in January. It will be answered on a tennis court, in a match, at some point in 2027, when Sinner slides into a wide forehand and finds out whether the knee holds.

Melbourne will wait — but it will not wait forever

The Australian Open has survived the absence of champions before. It has crowned winners in years when the favourite was injured, or suspended, or simply not there. It will do so again in January 2027. The tournament is bigger than any one player, and the sport is bigger than any one tournament. But the absence of Jannik Sinner will be felt — in the draw, in the crowds, in the storylines, and in the sense, shared by everyone who follows the sport, that the first Grand Slam of 2027 is missing something essential.

Sinner's record at Melbourne Park — two titles, two years, a reign that looked like it might last for a decade — is now a memory, and a target for whoever wins in January. The player who lifts the trophy on the final Sunday will be asked, inevitably, about the man who was not there. He will say, as players always do, that you can only beat who is in front of you. And he will be right. But the question will linger, and it will linger for a year, until Sinner returns — if he returns — for the 2028 tournament.

For Sinner himself, the wait is the hardest part. He is 25, he is a five-time major champion, and he is, by every measure, in the prime of his career. He should be preparing for Melbourne, for the defence of a title he has won twice, for the start of a season in which he would have been the player to beat. Instead, he is recovering from surgery in Finland, with no timeline, no return date, and no certainty about when he will play again.

That is the reality of a knee injury in professional tennis. It does not care about rankings, or titles, or reigns. It does not care that you have won the last two Australian Opens, or that you are the world No. 2, or that you are 25 years old and at the height of your powers. It cares only about whether the joint can withstand the demands you place on it — and Sinner's knee, after months of treatment, could not.

The surgery, in that sense, was not a defeat. It was an acceptance: an acceptance that the conservative treatment had failed, that the knee needed intervention, and that the only way back to the top of the sport was through an operating theatre in Finland. Sinner made that decision on Tuesday, November 24. On Thursday, November 26, Tennis Australia confirmed what it meant: no Australian Open in 2027.

Melbourne Park will go ahead without him. The tournament will be played, a champion will be crowned, and the season will move on. But the empty space where Jannik Sinner should have been — the defending champion's place in the draw, the first match on Rod Laver Arena, the question of whether he could make it three in a row — will be felt by everyone who watches. The Australian Open of 2027 will be a tournament defined, in part, by the player who was not there. And the player who was not there will be watching, recovering, and waiting for the day he can return to the court where he built his reign.

The bigger picture: a sport in transition

Sinner's absence is also a reminder of how fragile dominance is in modern tennis. The men's game, for two decades, was defined by three players — Federer, Nadal and Djokovic — whose reigns seemed unbreakable. Their absences, when they came, were events in themselves. Sinner and Alcaraz were supposed to be the next version of that story: two young players, sharing the biggest titles, defining an era.

But Sinner's knee has interrupted that story, and it has done so at a moment when the sport is already in transition. The old guard is gone. The new guard is two players. And when one of those two is absent, the sport feels the gap. The Australian Open of 2027 will be the first Grand Slam of the new era to be played without one of its two defining players — and it will be a test of whether the sport's depth, its next generation, is ready to fill the space.

That test will be watched closely, not only in Melbourne but around the world. The players who step into the vacuum — the contenders, the dark horses, the young stars — will have a chance to announce themselves on the biggest stage. And the player who wins will have a story to tell: the champion who won the tournament Sinner could not defend.

For Sinner, the story is different. It is a story of patience, of recovery, of a body that needs time. He has given no timeline, and he should not be pressed for one. The knee will heal when it heals. The return will come when it comes. And when it does, the sport will be waiting — because a five-time major champion, at 25, with two Australian Open titles to his name, is not a player the sport can afford to lose.

Until then, Melbourne will wait. The courts are ready. The crowds are ready. The tournament, on January 17, 2027, will begin without its defending champion. And Jannik Sinner, somewhere in Finland or in Italy, will be watching — and counting the days until he can return to the place where he built his reign, and where, in 2028, he hopes to build it again.
