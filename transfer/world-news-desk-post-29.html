TITLE: US Consumer Confidence Just Jumped 11.4 Points to 103.3 — Its Highest Level in Almost 2 Years LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: US consumer confidence jumped 11.4 points to 103.3 in November — its highest since January 2025 — as Americans grew more upbeat about jobs. ---BODY---

Times Square in New York City. American shoppers are feeling more confident about the economy than at any time in almost two years. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0).

American consumers are feeling better about the economy than they have in almost two years. The Conference Board, the research group that has measured the mood of the American shopper for decades, said on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, that its Consumer Confidence Index jumped 11.4 points in November to 103.3 — the highest reading since January 2025, the biggest one-month increase since May 2025, and a result that beat every forecast on Wall Street. The surge, the second monthly increase in a row, comes after confidence fell to its lowest level in more than a decade in September, and it arrives in the week of Thanksgiving, when more than 81 million Americans are travelling and the Christmas shopping season is about to begin. For retailers, for the White House and for the Federal Reserve, it is the most encouraging economic news in months — and a sign that the gloom that has hung over the American consumer all year may finally be lifting.

The numbers, released in the Conference Board's preliminary results for November, are striking in their breadth. The headline index rose from an upwardly revised 91.9 in October to 103.3 in November, a leap of 11.4 points that topped every estimate in a survey of economists — most of whom had expected a reading of around 92. The Present Situation Index, which measures how Americans feel about business and job conditions right now, rose 13.7 points to 132.8, its highest since July 2025. The Expectations Index, which measures how they feel about the next six months, climbed 9.9 points to 83.6, its highest since the end of 2024 — and, significantly, above the level of 80 that has historically signalled a recession ahead. The survey, conducted between November 1 and 17, found improvement in every part of the picture: current conditions, the short-term outlook for income, business and the labour market. As Fergus McCormick, a senior economist at the Conference Board, put it: "Consumer confidence rallied again in November, potentially due to lower uncertainty following the midterm elections. After two months of consecutive increases, the headline index is back to levels of confidence last seen in January 2025."

That last line is the key to the story. The midterm elections, held in early November, delivered a sweeping victory for the Democrats — who won 258 seats in the House of Representatives — and, according to the Conference Board, the result appears to have lifted the public mood. The sharp improvement in confidence was driven, the research group said, by respondents identifying as Democrats and Independents, who grew more optimistic about the economy and the job market after the vote. Whatever Americans think of the election result itself, the end of the campaign — and the reduction in political uncertainty that came with it — seems to have made them feel better about their own prospects. It is a reminder that consumer confidence is not only about wages and prices. It is also about whether people feel that the world around them is stable, and predictable, and moving in a direction they understand.

Jobs: plentiful again

The most important change in the survey is also the simplest: Americans think jobs are easier to find. In November, 30.8 per cent of respondents said jobs were "plentiful," up from 26.2 per cent in October — a sharp improvement in a single month. The share saying jobs were "hard to get" fell to 18.4 per cent. Those two numbers are the beating heart of consumer confidence, because for most Americans, the economy is not an abstraction. It is whether they can find work, keep work, and earn enough to pay the bills. When more than three in ten people say jobs are plentiful, it means that the labour market feels, to ordinary people, like a place of opportunity rather than of fear.

That feeling matters more than almost any other economic indicator, because it is what determines whether people spend. A consumer who believes jobs are plentiful is a consumer who will buy a new television, book a holiday, or take on a car payment. A consumer who fears losing their job will save, and wait, and hold back. The November survey suggests that the fear is receding. The Present Situation Index, at 132.8, is the measure of that feeling in the here and now — and it is the highest it has been since July 2025. The Expectations Index, at 83.6, is the measure of that feeling about the future — and its rise above 80, the threshold below which a recession has historically been signalled, is the first time it has crossed that line this year.

There is, however, a caution. A rival survey, run by the University of Michigan, registered a much slimmer improvement in November, and its headline index remains well below historical norms. The two surveys measure different things in different ways — the Conference Board asks about business and labour conditions, the University of Michigan asks about personal finances — and they have diverged before. The gap is a reminder that the recovery in confidence is real but uneven, and that not every American is feeling it. The Conference Board's own data shows that the improvement was driven disproportionately by Democrats and Independents, and that the mood of the country is still divided by politics, by income and by geography.

The September low — and the climb back

To understand how far confidence has come, it helps to remember where it was. In September 2026, the Consumer Confidence Index fell to its lowest level in more than a decade — a reading that reflected months of worry about prices, about jobs and about the direction of the economy. The summer had been difficult. Inflation, though lower than its peak, was still squeezing household budgets. The labour market, though still strong by historical standards, had begun to feel less secure. And the political atmosphere, with the midterm elections approaching, was charged with uncertainty. By September, the American consumer was, by the Conference Board's measure, as gloomy as they had been at any point since the spring of 2014.

The recovery since then has been swift. October brought the first increase, and November brought the second — and the biggest. The 11.4-point jump is the largest one-month increase since May 2025, and it has taken the index from its September low back to the level it stood at in January 2025, when Donald Trump began his second term as president. That is not a coincidence of timing. January 2025 was the last time Americans felt this good about the economy, and the return to that level, after almost two years of doubt, is itself a statement: the confidence that was lost has, at least for now, been found.

What changed? The Conference Board points to the midterm elections, and to the reduction in uncertainty that followed them. But there are other factors, too. Inflation expectations — what Americans think prices will do over the next 12 months — fell for a second consecutive month, to 5.7 per cent. That is still high by historical standards, and it means that a 100-dollar grocery bill today is expected to cost about 105 dollars and 70 cents in a year. But the direction matters: expectations are falling, not rising, and that gives people reason to believe that the worst of the price squeeze may be behind them. The stock market, too, has been strong — Wall Street recently had its best day in 19 years — and while most Americans do not own large amounts of stock, a rising market contributes to a general sense that the economy is moving in the right direction.

What it means for Thanksgiving and Christmas

The timing of the confidence surge could hardly be better for American retailers. The survey was released in the week of Thanksgiving, when a record 81.5 million Americans are travelling for the holiday, and days before Black Friday, the biggest shopping day of the year. Retailers have been watching consumer confidence all autumn, trying to judge whether Americans would spend freely this Christmas or hold back, and the November reading is the best news they could have hoped for. A confident consumer is a spending consumer, and a spending consumer is what the Christmas season depends on.

The early signs are encouraging. Black Friday sales have already begun, with discounts appearing earlier and deeper than in previous years, and the travel numbers suggest that Americans are willing to spend on experiences as well as on goods. The 81.5 million travellers this Thanksgiving week — a figure that includes both drivers and flyers — is a measure of a country that is moving, meeting and celebrating, and that is not holding back. The confidence survey suggests that the spending will follow. When people feel good about their jobs and their prospects, they buy presents. When they feel uncertain, they do not. This November, for the first time in almost two years, they feel good.

There are, of course, limits to what a single survey can tell us. Confidence is a mood, not a fact, and moods can change. The inflation that Americans expect — 5.7 per cent over the next year — is still far above the Federal Reserve's target of 2 per cent, and the prices that people pay every day, for food, for fuel, for housing, are still higher than they were a few years ago. A better mood does not put money in a pocket. As one analyst put it, confidence is not cash. The test of the November surge will come in the weeks ahead, when the Christmas shopping figures arrive, and when it becomes clear whether Americans spent as confidently as they answered a survey.

The Federal Reserve is watching

The confidence numbers also matter to the Federal Reserve, the central bank that sets interest rates, because consumer spending is the largest single component of the American economy — roughly two-thirds of everything the country produces is bought by its own people. When consumers are confident, they spend, and when they spend, the economy grows. When they are fearful, they save, and the economy slows. The Fed, which has been trying to bring inflation down without causing a recession, will read the November survey as evidence that the economy is stronger than it feared — and that, in turn, affects its decisions about interest rates.

A strong consumer makes the Fed's job both easier and harder. Easier, because a confident, spending public reduces the risk of a recession. Harder, because strong spending can keep prices high, and high prices are what the Fed has been fighting. The fall in inflation expectations to 5.7 per cent will reassure the central bank, but the level is still far above target, and the Fed will want to see it fall further before it can declare victory. The November survey, in other words, is good news for the economy, but it is not the end of the story. It is a chapter in a longer argument about whether the United States can have both low inflation and a strong consumer — and whether the confidence of November can survive the winter.

The politics of feeling better

There is a political dimension to the confidence surge that cannot be ignored, because the Conference Board itself has drawn attention to it. The improvement in November was driven by Democrats and Independents, and it followed an election in which the Democrats won a sweeping victory in the House of Representatives. That is not a coincidence. Consumer confidence has long been coloured by partisanship: people tend to feel better about the economy when their own party is doing well, and worse when it is not. The November survey is a textbook example of that pattern, and it is a reminder that the American economy is, in part, a story that Americans tell themselves about the country they live in.

That story has changed quickly. Two months ago, the American consumer was the gloomiest in more than a decade. Today, they are the most confident since January 2025. The facts of the economy — the jobs, the wages, the prices — have not changed nearly as much as the mood has. What changed was the election, and the sense that the political landscape had shifted. Whether that shift lasts, and whether the confidence it produced is built on something solid, will be tested in the months ahead. For now, though, the message of the November survey is clear: Americans feel better, and they feel better by a lot.

The question is what they will do with that feeling. The answer will come in the shopping figures of December, in the travel numbers of the holiday season, and in the next survey, when the Conference Board asks again. If the confidence holds, and if the spending follows, then November 2026 will be remembered as the month the American consumer came back. If it does not — if the mood fades, and the spending with it — then November will be remembered as a moment of relief, not of recovery. Either way, the numbers are worth celebrating for what they are: the biggest jump in confidence in a year and a half, the highest reading in almost two years, and a sign that, after a long and difficult year, Americans are starting to believe that things might be getting better.

How the index works — and why 103.3 matters

The Consumer Confidence Index is one of the most closely watched numbers in the American economy, and it is worth understanding what it actually measures. Each month, the Conference Board surveys a sample of American households, asking them how they feel about current business and labour conditions, and how they expect those conditions to change over the next six months. The answers are combined into an index in which a reading of 100 reflects the baseline set in 1985. A reading above 100 means Americans are, on balance, more confident than they were in that base year. A reading below 100 means they are less confident. The index is split into two parts: the Present Situation Index, which captures how people feel right now, and the Expectations Index, which captures how they feel about the future.

The Expectations Index is the part that economists watch most closely, because it has a record as an early warning system. When it falls below 80, it has historically signalled that a recession is on the way — not with certainty, but often enough that the threshold is treated as a line in the sand. In November, the Expectations Index rose to 83.6, its highest since the end of 2024, and the first time it has crossed above 80 this year. That crossing matters. It does not mean a recession has been avoided, and it does not mean one will not come. But it does mean that the measure which has warned of trouble in the past is, for the moment, pointing in a better direction.

The Present Situation Index tells a different, and in some ways more encouraging, story. At 132.8, up 13.7 points in a single month, it is the highest it has been since July 2025, and it measures something concrete: whether people can find work, whether businesses are hiring, whether the economy of today feels like a place of opportunity. The 30.8 per cent who said jobs were plentiful, against the 18.4 per cent who said they were hard to get, is the clearest expression of that feeling. It is also the number that matters most to the people who run the shops, restaurants and small businesses of America, because a customer who believes jobs are plentiful is a customer who will spend.

It is worth noting, too, that the November survey was conducted between November 1 and 17 — a window that covered the midterm elections and their immediate aftermath. The timing means the survey captures the mood of a country that had just voted, and that was reacting to the result. The Conference Board has said that the improvement was driven by Democrats and Independents, and the survey period explains why: the people whose views changed most were those who felt, after the election, that the political landscape had moved in their favour. That is not a criticism of the survey. It is a description of how consumer confidence works — as a measure of feeling, not only of fact.

The consumer is the economy

There is a reason economists care so much about a survey of feelings. Consumer spending accounts for roughly two-thirds of everything the American economy produces — more than investment, more than government spending, more than exports. When Americans spend, the economy grows. When they stop, it shrinks. The factories, the shops, the restaurants, the airlines and the hotels of the country all depend, in the end, on the willingness of ordinary people to open their wallets. That is why the Conference Board's survey is treated as a leading indicator: it tells you, before the spending figures arrive, whether the spending is likely to come.

The November survey, on that reading, is a green light. It says that the American consumer, after months of caution, is ready to spend again. The test will come in the hard data of the weeks ahead — the retail sales figures for November and December, the travel numbers, the reports from the shops themselves. If the spending matches the confidence, then the recovery is real, and the economy will end the year on a high. If it does not — if people told a survey they felt confident but kept their money in their pockets — then the survey will be remembered as a false dawn. The history of consumer confidence is full of both outcomes, and the only way to know which one November will be is to wait for the tills to ring.

For small businesses, the stakes are immediate. A local shop, a neighbourhood restaurant, a family-run hotel — these are the businesses that live or die on the Christmas season, and a confident customer is the difference between a good year and a bad one. The November survey gives them reason to hope, and to stock their shelves, and to hire the extra staff that the season demands. That, in turn, is how confidence becomes self-fulfilling: the expectation of a good Christmas leads businesses to hire, hiring leads to more jobs, more jobs lead to more confidence, and the circle turns. It is the virtuous circle that the American economy has relied on for generations, and it is the circle that the November survey suggests may be turning again.

What to watch next

Several things will determine whether the November surge lasts. The first is the Christmas shopping season itself. If sales are strong, confidence will be reinforced, and the virtuous circle — confidence leading to spending, spending leading to jobs, jobs leading to confidence — will continue. If sales disappoint, the mood could darken as quickly as it brightened. The second is inflation. If the price of food, fuel and housing continues to rise, the improvement in confidence will be undermined, no matter how plentiful jobs feel. The third is the labour market. The November survey shows that Americans believe jobs are plentiful. If that belief is confirmed by the employment figures in the months ahead, confidence will hold. If layoffs rise, it will not.

The fourth is politics. The midterm elections lifted confidence by reducing uncertainty, but the political calendar does not stop. The new Congress will take its seats in January, and the debates that follow — over spending, over taxes, over the direction of the country — will shape the mood of the consumer as surely as any economic statistic. The Conference Board's survey is, in the end, a measure of how Americans feel about the future, and the future is made of politics as much as of economics. November's reading tells us that, for the moment, Americans feel better about that future than they have in almost two years. That is no small thing. In a year that has given them little to celebrate, it is a reason — cautious, qualified, but real — for hope.
