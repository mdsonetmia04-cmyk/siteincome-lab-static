TITLE: Kim Jong Un Just Ordered Tactical Nuclear Weapons on Every Battlefield — and Called It "Irreversible" LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Kim Jong Un inspected new nuclear weapons factories and ordered tactical nuclear weapons deployed across every battlefield, calling it irreversible ---BODY---

North Korean leader Kim Jong Un in 2024. On November 25 he toured nuclear weapons facilities and ordered tactical nuclear weapons deployed across multiple domains. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Kim Jong Un has just made his intentions about nuclear weapons clearer than ever. On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the North Korean leader toured factories that produce nuclear weapons material, inspected halls full of what analysts believe are uranium-enrichment centrifuges and miniaturised warheads, and ordered his country to deploy tactical nuclear weapons — smaller, battlefield nuclear weapons — across every domain of its military. Then he said something that should worry every capital in the region: North Korea's status as a nuclear weapons state, he declared, is "absolutely inviolable", and any attempt to deny it would be regarded as "an open declaration of war".

North Korea's official Korean Central News Agency, KCNA, reported the visit on Thursday, November 26, publishing photographs of Kim walking between dense rows of tall machines that analysts identified as gas centrifuges, used to enrich uranium into weapons-grade material, and touring displays of what appeared to be standardised, miniaturised tactical nuclear warheads. The agency said Kim visited two nuclear materials production factories — one newly built and one being expanded — as well as a nuclear-powered equipment production base and a nuclear weapons research institute. It did not say where any of the facilities are located.

"Our nuclear capability is now a reality," KCNA quoted Kim as saying. He added that North Korea should "focus on utilising tactical nuclear weapons across multiple domains for different missions", and called the recent achievements in nuclear weapons production a major success. He said North Korea is "systematically and steadily expanding nuclear-material production capacity", and described the possession of nuclear weapons, and the trend of strengthening that capability "in quality and quantity", as an "irreversible reality".

The words matter, but the photographs may matter more. This was the fourth time Kim has publicly visited a nuclear facility — after trips in September 2024, January 2025 and June this year — but analysts who studied the new images said this one contained something they had not seen before. Alongside the centrifuges and warheads, KCNA showed Kim touring a facility that appears to produce equipment for a nuclear-powered submarine, a programme North Korea has never publicly displayed in this detail before. Ankit Panda, an analyst at the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, wrote that the images from the base, which he described as a naval reactor facility, were "a very notable disclosure". Shin Jong-woo, secretary-general of the Korea Defence and Security Forum, said the facility was believed to be producing submarine reactors and parts, and that the pictured equipment could be used for a nuclear-powered submarine planned for 2027.

If that reading is right, then Kim's tour was not just about the weapons North Korea already has. It was a preview of the weapons it wants next: a nuclear-powered submarine that could carry nuclear missiles far from North Korean shores, hidden under the sea, and essentially impossible to destroy in a first strike. That is a capability that only a handful of countries in the world possess, and it would transform North Korea from a regional nuclear threat into a much harder problem for the United States and its allies.

What are tactical nuclear weapons — and why does Kim want them everywhere?

The phrase at the centre of Kim's order — "tactical nuclear weapons" — needs explaining, because it describes a very different kind of weapon from the ones most people picture when they think of nuclear war. Strategic nuclear weapons are the big ones: long-range missiles designed to destroy cities and entire countries, the weapons that have kept the great powers in a state of mutual deterrence for 80 years. Tactical nuclear weapons are smaller, lower-yield warheads designed for use on a battlefield — against enemy troops, tanks, airfields and ships, rather than against cities.

That distinction matters enormously, and it is also what makes tactical weapons so dangerous. Because they are smaller, military planners are more tempted to think of them as usable — as just another weapon in the arsenal, rather than as a line that must never be crossed. Because they are designed for the battlefield, they are deployed closer to the front lines, in larger numbers, and under the control of field commanders rather than kept locked away under the direct control of a head of state. And because North Korea's rival, South Korea, sits just across one of the most heavily fortified borders on earth, tactical nuclear weapons deployed along that border would put a nuclear threat within minutes of Seoul, a city of nearly 10 million people.

Kim's order was explicit about this. He said the deployment of low-yield battlefield nuclear weapons across multiple domains for various missions is "crucial and urgent", given what KCNA described as North Korea's "inevitable reality facing the border with the most dangerous enemy state" — a reference to South Korea. North Korea has in recent years focused intensely on developing and deploying exactly these weapons, and the warhead at the centre of that effort is one the world has already seen: the Hwasan-31.

In 2023, KCNA published images of what it called the Hwasan-31 tactical nuclear warhead, a compact, standardised device that North Korea said could be fitted to a range of delivery systems, including short-range missiles. The photographs released on Thursday showed Kim and his officials inspecting objects that analysts said looked like miniaturised, standardised tactical nuclear warheads of exactly that type — suggesting that the Hwasan-31 has moved from a prototype shown to the cameras into a weapon being produced in quantity. "Systematically and steadily expanding nuclear-material production capacity" is, in plain language, a promise to make more of them, faster.

That is the core of the announcement: not a single new weapon, but a production line. Two factories — one new, one expanding — turning out the material and the warheads for a tactical nuclear arsenal, with orders from the top to put those weapons into service across the army, the navy and, if the submarine programme is real, under the sea as well.

Why now? The meeting that never happened

Kim's tour came days after a widely speculated surprise meeting with US President Donald Trump failed to materialise during Trump's visit to Asia last week. Trump had offered to meet Kim during the trip, and there was intense speculation in Seoul, Washington and Tokyo that the two leaders — who met three times during Trump's first term — might meet again. In the end, they did not. Kim ignored the overture, and the inspection of the nuclear facilities is widely read as his answer.

With dialogue with Washington having fallen through, Kim's visit to the nuclear facilities was seen by analysts as Pyongyang's way of making its terms for dialogue clearer: it will not discuss giving up its nuclear weapons, at any price, under any circumstances. The language Kim used leaves no room for doubt. "The validity and substantiality of our possession of nuclear weapons and the trend of bolstering up its nuclear capability in quality and quantity are an irreversible reality," he said, according to KCNA. "Our possession of nuclear weapons is an absolutely irreversible choice as long as malicious forces exist that use nuclear weapons as a means of achieving hegemony." And any attempt to deny North Korea's nuclear status, he added, would be regarded as "an open declaration of war".

That is a message aimed at more than Washington. It is aimed at Seoul, which has spent the year trying to restart dialogue with the North; at Beijing, which has pressed Pyongyang to return to negotiations; and at the United Nations, whose Security Council resolutions demand that North Korea abandon its nuclear programme entirely. Kim's answer to all of them is the same: the programme is not negotiable, it is not reversible, and it is expanding.

Kim justified the build-up by citing what he described as growing nuclear threats from the United States and its allies. KCNA said he described his latest inspection visit as part of efforts to strengthen North Korea's defence capabilities in response to "variable nuclear threats approaching the Korean Peninsula moment by moment". North Korea also said "the danger of using nukes is increasing day by day due to the enemies' moves to gradually facilitate the nuclear technology transfer and access to nuclear weapons within the so-called alliance" — a reference to the deepening military cooperation between the United States, South Korea and Japan.

South Korea's response was swift. Its government said that for the sake of peace on the Korean Peninsula and in the region, "North Korea must immediately cease its attempts to justify its nuclear development and comply with UN Security Council resolutions". But Seoul's statement, like the many that have preceded it, has no mechanism to enforce it. North Korea has ignored Security Council resolutions for two decades, and the resolutions themselves have been weakened by the divisions between the Council's permanent members, with Russia and China no longer willing to support new sanctions.

What the photographs reveal — centrifuges, warheads and a submarine reactor

The most revealing part of Thursday's KCNA report was not the words but the pictures, and analysts around the world spent the day studying them frame by frame. The photographs showed Kim and senior military officials walking between dense rows of what appeared to be centrifuges — tall, cylindrical machines that spin uranium gas at enormous speeds to separate the fissile isotope uranium-235 from the far more common uranium-238. Enriched uranium is the fuel of a nuclear weapon, and the number of centrifuges in a hall is a direct measure of how much of it a country can produce, and how fast.

North Korea has disclosed a uranium enrichment site only a few times since it showed one at its main Yongbyon nuclear complex to visiting American scholars in 2010. The new photographs appear to show a much larger and more modern facility — or possibly two of them, given that KCNA said Kim visited two production factories. The agency did not say where they are, and outside analysts have not been able to identify the location from the images alone. What they could see, however, was scale: hall after hall of machines, arranged in neat rows, suggesting an industrial operation rather than a laboratory.

The second set of images showed the warheads themselves — or, more precisely, what analysts believe are warheads. The objects on display were compact, standardised and numerous, consistent with the Hwasan-31 design North Korea unveiled in 2023. A standardised warhead matters because it can be fitted to many different delivery systems: short-range ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and potentially the torpedoes and underwater drones North Korea has also been developing. "Across multiple domains for different missions" is, in military terms, exactly that: the same warhead, on land, at sea and under the sea.

The third set of images was the most surprising. KCNA said Kim also toured a "nuclear-powered equipment production base", and the photographs from that site showed large, heavy industrial equipment that analysts said looked like components for a naval nuclear reactor. North Korea announced in 2023 that it was building a nuclear-powered submarine, and satellite imagery has shown construction work at its Sinpo shipyard, but this is the first time the country has publicly shown the industrial base behind the programme. If the equipment really is for a submarine reactor, and if the submarine is genuinely planned for 2027, as Shin Jong-woo suggested, then North Korea is about a year away from joining the small club of countries — the United States, Russia, China, Britain, France and India — that operate nuclear-powered submarines.

A nuclear-powered submarine does not need to surface for air, and it can stay submerged for months, travelling thousands of miles. Armed with nuclear missiles, it becomes the hardest weapon in the world to find and destroy, and the ultimate guarantee that a country can strike back even after suffering a devastating attack. For North Korea, which has always feared a decapitating first strike by the United States, that is the point: a submarine-based deterrent cannot be wiped out in a single blow, and so it makes the country's nuclear threat credible even against a far stronger enemy.

Six tests, one programme, no way back

North Korea has conducted six nuclear tests since 2006, with its last and most powerful test carried out in 2017 — an explosion that experts estimated at between 100 and 250 kilotons, many times more powerful than the bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Since then, the country has not tested another device, but it has not stopped building. It has tested dozens of missiles, including intercontinental ballistic missiles capable of reaching the United States, solid-fuelled missiles that can be launched with little warning, and hypersonic weapons designed to evade missile defences.

The tactical nuclear programme is the newest layer of that build-up, and in some ways the most dangerous one. Strategic weapons are, paradoxically, stabilising: they are so destructive that no rational leader would use them first. Tactical weapons, being smaller and designed for the battlefield, lower the threshold for use — and once any nuclear weapon is used, even a small one, there is no guarantee the conflict stays small. That is why Kim's order to deploy them "across multiple domains for different missions" has alarmed analysts more than a new missile test would have.

The order also fits a pattern. In recent years, North Korea has rewritten its constitution to enshrine its status as a nuclear weapons state, passed a law authorising the pre-emptive use of nuclear weapons, and unveiled a growing menagerie of delivery systems — from super-large multiple rocket launchers to underwater nuclear drones. Each step has been presented as defensive, a response to the threat from the United States and its allies. Each step has also made the programme harder to reverse, which is precisely what Kim now says openly: it is irreversible.

There is a domestic logic to all of this as well. The nuclear programme is, as one analysis put it, the backbone of Kim's authoritarian rule — the achievement he can point to as proof that his leadership has made North Korea a power that the world must take seriously, despite its poverty and isolation. The photographs of the centrifuge halls and the warheads are not just a message to Washington; they are a message to the North Korean people, and to the party and military elites whose support Kim depends on, that the programme is advancing and that their leader is in command of it.

What happens next

The immediate question is what the United States, South Korea and Japan will do. The three allies have responded to previous North Korean provocations with joint military exercises, with sanctions, and with statements of condemnation — and none of it has slowed the programme. The Trump administration, having failed to secure a meeting with Kim during its Asia trip, now faces the same dilemma its predecessors faced: North Korea will not negotiate away its nuclear weapons, and there is no military option that does not risk a catastrophic war on the Korean Peninsula.

One possibility is that Washington will try again, offering Kim a meeting with fewer preconditions, in the hope that personal diplomacy can achieve what pressure has not. Trump has shown, across both of his terms, that he believes in the power of a face-to-face meeting with Kim, and the fact that the two men have met three times before gives that approach a precedent, even if the previous meetings produced no lasting agreement. Another possibility is that the allies will focus on deterrence — more exercises, more missile defence, more visible demonstrations of the American nuclear umbrella over South Korea and Japan — and accept that North Korea's arsenal will keep growing in the meantime.

A third possibility, and the one that worries analysts most, is that North Korea will test something. The country has not conducted a nuclear test since 2017, and there have been repeated reports that its Punggye-ri test site has been prepared for a seventh test. A test of a tactical nuclear device — a smaller explosion, designed to prove that the Hwasan-31 works — would be the logical next step in the programme Kim has just described, and it would be a dramatic escalation. It would also be a test of the world's response: whether the Security Council can still agree on anything, whether China will restrain its neighbour, and whether the United States will answer with diplomacy or with force.

For the people of South Korea, the announcement lands as a reminder of a reality they live with every day: that the most dangerous border in the world is 35 miles from their capital, and that the man on the other side of it has just ordered nuclear weapons onto every battlefield he can reach. For the rest of the world, it is a reminder that the nuclear age is not over, that the number of countries with nuclear weapons is not shrinking, and that the weapons themselves are getting smaller, more numerous and more usable — which is to say, more dangerous — by the year.

Kim Jong Un's message on Thursday was, in the end, a simple one. His country's nuclear weapons are real, they are growing, they are going everywhere, and they are never going away. The photographs of the centrifuges and the warheads were the evidence. The words — "absolutely inviolable", "irreversible", "an open declaration of war" — were the warning. The world now has to decide what, if anything, it intends to do about it.
