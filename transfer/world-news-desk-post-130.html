Volodymyr Zelenskyy has finally said out loud what he has been holding back for months. In an interview with the BBC, broadcast on Friday, 27 November 2026, the Ukrainian president was asked how he felt when Donald Trump embraced Vladimir Putin at a summit in China and called the Russian leader "my friend." His answer was unsparing.

"Of course, it's up to the president of the United States, but… it's strange," Zelenskyy said, speaking in English from his office in Kyiv. "He is an aggressor and a killer. He can't be a friend. Just cannot be. It's the same that somebody would say about Hitler during the Second World War, that he's a good friend. No, he's not a friend."

It was the sharpest public rebuke Zelenskyy has delivered to an American president since the war began almost five years ago — and it landed on a day when the frustrations behind it were piling up on every front: paid-for Patriot missiles that have not arrived, a European Union that cannot agree on its next loan to Kyiv, and a night in which 155 Russian drones rained down on Ukrainian cities. The interview was not just about a hug. It was about a Ukrainian president who believes Washington is no longer pressing Moscow hard enough — and who decided, on camera, to say so.

The hug in Shenzhen

The moment Zelenskyy was reacting to happened on 19 November, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, China, where Trump held a brief bilateral meeting with Putin. As Trump entered the meeting room where Putin was waiting, he pointed at him, smiled, and said: "Who is that? He's my friend." Days later, Trump posted a photograph on his Truth Social platform of the pair shaking hands there, and titled it "My Friend."

It was the culmination of a week in which Trump repeatedly showed affection towards Putin — and towards Chinese President Xi Jinping — during a historic trilateral summit in Shenzhen. For Ukrainians, the images were painful in a way that is hard to overstate. Putin's forces launched the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. Since then, Moscow's invasion has killed hundreds of thousands of troops and thousands of Ukrainian civilians, and the International Criminal Court has issued an arrest warrant for Putin for war crimes, accusing him of personal responsibility for the abductions of children from Ukraine. Zelenskyy said in the interview that many people found Trump's greeting deeply strange, and that it was particularly painful for families who had lost their sons and daughters and were pinning their hopes on the American president.

"He can't be a friend," Zelenskyy told the BBC. "Just cannot be." He added that it was up to Trump what he said or posted — but the comparison he chose, to Adolf Hitler, was not accidental. It was the most loaded historical parallel available to a European leader, deployed deliberately, in English, on the BBC, to an audience that would understand exactly what it meant.

Trump's defence — and the Kremlin's answer

Trump, speaking to reporters at Mar-a-Lago on Thursday, defended his comments again. "We want to make a deal. We want to stop the killing," he said. "I could walk in and say, 'I don't like you!' and then we have the same problem that we have with Zelenskyy and Putin." He added: "The biggest problem with that settlement is the fact that you have two leaders that really hate each other."

It is a familiar argument from this president: that personal warmth is a negotiating tool, not an endorsement, and that flattery is the price of a deal. But Zelenskyy's interview made clear that Kyiv no longer accepts the premise. "It's not about comfortable talks; it's about a strong country that can pressure," he told the BBC interviewer. "We need more progress." His message, in substance, was that pressure on Moscow — not friendship with it — is what ends wars, and that Washington is not applying enough of it.

The Kremlin, for its part, seized on the interview within hours. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov said on Friday that Zelenskyy was "insulting his counterpart," and that the Ukrainian leader's words showed he "is not willing to follow the path of a peace settlement." It was a neat inversion: the man whose army invaded Ukraine presenting himself as the party of peace, and the leader of the invaded country as the obstacle to it. Peskov had called the ICC's arrest warrant for Putin "outrageous and unacceptable" back in 2023, and Russia considers the court's decisions "legally void." The Trump administration, meanwhile, has imposed sweeping sanctions on the court as part of its campaign to dismantle the tribunal over investigations into the United States and its allies, including Israel.

Zelenskyy's comments were broadcast a day after Jared Kushner, Trump's son-in-law and a US envoy trying to mediate an end to the war, complimented Putin on being a "grandmaster" of politics. It was weeks after Kushner's partner in the peace efforts, Steve Witkoff, described Putin as a "humanitarian." For Kyiv, the pattern is now unmistakable: the men negotiating on America's behalf keep praising the man who started the war, while the weapons Ukraine has already paid for sit undelivered.

The missiles that have not arrived

Behind the war of words is a war of logistics, and it is going badly for Ukraine. Zelenskyy complained in the interview that the Pentagon is not delivering to Ukraine paid-for Patriot missiles that could stop Russia's ballistic missiles — and that the delays are costing lives. As Russian attacks on Ukraine's energy infrastructure intensify ahead of winter, Ukraine is facing a serious shortage of interceptors, the missiles that shoot down incoming ballistic missiles before they hit power stations, hospitals and homes.

He also complained that SpaceX's military-grade version of the Starlink satellite internet service — the backbone of Ukraine's drone operations and battlefield communication — is not working properly. Together, the two complaints describe an army fighting a grinding war of attrition with its two most important lifelines, American air defence and American satellite communications, both faltering at the same time.

The timing could hardly be worse. The grinding aerial bombardment between Russia and Ukraine has escalated recently. According to the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project, or ACLED, which tracks violence, Russia launched close to 300 aerial attacks a week through 20 November on Ukraine's civilian infrastructure. Zelenskyy warned on Thursday that a major Russian attack is looming. Overnight on Friday, Ukrainian air defences intercepted 110 of 155 Russian drones launched at the country, including 51 jet-powered Shahed-type drones. Hits were recorded at 27 locations across nine Ukrainian regions, targeting railway infrastructure, energy facilities, civilian businesses and residential buildings, killing at least one person and wounding at least six, according to Ukrainian Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi.

Ukraine is hitting back. In a post on X on Friday, Zelenskyy said Ukraine had struck targets across a string of Russian regions during the week, including oil facilities, explosives-production sites, military airfields and aircraft. The Russian Defence Ministry said on Friday that its forces had overnight intercepted 318 Ukrainian drones over ten Russian regions and illegally annexed Crimea. Residents of St. Petersburg reported explosions in eight districts of the city, according to the Russian online news outlet Astra. Seventy apartments in a multi-storey building were damaged in a drone attack on the Black Sea port of Novorossiysk, local officials said. Officials reported that Ukraine's attacks killed at least two people and wounded six others.

It is a war of drones and missiles now, fought above the heads of civilians on both sides — and the side with more interceptors wins the winter. That is why the Patriot delays matter more than any diplomatic insult, and why Zelenskyy raised them in the same breath as the "my friend" remark.

Europe cannot agree on the money

If Washington is the problem, Europe was supposed to be the answer. It is not working out that way either. European Union leaders on Thursday failed to reach consensus after fraught talks over a new 49-billion-dollar loan for Ukraine's economic and military needs next year. The bloc's leaders ended an extraordinary summit in Brussels without agreement on the next long-term budget or on the additional financing for Kyiv, after more than twelve hours of negotiation. Twenty-four states backed the loan, but no final decision will come until December at the earliest.

Europe has in the past vowed to stay the course in helping Ukraine fend off Russia's invasion. But the failure in Brussels exposed the same fault lines that have been widening all year: governments that want to keep funding Kyiv, governments that want to spend the money at home, and a calendar full of elections over the next twelve months that could amplify the influence of parties reluctant to send more money to Ukraine and fray the continent's support still further.

For Zelenskyy, the European deadlock and the American drift are two halves of the same crisis. His country is fighting a war that has lasted almost five years, against an enemy with more men, more missiles and more money — and the two partners it depends on most are, simultaneously, arguing about whether to pay and complimenting the man who started it all. Small wonder, then, that the interview sounded less like diplomacy and more like a cry for help.

Nine children come home

Amid the bleakness, Friday brought one moment of uncomplicated good news. A bus pulled up at Ukraine's northern border in the Chernihiv region, and an 8-year-old boy ran out of it. "Mom!" he shouted into the crowd. His mother, Alina Sydorenko, was there to reunite with him after nearly five years apart. She hugged him tight as he kept saying, "Mother, mother."

The boy was one of nine Ukrainian children returned on Friday from Russia and Russian-occupied territories of Ukraine. "I want to show him everything that he missed and was taken away from him," Sydorenko said. Some of the children had lost their parents, who died in occupied territory. Relatives met them off the bus.

Zelenskyy announced the latest Bring Kids Back UA operation on social media, thanking US First Lady Melania Trump for her role in mediating recent wartime reunifications, which so far have brought 43 children home, the White House said on Wednesday. In all, Ukraine has so far managed to bring home 2,875 children, according to Zelenskyy. "We remember each and every one of them. We are doing everything we can to find them and return them to their families," he said.

The returns are a reminder of what the war is actually about, beneath the summits and the sanctions. Thousands of Ukrainian children were taken to Russia and Russian-occupied territory after the invasion — the abductions that form the basis of the ICC's arrest warrant for Putin. Each bus that comes back across the border is a small victory. Each one is also a measure of how many are still missing.

A war that keeps spreading

Zelenskyy's interview came in a week when the war showed new signs of spilling beyond Ukraine's borders. A recent Ukrainian strike on a Russian navy vessel docked in Libya raised alarm in Europe and Washington that the conflict could spread to other countries. In an apparent attempt to defuse tensions, Ukrainian Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said he held a phone call with Libyan Foreign Minister Al-Taher Al-Baour, informing him that Ukraine had no intention of drawing Libya into the war. Sybiha said in comments posted on X late on Thursday that he reaffirmed Kyiv's support for Libya's sovereignty and its efforts to reunify the country.

It was a diplomatic clean-up operation, but the underlying fact is hard to ignore: Ukraine is now striking Russian assets in the Mediterranean, thousands of kilometres from its own front lines, because that is where Russia's navy has gone. The war that began with tanks crossing a border in February 2022 now touches Libya, the Black Sea, the skies over St. Petersburg and the energy grids of nine Ukrainian regions in a single night.

More recently, Trump and Zelenskyy have clashed as Trump blamed Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries for high energy costs and threatened to cut off intelligence support, while Zelenskyy has said Trump's position is encouraging Russia to continue its attacks. It is a remarkable reversal of the dynamic of the war's early years, when Washington was Kyiv's most reliable backer and Moscow was diplomatically isolated in the West. Today, the American president calls the Russian leader his friend, his envoys call him a grandmaster and a humanitarian, and the Ukrainian president is left to explain, on the BBC, why that hurts.

A relationship that has never recovered

The unease between Trump and Zelenskyy is not new. Their relationship has long been difficult, most memorably breaking down publicly in February 2025, when the American president rebuked the Ukrainian leader in the Oval Office in front of the cameras. It never fully recovered. What is new is the openness with which Zelenskyy is now criticising the American approach — not in private diplomatic channels, but in a televised interview, in English, on the BBC, using the most provocative historical comparison in the European political vocabulary.

There is a calculation behind the bluntness. Zelenskyy knows that Trump's peace efforts are the only diplomatic process under way, and that alienating Washington entirely would be catastrophic for Ukraine. But he also knows that silence has not worked: the missiles have not arrived, the money has not been agreed, and the compliments for Putin keep coming. The interview was a gamble that public pressure — from a Ukrainian president speaking directly to Western audiences — might move what private diplomacy has not.

Whether it works will depend on what happens next in Washington. Trump has said he wants a deal, and that stopping the killing is his priority. Zelenskyy has said he wants the same thing — but on terms that involve pressuring Moscow, not befriending it. "We need more progress," he said. Until that progress arrives, the war grinds on: 155 drones a night, 27 impact sites, one dead, six wounded, and a president in Kyiv who has decided that the time for polite silence is over.

The hug in Shenzhen lasted a few seconds. Its consequences are still unfolding — in the skies over Ukraine, in the budget talks in Brussels, and in the widening gap between two leaders who, as Trump himself put it, really hate each other. Zelenskyy's message to the world was simple: a killer cannot be a friend. The question now is whether anyone in Washington, or in Moscow, is listening.

What the "my friend" moment reveals about Trump's peace strategy

The Shenzhen summit was not an accident, and the hug was not an improvisation. Trump travelled to China in November for a trilateral summit with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin — a meeting designed to show that the world's three most powerful leaders could sit down together and settle the world's biggest war. The optics were the point: handshakes, smiles, and a photograph titled "My Friend" posted to millions of followers. For Trump, the logic is consistent with everything he has said about diplomacy since returning to office: relationships open doors that ultimatums close, and a leader who will not talk to his counterpart cannot make a deal with him.

There is a case for that logic, and it is worth stating fairly. Trump has invited Putin to the G20 summit in Florida on 14–15 December, saying, "If it were up to me, I'd like to have him" — a move that would bring the Russian leader back into the room with the world's richest nations for the first time since the invasion. His envoy Jared Kushner has been shuttling between capitals trying to arrange the largest Russia–Ukraine prisoner swap of the war, with 1,200 to 1,300 prisoners expected to be exchanged on each side before Christmas. These are real diplomatic efforts, and they depend, to some degree, on Trump maintaining a working relationship with Moscow.

But the counter-argument, the one Zelenskyy made on the BBC, is that the relationship has become the policy — that warmth towards Putin has replaced pressure on him. When the American president's envoys call the Russian leader a "grandmaster" and a "humanitarian," when the American president posts photographs titled "My Friend," and when the missiles Ukraine has already paid for do not arrive, Kyiv hears a message that no amount of diplomatic spin can soften: that Washington now treats the aggressor and the victim as moral equals. Zelenskyy's Hitler comparison was his way of saying that some friendships are not neutral — that calling a killer a friend, in public, in wartime, is itself a political act with consequences.

The coming weeks will test which reading is right. The G20 invitation, the prisoner swap, the European loan decision in December, and the winter campaign on Ukraine's energy grid will all arrive within days of each other. If Trump's warmth produces a deal — a ceasefire, a swap, a settlement — then the hug in Shenzhen will be remembered as the opening move of a peace. If it produces nothing, and the drones keep falling on Ukrainian power stations while the interceptors run out, then Zelenskyy's interview will be remembered as the moment Kyiv stopped pretending that friendship with Moscow was a strategy for ending the war.
