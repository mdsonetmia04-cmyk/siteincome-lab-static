Joe Biden wrote a book about his presidency, and almost nobody bought it. "Promise Me, America," the former president's memoir, sold around 21,000 copies during its first week on sale, according to Circana, the firm that tracks the physical book sales market. The book went on sale on 17 November 2026, published by Little, Brown and Company, and the number landed like a thud across the publishing world on Friday, 27 November: 21,000 copies, for a former president, for a book that reportedly carried a $10 million advance.

And yet, on the evening of 25 November, Biden announced on X that the book was No. 1 on The New York Times bestseller list. "I am humbled and honored that PROMISE ME, AMERICA is #1 on the New York Times bestseller list, and that's because of you," he wrote. "Thank you to everyone who read it." He added that he wrote the book "to tell the story of what we did together." Both things are true at the same time: the book topped the most famous bestseller list in the world, and it sold a fraction of what his predecessors sold. That contradiction — a No. 1 bestseller that barely sold — is the story of the week in publishing, and it says something uncomfortable about Joe Biden, about the book business, and about what Americans want to read about their former presidents.

21,000 copies — and what that number really means

Twenty-one thousand copies is not a small number for most authors. For a debut novelist, it would be a triumph. For a former president of the United States, it is a flop by every historical measure. Circana tracks around 85 percent of the physical books market in the United States, so the true figure, including the remaining 15 percent of stores and some other channels, is somewhat higher — but not by enough to change the story. The book sold well enough to top The New York Times nonfiction bestseller list, but it did not approach the level of memoirs by such predecessors as Barack Obama and Bill Clinton.

The comparisons are brutal. Barack Obama's "A Promised Land," published in 2020, sold more than 800,000 copies within 24 hours of going on sale, and topped one million by the end of the week. Bill Clinton's "My Life," released in 2004, topped one million sales within its first two weeks. Biden's 21,000 in a full week is roughly what Obama sold in about 40 minutes. Put another way: for every copy of Biden's memoir sold in its first week, Obama's sold roughly 38 in its first day alone.

The gap is not only about Biden. Books by and about the Bidens have in general attracted less interest than books about other presidential families. Former First Lady Jill Biden's "View from the East Wing," published in June 2026, has sold around 46,000 copies in total, according to Circana — more than twice her husband's first-week figure, but still a modest number for a former first lady. By comparison, the White House memoirs by Michelle Obama and Hillary Clinton were both million sellers within a month of their release. The Biden family, for all its decades in public life, has never been a publishing phenomenon — and the numbers now prove it beyond doubt.

A $10 million advance — and the maths behind it

The sales figure stings more because of what the book reportedly cost. According to the New York Post, Biden earned a $10 million advance for his recollections — the money a publisher pays an author upfront, betting that sales will eventually earn it back. At a typical hardcover price, a publisher needs to sell hundreds of thousands of copies to recoup an advance of that size, once printing, distribution, marketing and the bookseller's share are accounted for. At 21,000 copies a week, the maths does not work. Even if sales continue at the same pace for a year — and first-week sales are almost always the strongest week a book will ever have — the book would sell barely a million copies, and the publisher would still be a long way from earning back its investment.

To be fair, advances for presidential memoirs have always been gambles, and publishers have always known it. Obama's advance was reported to be in the tens of millions, and his book repaid it many times over. Clinton's was reported at around $10 million to $12 million, and his book repaid it within weeks. George W. Bush's "Decision Points" sold well. Even Donald Trump's books have sold in large numbers. The bet on Biden was that a former president's account of his single term — and, above all, of his decision to step aside in 2024 — would be a book the country needed to read. The first week's sales suggest the country disagrees.

Little, Brown and Company, the publisher, released a statement saying it was "immensely proud" of the book topping The New York Times nonfiction bestseller list. It is a statement that says everything about how publishing works in 2026: the list ranking is the story now, not the sales. A No. 1 on the Times list is a marketing asset that can be printed on the paperback, quoted in advertisements and used to sell the book for years. The fact that the ranking was achieved with 21,000 copies — a figure that would not have made the list in most years — is a detail the publisher would rather not dwell on.

Why so few people bought it

There are several reasons, and none of them is a mystery. The first is promotion — or the lack of it. Sales may have suffered from the 84-year-old Biden's limited promotion for the book. He is battling stage 4 prostate cancer, and he has said that the illness will limit him from promoting the memoir. A presidential book tour is normally a machine: morning television, evening talk shows, bookstore signings, podcast interviews, a relentless schedule designed to put the author in front of millions of potential buyers. Biden has done almost none of it. Without the tour, the book had to sell on its reputation alone — and its reputation, among reviewers, was not strong.

The second reason is the reviews. "Promise Me, America," published last week, received lukewarm reviews. Critics found the book mostly unrevealing about Biden's time in office and about his decision in 2024 not to run again amid widespread concern about his age and health. A New York Times review was headlined "Reflecting on 2024, Joe Biden Finds No Faults of His Own." Professional reviewers also spotted factual inaccuracies in its 480 pages. The book drew fire for what critics called denial, with Gaza and racial-equity omissions flagged among its gaps. For a memoir whose entire selling point was supposed to be candour — the inside story of a presidency and the decision that ended it — the verdict that it reveals little and admits less was fatal.

The third reason is the hardest to say politely: the public has moved on. Biden left office nearly two years ago. The 2024 election, the decision to step aside, the questions about his age and health — all of it was litigated exhaustively, in real time, on television and social media, while it was happening. A memoir published in November 2026 arrives after the argument is over. Readers who wanted Biden's account of his presidency have already formed their opinions, and a 480-page book that finds no fault in its author is unlikely to change them.

There is also the question Biden himself raised. He has said he would not have run for a second term in 2024 if he had known he had cancer — a remark that reframes the most consequential decision of his presidency as a medical one, and that raises, rather than answers, the questions about what he and those around him knew, and when. It is the kind of revelation that should have been the centrepiece of a memoir. Instead, it emerged in interviews around the book, not in the book itself — another sign, critics say, of a memoir that holds back precisely where it should open up.

The No. 1 bestseller that barely sold

How does a book sell 21,000 copies and still top The New York Times bestseller list? The answer lies in how the list works — and in how much the book market has changed. The Times list ranks books. It does not publish how many copies any of them sold. In a strong week, the No. 1 nonfiction book might sell 100,000 copies or more. In a weak week, in late November, with the market fragmented across thousands of titles, 21,000 can be enough. The list measures relative performance, not absolute success — and this week, relatively speaking, a book that sold 21,000 copies was the best-selling nonfiction hardcover in America.

That is not a criticism of the Times, and it is not a trick. It is a reflection of a publishing market in which the audience for serious nonfiction has shrunk, and in which even a former president cannot command the readership his predecessors did. Obama sold 800,000 copies in a day in 2020, in a market that was already fragmenting. Six years later, the market has fragmented further, and Biden — older, sicker, less promoted, and less revealing — could not hold even a fraction of that audience.

The irony is that the No. 1 ranking is real, and it matters. Biden's announcement on X was not false: the book is No. 1 on the Times list, and for a former president who has spent his career being underestimated, that ranking is a genuine achievement. But it is an achievement that invites the obvious question: if the No. 1 nonfiction book in America sold 21,000 copies, what does that say about the other books on the list — and about the country that is not reading them?

Presidential memoirs: a history of huge numbers

The presidential memoir is one of the great commercial genres in American publishing, and its history is a history of enormous numbers. Ulysses S. Grant's memoirs, published in 1885, sold hundreds of thousands of copies and saved his family from ruin. Harry Truman's sold well. Dwight Eisenhower's sold well. Ronald Reagan's "An American Life" was a bestseller. Bill Clinton's "My Life" sold more than a million copies in two weeks and went on to sell millions more around the world. Barack Obama's "A Promised Land" sold more than 800,000 copies in its first 24 hours, topped a million in a week, and has since sold millions more in dozens of languages.

Even memoirs by losing candidates and first ladies have done better than Biden's. Hillary Clinton's "Living History" sold a million copies in its first month. Michelle Obama's "Becoming" sold more than 10 million copies worldwide and became one of the best-selling memoirs in history. Against that background, 21,000 copies in a week is not merely a disappointment. It is the weakest first-week performance by a modern former president's memoir that anyone in publishing can remember — and the contrast with Obama, Biden's own former running mate and the man whose administration he served in for eight years, is the comparison that will follow this book forever.

Part of the difference is generational. Obama was 59 when his memoir was published, a global celebrity with a massive social media following and a story — the first Black president — that the world wanted to read. Clinton was 58, a political phenomenon with a scandal-filled presidency that readers were hungry to hear about from the inside. Biden was 84 when his book was published, battling cancer, out of office for nearly two years, and writing about a presidency that ended in a decision to step aside that he still will not fully explain. The book's premise was reflection. The market wanted revelation. It got neither, and it stayed away.

What happens to the book now

The book is not dead. Presidential memoirs have long lives: they sell steadily for years, they are assigned in university courses, they are quoted in histories, and they are reissued in paperback with new introductions. The paperback edition of "Promise Me, America," when it arrives, will carry the words "No. 1 New York Times Bestseller" on its cover — and that label, honestly earned under the list's rules, will sell copies that the hardcover could not. The book may yet find its audience, slowly, among historians and students and the readers who buy presidential memoirs as a matter of habit.

But the first week is the verdict that matters in publishing, and the verdict is in: 21,000 copies, a $10 million advance, a No. 1 ranking built on a weak week, and reviews that found the book unrevealing about the very decisions it was supposed to explain. For Little, Brown, the advance is almost certainly a loss. For Biden, the book is a coda to a career that ended not with a triumph but with a withdrawal — and the sales suggest the country has already turned the page.

There is a final irony worth noting. Biden wrote the book, he said, "to tell the story of what we did together." It is a generous framing: the presidency as a collective effort, the book as a shared record. But a memoir is, in the end, a personal document, and its success depends on the person at its centre being willing to be personal — to admit fault, to reveal doubt, to tell the reader something they did not already know. The reviewers' verdict was that Biden did none of those things. The readers' verdict, delivered in the only language publishing understands, was 21,000 copies in a week. The book topped the list. The list, this time, was not enough.

The advance economy: why publishers keep paying millions

To understand how a publisher ends up paying $10 million for a book that sells 21,000 copies in a week, you have to understand the strange economy of the presidential memoir. Advances are not payments for books. They are bets on fame. A publisher pays a former president millions of dollars before a single word is written, because the publisher is not buying a manuscript — it is buying the right to publish the most famous person in the country, and the assumption that fame converts into sales.

Most of the time, the assumption holds. Obama's advance was repaid many times over. Clinton's was repaid in weeks. Even memoirs by presidents who left office under a cloud have sold well, because scandal sells and because the authors, whatever their faults, were willing to be candid about them. The system works because the author has something the public wants: the inside story, told by the only person who was in the room.

It fails when the author does not have that something — or will not give it. Biden's book, according to its reviewers, is a memoir that declines to memoir: it recounts events the public already knows, it finds no fault in its author, and it sidesteps the questions that made the book worth writing in the first place. A publisher can market a book, it can place it on the front table of every bookshop in America, and it can buy advertisements. What it cannot do is make readers buy a book that does not tell them anything new. The $10 million was a bet that Biden would be candid. The first week's sales are the market's answer to that bet.

There is a wider lesson here for the publishing industry, and it is not a comfortable one. The era of the guaranteed presidential bestseller may be ending. The audience that once bought a million copies of a former president's memoir in a fortnight is older, smaller, and more distracted than it was in 2004 or 2020. Younger readers get their political narratives from podcasts, documentaries and social media, not from 480-page hardcovers. A former president's memoir now competes not with other books, but with everything — and "Promise Me, America" shows what happens when it loses that competition.

What the flop means for Biden's legacy

Books are not the measure of a presidency, and it would be unfair to judge Joe Biden's four years in office by a single week of book sales. His presidency included the largest infrastructure investment in generations, the CHIPS Act, the withdrawal from Afghanistan, and the management of a pandemic's aftermath — achievements his supporters will argue about for decades, and that no sales figure can erase. A memoir's commercial failure does not rewrite the history books.

But memoirs are the measure of something else: how a president wants to be remembered, and whether the country is willing to listen. Biden's book was his chance to frame his own legacy — to explain the decision to step aside in 2024, to defend his record, to tell the story of his presidency in his own words, on his own terms. The reviews say he did not take that chance. The sales say the country noticed.

There is also a human dimension that the numbers obscure. Biden is 84 years old and battling stage 4 prostate cancer. He wrote this book while ill, promoted it while ill, and announced its No. 1 ranking while ill. Whatever one thinks of the book's contents, the effort itself deserves respect — and the fact that he still topped the Times list, even with 21,000 copies, is a reminder that his name still carries weight. The flop is commercial, not personal. But in publishing, the commercial verdict is the one that gets printed, and this week it was printed in numbers that no one at Little, Brown will want to remember: 21,000 copies, against 800,000 in a day for the man who was once his vice-presidential running mate's boss — and, in the end, his successor's predecessor.
