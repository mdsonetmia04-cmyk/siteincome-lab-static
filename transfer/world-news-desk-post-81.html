For a few days, Ariel Antonio Velasquez-Morales was the luckiest man in Massachusetts. He had walked into a small market in East Boston, spent $10 on a scratch ticket, and walked out a millionaire. The Massachusetts State Lottery announced his win on November 18, 2026, and a few days later it posted a photograph on Facebook showing him smiling gently as he gripped an oversized ceremonial check for $1 million. It was the kind of picture lotteries love: an ordinary person, an extraordinary stroke of luck, a life changed in an instant.

Then, on Tuesday, November 24, federal immigration officers arrested him in the same East Boston neighbourhood where he had bought the winning ticket. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, known as ICE, said Velasquez-Morales, a Salvadoran national, had been living in the United States illegally for more than a decade, and that an immigration judge had ordered him deported back in 2014. He now faces removal from the country — and the $1 million prize that made him briefly famous has become the centre of a national argument about luck, the law and what a lottery win is really worth.

"Looks like his luck ran out," Micah Bock, ICE's Assistant Director of Public Affairs, said in a statement announcing the arrest. The line was blunt, and it was deliberate. Within hours, the story had travelled around the world: a man wins $1 million, poses for the photograph, and is arrested days later by the same government that sold him the ticket.

A $10 ticket and 11 matching words

The ticket that changed Velasquez-Morales's life cost $10 and came from Sumner Market, a store near Maverick Square in East Boston. It was a "$2,000,000 50X Cashword" scratch ticket, a game in which players scratch cards that resemble crossword puzzles and try to reveal complete words. According to the Massachusetts State Lottery, it was the first puzzle Velasquez-Morales had ever scratched. He matched 11 words — enough to win the game's $1 million prize.

The lottery announced the win on November 18, and three days later, over the weekend, it shared the photograph of Velasquez-Morales holding the oversized check. The image was exactly what the lottery wanted: a winner, smiling, holding proof of his good fortune. What the lottery did not know — or did not say — was that the man in the photograph was wanted by ICE under a final deportation order that had been in place for more than 12 years.

Velasquez-Morales chose to take his winnings as a one-time cash payment, which would give him a lump sum of $650,000 before taxes, according to ICE. The alternative — taking the $1 million in annual instalments over many years — would have required him to remain reachable, and taxable, in the United States for decades. He chose the cash. It was a decision that, in hindsight, looks like the only practical one available to a man who was about to be taken into federal custody.

Four days after the lottery posted his photograph, the ICE Boston field office reposted the very same image on X, the social media platform, alongside an announcement of his arrest. The two posts, side by side, told the whole story in a single glance: the same smiling man, the same oversized check, two very different endings. The lottery's post celebrated a winner. ICE's post announced a detainee.

The arrest in East Boston

ICE agents arrested Velasquez-Morales early on Tuesday, November 24, in East Boston, the neighbourhood where he bought the winning ticket and where he had lived. Three ICE sources told Fox News that he ran when officers moved in to arrest him, but was quickly caught after a brief foot chase. Multiple officers' body cameras captured the encounter, the sources said.

The agency later issued a formal news release confirming the arrest and laying out its case. According to ICE, Velasquez-Morales entered the United States illegally in 2013, during the Obama administration, crossing the border in the Rio Grande Valley in Texas, near Roma. Border Patrol agents apprehended him, issued him a notice to appear before an immigration judge, and released him into the country with a list of free legal service providers — a standard practice at the time for migrants who were not considered enforcement priorities.

The following year, in April 2014, an immigration judge issued a final order of removal against him, according to ICE and to three agency sources who spoke to Fox News. That order meant that, in the eyes of the law, Velasquez-Morales had no legal right to remain in the United States — and had not had one for more than 12 years. ICE said he had been wanted under that final order ever since.

As of the evening of Wednesday, November 25, his name did not appear in ICE's online detainee locator database, and it was not immediately clear whether he had legal representation, according to USA Today. ICE said he will remain in custody pending his removal from the United States. The agency has not disclosed where he is being held.

Thirteen years in the shadows

Velasquez-Morales's story begins, in the official record at least, in 2013. That year, he crossed the U.S.-Mexico border illegally in the Rio Grande Valley, one of the busiest corridors for unauthorised crossings in the country. He was apprehended by Border Patrol agents near Roma, Texas, a small border town on the Rio Grande. Like many migrants apprehended at the time, he was issued a notice to appear before an immigration judge and released into the United States while his case was pending.

The release was not an act of generosity. It was a product of capacity: the immigration courts were backlogged, detention space was limited, and migrants who were not considered security threats were routinely released with instructions to appear for hearings at a later date. Velasquez-Morales was given a list of free legal service providers and sent on his way.

A year later, in April 2014, an immigration judge ordered him removed from the United States. What happened between the notice to appear and the removal order is not clear from the public record — whether he attended his hearings, whether he had a lawyer, whether he applied for any form of relief. What is clear is the outcome: a final order of removal, issued when Barack Obama was president, that remained unexecuted for more than a decade.

For 13 years, Velasquez-Morales lived in the United States — in Boston, in East Boston, in the neighbourhood where he eventually bought a $10 scratch ticket. He was, by ICE's account, an undocumented immigrant living under a final deportation order. He was also, by the Massachusetts Lottery's account, an ordinary customer who bought a ticket at a local market and won.

That is the tension at the heart of this story. For 13 years, the removal order sat in a file. For 13 years, Velasquez-Morales lived, worked and shopped in East Boston without being arrested. Then, in the space of six days, a lottery win made him visible — and visibility, in the current enforcement climate, has consequences.

What happens to the $650,000?

The most immediate question — after the shock of the arrest itself — is what happens to the money. Velasquez-Morales chose the lump-sum cash option of $650,000 before taxes. The prize was awarded to him on November 18, and the lottery's announcement made the win a matter of public record. But whether he will ever receive the money, and in what form, is now uncertain.

Massachusetts law does not bar undocumented immigrants from buying lottery tickets or claiming prizes. The Massachusetts State Lottery says anyone 18 or older can buy a ticket from a licensed retailer, and the rules of the game do not ask about immigration status. The lottery is, in that sense, blind: it sells tickets to whoever walks into a store with $10, and it pays whoever holds the winning ticket.

But claiming a prize is a different matter from buying a ticket. Winners must present identification, complete tax forms and, in the case of a lump-sum payment, provide the information the state needs to withhold federal and state taxes. The process takes time. In Velasquez-Morales's case, the arrest came six days after the prize was announced — and it is not clear from public reporting whether the lump-sum payment had actually been processed and transferred before he was taken into custody.

If the money has not yet been paid, his detention complicates everything. A person in ICE custody pending removal cannot easily complete the paperwork, attend the appointments or manage the bank transfers that a $650,000 payment requires. If the money has been paid, it may be sitting in a bank account he cannot reach, or it may be subject to claims, fees or legal costs. And if he is deported to El Salvador, the practical question of how — or whether — he can access funds held in the United States becomes a legal and logistical maze.

There is also the question of taxes. Lottery winnings in the United States are subject to federal income tax, and Massachusetts withholds state tax as well. The $650,000 lump sum is a pre-tax figure; the amount that would actually reach a winner's bank account would be substantially less after federal withholding of 24 percent and Massachusetts state tax. For a winner facing deportation, the tax question is not academic: the Internal Revenue Service does not waive its claim because the winner is in custody.

None of this has been resolved publicly. ICE's statement did not address the prize money. The Massachusetts Lottery has not said publicly whether the payment was completed before the arrest. And Velasquez-Morales, in custody, has not spoken to reporters. The $650,000 — the money that made him briefly the most famous lottery winner in America — is, for now, in limbo.

The photograph that gave him away

The detail that has captured the public imagination is the photograph. The Massachusetts State Lottery posted it on Facebook on a Saturday, three days after announcing the win. It showed Velasquez-Morales smiling gently, holding an oversized check for $1 million. It was a routine piece of lottery publicity — the kind of image state lotteries post every week, celebrating winners and encouraging others to play.

Four days later, ICE reposted the same photograph on X with its own announcement. The agency did not need to describe the man it had arrested; the lottery had already provided a clear, recent, high-quality photograph of him, smiling, holding a check with his name on it. The image that was meant to celebrate his luck became, in effect, a wanted poster.

Whether ICE found Velasquez-Morales because of the photograph is not clear from the public record. The agency said he had been wanted under a final removal order since 2014, and it is possible — even likely — that agents already knew where he lived and were planning an arrest. But the timing is striking: the lottery announced the win on November 18, posted the photograph on November 21, and ICE arrested him on November 24. The photograph, at the very least, made the arrest easy to publicise. ICE's decision to repost it was a deliberate piece of messaging: the agency wanted the public to see the before and after.

The episode has also raised an uncomfortable question for state lotteries across the country. Lottery winners are, by law and by custom, publicised. Many states require winners to be identified by name; others allow anonymity, but Massachusetts is not one of them. The publicity is part of the business model: it proves the games are real, it creates the dream that sells the next ticket, and it generates the news coverage that keeps the lottery in the public eye.

But for a winner who is undocumented, that publicity is a risk. A photograph on Facebook, a name in a press release, a ceremony with an oversized check — each of these is a public record, and each of them is visible to the same federal agencies that enforce immigration law. Velasquez-Morales's case is an extreme example, but it is not a hypothetical one. It happened, in six days, in the most public way possible.

Lottery officials have not said whether they will change their publicity practices. The Massachusetts State Lottery's rules are set by state law, and the commission that oversees it — two of whose five members are appointed by the governor — has not commented on the case. For now, the system works as it always has: winners are named, photographed and celebrated. The difference is that everyone now knows what can happen next.

An enforcement climate that has changed

Velasquez-Morales's arrest did not happen in a vacuum. It happened in the middle of the most aggressive immigration enforcement campaign in modern American history. Since returning to the White House, President Donald Trump has made the removal of undocumented immigrants a central priority of his administration, directing ICE to pursue not only recent arrivals and people with criminal records, but also long-term residents with old removal orders — the category into which Velasquez-Morales falls.

The numbers tell the story. ICE arrests have surged in 2026, with agents conducting workplace raids, home arrests and street operations in cities across the country. The administration has expanded the categories of people considered enforcement priorities, rescinded the previous administration's guidance that limited arrests in sensitive locations, and pushed for the rapid execution of old removal orders — orders like the one issued against Velasquez-Morales in April 2014.

In that context, his arrest is not an anomaly. It is the system working as the administration intends it to work: an old removal order, a known address, an arrest. What makes his case different is the lottery win — the $1 million, the photograph, the six-day gap between celebration and custody. Without the win, his arrest would have been one of thousands, noticed by almost no one. With it, it became a story that travelled around the world.

The administration's supporters have welcomed the arrest as proof that the law applies to everyone, including lottery winners. They point to the 2014 removal order and argue that Velasquez-Morales had 12 years to leave voluntarily and did not. They note that he entered the country illegally, that a judge ordered him removed, and that the law is the law regardless of how much money a person wins on a scratch ticket.

Critics of the arrest have taken a different view. They argue that a man who lived in the United States for 13 years, who bought a lottery ticket at a local market like any other resident, and who harmed no one, should not be the target of a high-profile enforcement operation. They point to the photograph — the lottery's celebration turned into ICE's announcement — and see in it a cruelty that goes beyond the law. And they ask a practical question: what does it achieve, beyond the headline, to deport a man who has just won $1 million?

That argument is unlikely to change the outcome. The removal order is final, the arrest has been made, and ICE has said Velasquez-Morales will remain in custody pending removal. The debate will continue, but the legal process is, for now, straightforward.

A $10 ticket, a 13-year wait, and a question with no easy answer

There is a temptation to treat Velasquez-Morales's story as a parable — a tale of luck and its limits, of a man who won the lottery and lost everything. But the story resists a simple moral. He did break the law: he entered the United States illegally in 2013, and a judge ordered him removed in 2014. He also lived in the country for 13 years, bought a ticket at a neighbourhood market, and won a prize that the state was happy to publicise.

The lottery, for its part, did nothing wrong. It sold a ticket, it announced a winner, it posted a photograph. That is what lotteries do. ICE, for its part, did what the law required it to do: it executed a final removal order that had been outstanding for more than a decade. That is what ICE does. The collision between the two — the celebration and the arrest, six days apart — is what makes the story so striking.

For the people of East Boston, the story is local before it is national. Sumner Market, near Maverick Square, is a neighbourhood store. The $10 ticket was bought there, by a man who lived nearby. The arrest happened there, too. The neighbourhood that sold him the ticket watched him win, and then watched him be taken away. The oversized check, the photograph, the foot chase — all of it happened within a few blocks of where he bought the ticket.

And for the millions of Americans who play the lottery every week, the story carries a quieter message. The lottery is a game of chance, and the odds of winning $1 million on a $10 scratch ticket are vanishingly small. Velasquez-Morales beat those odds. He matched 11 words on the first puzzle he ever scratched. He won $1 million. And then, six days later, he was arrested. The luck that made him a millionaire did not, in the end, change his legal status. It only made him visible.

Whether he will ever see the $650,000 remains to be seen. Whether he will be deported to El Salvador, a country he left more than 13 years ago, is a matter for the immigration courts and for ICE. What is certain is that the photograph — the smiling man, the oversized check, the $1 million prize — will outlast both the win and the arrest. It is, for now, the last public image of Ariel Antonio Velasquez-Morales: a lottery winner, holding his prize, six days before his luck ran out.
