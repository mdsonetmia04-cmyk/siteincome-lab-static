TITLE: Black Friday 2026 Is Here Early — and Thanksgiving Gas Just Hit an 8-Month Low LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Black Friday 2026 sales started early as Thanksgiving gas fell to an 8-month low of $3.62 a gallon, while 81.5 million Americans hit the road. ---BODY---

Shoppers rush into a store on Black Friday. Photo: Powhusku / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0).

Black Friday 2026 has arrived early, and it has brought an unexpected gift with it: the cheapest Thanksgiving gasoline in eight months. Major retailers across the United States have already launched their Black Friday sales, days before the traditional Friday rush, as the biggest shopping week of the year collides with the busiest travel week of the year. Gas prices have fallen to a national average of $3.62 a gallon on Thanksgiving Day — down about 20 cents in a single week, and the lowest the country has seen since the spring. And 81.5 million Americans are on the move, driving and flying to family dinners, to shopping malls, and to the deals that have already begun.

It is a strange and welcome convergence. For years, Thanksgiving week has meant two things at once: the most expensive travel of the year, and the most aggressive shopping of the year. This year, the travel is cheaper than it has been in months, the shopping started earlier than ever, and the economy is giving consumers a rare piece of good news at exactly the moment they need it most. Here is what is happening at the pump, in the stores, and on the roads — and what it means for the holiday season ahead.

At the pump: $3.62 and falling

The headline number is $3.62. That is the national average price of a gallon of regular gasoline predicted for Thanksgiving Day by GasBuddy, the fuel-price tracking service — and it represents an eight-month low. Other reports put the figure in the same range, at $3.63 to $3.69 a gallon, down roughly 20 cents in a week. For a country that has spent the last several years watching fuel prices climb, the drop is a genuine relief, and it arrives at the moment when more Americans are driving than at any other time of the year.

The decline has been swift. Gas prices have fallen about 20 cents in a single week, driven by lower crude oil prices, by seasonal demand patterns, and by the easing of the refinery constraints that pushed prices higher in the autumn. The national average is now more than 50 cents lower than it was at this time last year — a difference that adds up quickly for a family driving hundreds of miles to Thanksgiving dinner. A 400-mile round trip in a car that gets 25 miles per gallon uses 16 gallons of fuel. At $3.62 a gallon, that trip costs about $58. At last year's prices, it would have cost more than $66. The savings are modest in absolute terms, but they are real, and they come at a time when every dollar matters to households squeezed by years of inflation.

The picture is not uniform across the country. Gas prices vary by state, by region, and by station, and some parts of the country are paying considerably more than the national average. California, Hawaii, and the Pacific Northwest remain the most expensive markets, while the Gulf Coast and the Southeast are the cheapest. But the direction of travel is the same almost everywhere: down, and down fast. For the 81.5 million Americans traveling this week — most of them by car — the falling prices are the first piece of good news in a holiday season that has been defined by rising costs.

On the road: 81.5 million travelers

The American Automobile Association expects 81.5 million Americans to travel this Thanksgiving week, one of the largest holiday travel volumes in the country's history. The vast majority of those travelers — tens of millions of them — will drive, and they will do so on roads that are already crowded, at gas stations that are already busy, and in weather that is, in much of the country, turning cold.

The scale of the movement is extraordinary. Eighty-one and a half million people is roughly a quarter of the entire population of the United States, all traveling in the same few days, all heading to the same family tables. The highways will be packed. The airports will be packed. And the systems that move all of those people — the airlines, the highways, the fuel stations, the rest stops — will be tested in ways they are tested only a few times a year.

That test has already produced its first failure. Delta Air Lines suffered an automation failure on Sunday — a problem with the weight-and-balance system that calculates how an aircraft's load is distributed — that delayed flights out of Atlanta, the airline's largest hub and the busiest airport in the world. The failure was resolved, but it was a reminder of how fragile the travel system is, and how quickly a single technical problem can ripple through thousands of flights and hundreds of thousands of passengers.

The weather is adding its own complications. King tides — exceptionally high tides caused by the alignment of the moon and the sun — are hitting the Pacific Coast, with water levels running up to 2.8 feet above normal and a 30-foot surf warning in effect. Washington State Ferries, which carry hundreds of thousands of passengers across Puget Sound, have canceled sailings, affecting an estimated 300,000 travelers. In South Florida, coastal flooding is underway. And across the country, the first cold snap of the winter is settling in, bringing with it the rain, snow, and ice that make holiday driving dangerous.

For the travelers themselves, the advice is the same as it is every year: leave early, expect delays, and be patient. For the system as a whole, the week is a stress test — and the results will tell us a great deal about how well the country's transportation infrastructure is holding up.

In the stores: Black Friday came early

While the travelers are on the road, the shoppers are already in the stores — because Black Friday, this year, started before Friday. Major retailers across the country have launched their Black Friday sales early, with deals going live days ahead of the traditional post-Thanksgiving rush. It is a pattern that has been building for years, as retailers stretch the shopping season earlier and earlier in a bid to capture spending before their competitors do, and this year the early start is more pronounced than ever.

The deals themselves are the familiar mix of the holiday season: electronics, appliances, clothing, and toys, discounted by amounts that range from modest to dramatic. Robot vacuums are among the most heavily promoted items this year, with Roomba models starting at $149.99 and the Roborock Q7 M5+ discounted by $160 — the kind of deep cuts that define Black Friday's appeal. Televisions, laptops, and smartphones are discounted across the board. And the retailers, from the big-box giants to the online marketplaces, are competing on price, on shipping, and on the promise that the deals will not last.

The early start reflects a deeper change in how Americans shop. Black Friday was once a single day — the Friday after Thanksgiving, when shoppers lined up before dawn outside stores and rushed the doors when they opened. That image, of crowds surging into a store at 5 a.m., is the one that defines the holiday in the popular imagination. But the reality has changed. Online shopping now accounts for a large and growing share of holiday spending, and the deals that once lasted a single morning now stretch across a week or more. The stores still open early, and the crowds still come, but the center of gravity has shifted — from the doorbuster to the website, from the single day to the season.

For retailers, the stakes are enormous. The holiday season — the weeks between Thanksgiving and Christmas — accounts for a large share of annual retail sales, and Black Friday week is the peak of that season. A strong Black Friday can make a retailer's year. A weak one can break it. This year, with consumer confidence uncertain and inflation still weighing on household budgets, the retailers are pulling out every stop: early sales, deep discounts, free shipping, and buy-now-pay-later options designed to make the spending feel easier than it is.

The economics of a holiday

The convergence of cheap gas and early sales is happening against a backdrop of economic uncertainty that makes this holiday season unlike any in recent memory. Inflation has eased from its peak, but prices remain far above where they were a few years ago, and households are still adjusting to the higher cost of living. Wages have risen, but for many families, the gains have not kept pace with the cost of food, housing, and transportation. Consumer confidence is fragile, and the holiday season — the moment when Americans spend the most — is the test of whether that confidence will hold.

The gas price drop is a small but meaningful boost. Lower fuel prices put money directly back into consumers' pockets, and they do so at the moment when consumers are spending the most. The 20-cent drop in a single week is worth roughly $8 billion a year if it holds — money that would otherwise go to fuel stations and that can now go to gifts, to food, and to the other spending that defines the holidays. It is not a transformation of the economy. But it is a tailwind, and it arrives at exactly the right time.

The early Black Friday sales are a different kind of signal. Retailers do not start their biggest sales early when they are confident that shoppers will come on their own. They start early when they are worried — when they fear that demand is soft, that consumers are cautious, and that the spending they need will not materialize without a push. The early start is a bet that discounts will bring shoppers out, and it is also an admission that the retailers are not certain the shoppers will come otherwise.

What the holiday season will show, in the end, is whether the American consumer — the engine of the world's largest economy — is still spending, still confident, and still willing to open their wallet for the holidays. The early data will come in the days after Black Friday, when retailers report their sales and analysts tally the results. Until then, the only certainty is that the stores are open, the deals are live, and the shoppers are deciding.

What to watch this week

For anyone following the holiday economy, there are three numbers to watch in the coming days. The first is the gas price — whether the $3.62 average holds, falls further, or rebounds as Thanksgiving demand peaks. The second is the Black Friday sales figures — the total spending, the split between online and in-store, and the categories that sold best. And the third is the travel data — whether the 81.5 million travelers made it to their destinations, and whether the system held up under the strain.

There are also the human stories that the numbers do not capture. The family driving 400 miles on $58 of gas. The shopper who found the robot vacuum at $149.99. The traveler whose flight was delayed in Atlanta, and who made it home anyway. The retailer who opened early and sold out by noon. These are the stories that make the holiday season what it is — not a set of statistics, but a week in which millions of people, all at once, try to get home, and try to give something to the people they love.

That is the real meaning of Black Friday and Thanksgiving week. It is not about the deals, or the gas prices, or the travel numbers. It is about the fact that, once a year, the country moves — all at once, in the same direction, toward the same tables — and that the economy, for all its complexity, exists to make that movement possible. This year, the movement is cheaper than it has been in eight months, the deals started earlier than ever, and the season is underway. The only question left is how it ends.

The history of Black Friday: from Philadelphia to the world

Black Friday was not always a shopping holiday. It was, originally, a police term. In the 1950s and 1960s, police officers in Philadelphia used the phrase "Black Friday" to describe the chaos that followed Thanksgiving — the day when suburban shoppers and tourists flooded into the city for the Army-Navy football game, clogging the streets, overwhelming the sidewalks, and making the officers' jobs miserable. The name was not a compliment. It was a complaint, and it stuck.

Retailers, who stood to profit from the crowds, did not like the name — or at least, they did not like what it implied. In the 1980s, they began promoting a different explanation: that "Black Friday" referred to the moment when stores moved from operating "in the red" — at a loss — to operating "in the black," at a profit, for the year. The accounting explanation was friendlier, and it caught on, but the Philadelphia origin is the documented one. The day was named by the police, not by the accountants, and it was named for the crowds, not for the profits.

The holiday grew slowly, then all at once. Through the 1980s and 1990s, Black Friday became the unofficial start of the Christmas shopping season, and the "doorbuster" — the deeply discounted item available only in limited quantities, only to the first shoppers through the door — became its signature. Shoppers lined up before dawn. Stores opened at 5 a.m., then at 4 a.m., then at midnight. In 2013, some stores began opening on Thanksgiving Day itself, a practice critics called "Black Thursday" and that drew protests from workers and from shoppers who believed the holiday should be protected.

The internet changed everything. In 2005, the National Retail Federation coined the term "Cyber Monday" for the Monday after Thanksgiving, when online shopping surged as people returned to work and to their computers. What began as a marketing label became a second holiday, and then a third — a whole week of online deals that rivaled the in-store event. The pandemic accelerated the shift: in 2020, with stores closed or restricted, Black Friday moved decisively online, and it has never fully returned. The crowds still come, and the stores still open early, but the center of the holiday is now the website, and the deals now stretch across weeks rather than hours.

And the holiday has gone global. Black Friday is now observed in countries around the world — in Britain, in Canada, in Australia, in Brazil, in India — wherever retailers have adopted the American model of a post-Thanksgiving sale. The name travels, the discounts travel, and the crowds travel with them. What began as a Philadelphia police complaint has become one of the world's most recognizable commercial events.

How to shop Black Friday without regretting it

For shoppers, the most important question about Black Friday is not what to buy. It is how to buy without being fooled. The discounts are real, but so are the traps — and the retailers, who have spent a year preparing for this week, know exactly how to set them.

The first rule is to make a list before you shop, and to stick to it. The entire machinery of Black Friday — the flashing banners, the countdown timers, the "only 3 left in stock" warnings — is designed to make you buy things you did not plan to buy. A list is the simplest defense against that machinery, and it is the one that consumer advocates recommend most often. Decide what you need, decide what you are willing to pay for it, and do not let the excitement of the day change either decision.

The second rule is to check the price history. Not every Black Friday discount is what it appears to be. Consumer groups have repeatedly documented a pattern in which retailers raise the price of an item in the weeks before Black Friday, then "discount" it back to — or barely below — its original price, creating the appearance of a bargain where none exists. Price-tracking websites and browser tools can show you what an item actually cost last month, last season, and last year. If the "discount" brings the price back to where it was in October, it is not a deal. It is a performance.

The third rule is to know the categories. Some things are genuinely cheap on Black Friday, and some are not. Televisions, laptops, small appliances, and toys are the categories where the deepest discounts are real — the items that retailers use to draw shoppers in, and that they price aggressively to do so. Winter clothing, jewelry, and furniture, by contrast, are often cheaper at other times of the year. And the biggest-ticket items — the ones that look like the best deals — are often older models, cleared out to make room for the new versions that arrive in January.

The fourth rule is to read the return policy before you buy, not after. Black Friday purchases often come with shortened return windows, restocking fees, or final-sale terms that do not apply the rest of the year. A deal that cannot be returned is not a deal. It is a gamble. And the fifth rule is to be careful with buy-now-pay-later plans. They make the spending feel easier, and they are designed to do exactly that — to separate the pleasure of buying from the pain of paying, and to leave the bill for January. The most expensive thing about Black Friday is not what you buy. It is what you buy that you cannot afford.

None of this is an argument against shopping. The deals are real, the savings are real, and for families who plan carefully, Black Friday is the best time of the year to buy the things they need. It is simply an argument for shopping with your eyes open — for knowing the price before the discount, for knowing the return policy before the purchase, and for knowing your budget before the banners start flashing.

The bottom line

Black Friday 2026 started early across major retailers, with robot vacuums leading the deals — Roomba from $149.99, Roborock Q7 M5+ discounted by $160. Thanksgiving gas fell to an eight-month low of $3.62 a gallon, down about 20 cents in a week and more than 50 cents from last year. And 81.5 million Americans are traveling this week, by road and by air, through king tides on the Pacific Coast, a Delta systems failure in Atlanta, and the first cold snap of winter.

It is a holiday week of contrasts: cheaper travel and earlier sales, set against economic uncertainty and a fragile consumer. The numbers to watch are the gas price, the Black Friday totals, and the travel data. And the story to watch is the oldest one in the American economy — whether, when the moment comes, people spend. This week, they are deciding.
