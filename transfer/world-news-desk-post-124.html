TITLE: Hackers Just Raided FIFA — 12GB of Passports, Financial Records and World Cup Files Stolen by a Brand-New Gang LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Hackers raided FIFA and stole 12GB of files — passports, financial records and World Cup contracts — months before its presidential election. ---BODY---

FIFA President Gianni Infantino in 2024. World football's governing body confirmed on Thursday, 26 November 2026, that it had been the victim of a criminal cyberattack, and a newly emerged hacking group claims to have stolen more than 12 gigabytes of its internal files. Photo: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

The most powerful organisation in world football has been hacked. FIFA confirmed on Thursday, 26 November 2026, that it was the victim of a cyberattack in which hackers gained access to what soccer's world governing body describes as a limited set of personal and business information. A hacking group calling itself the Handsome Boy Hacking Club claims it took more than 12 gigabytes of FIFA's internal files — including identity and immigration documents, financial records, legal papers and material linked to the governance of the sport — and has posted some of them online.

"FIFA can confirm that it has been the victim of a criminal cyberattack resulting in unauthorised access to a limited set of personal and business information," a FIFA spokesperson told Reuters. The organisation said it was working closely with the relevant authorities, including law enforcement. "FIFA condemns in the strongest possible terms the unlawful acquisition and publication of information, particularly personal data," the governing body said in its statement, calling the theft "a serious and unacceptable violation of privacy".

FIFA did not say when the cyberattack took place, or the precise nature of the hacked information. It did not say how many people or organisations were affected, or what the business information included. What is known comes from the hackers themselves, from the documents they have published, and from people familiar with the matter who have spoken to news organisations on condition of anonymity. Taken together, the picture is of a breach that was small in scale — FIFA says the compromised material accounted for less than 0.1 percent of the organisation's total data — but serious in kind, because of what was taken and who took it.

The gang that came out of nowhere

The Handsome Boy Hacking Club — HBHC, as it styles itself — is a newly emerged ransomware and data-extortion group, first observed in November 2026. Threat intelligence platforms recorded its first activity on 19 November, when it listed three organisations on its data-leak site: the United States-based firearms manufacturer Smith & Wesson, the healthcare provider UCHealth, and the telecommunications company Frontier Communications. On 20 November, the group added FIFA to its victim list, bringing the number of publicly recorded claims to four.

The group appeared to claim responsibility for the FIFA breach before FIFA itself made its announcement. It shared a screenshot on X showing what it claimed were FIFA files. Three days later, the group appeared to claim responsibility again in a reply to a post reporting that more than 12 gigabytes of FIFA internal documents had been leaked. At this moment, the group's X account has been suspended, and its Tor leak site is unreachable — the usual fate of a data-extortion gang once its name becomes public, though it is rarely the end of the gang itself.

According to reports, the stolen documents include photographs of passports, financial documents and sponsorship contracts linked to the 2026 World Cup. Several gigabytes of files were stolen from FIFA and posted on the dark web on Friday, 20 November. A few days later, several other internal documents were also leaked. The files, according to Le Temps, the Swiss newspaper that first reported the leak in detail, include some details of the plan to sell part of the World Cup's commercial rights to investors — though FIFA and people familiar with the matter insist that the documents relating to that plan were not among those accessed.

The group, founded this month, declined to give details of its motives or further information about its origins when contacted by Le Temps. But it did confirm one thing: it had submitted a ransom demand to the Zurich-based organisation, which was not paid. "It is a purely financial matter, and unfortunately FIFA did not pay," the group said. That is the standard language of a data-extortion gang — the theft is a business, the ransom is the price, and the leak is the punishment for not paying.

How they got in — and what they did not get

One of the most striking details of the FIFA breach is how the hackers got in. The attack came about after hackers impersonated staff rather than a system breach, according to a person familiar with the matter. FIFA believes the attackers gained access by impersonating members of its staff, rather than exploiting a systematic or technical failure. In other words, this was not a failure of FIFA's firewalls or its software. It was a failure of trust — someone, somewhere, was persuaded that the person on the other end of the conversation was a colleague.

That distinction matters, and FIFA is keen to draw it. The organisation's position is that its systems held; its people were deceived. The attack targeted FIFA as an organisation, rather than specific individuals, according to ESPN, citing a source. The breach, in other words, was a social engineering attack — the oldest trick in the hacker's book, and still, in 2026, one of the most effective.

What the hackers did not get is also significant. Hackers did not access documents relating to the proposal called FIFA Forward Enterprise, the person familiar with the matter said. That proposal — FIFA President Gianni Infantino's plan to open up the World Cup to investors by creating a commercial subsidiary — was abandoned under pressure from many quarters, and it has been one of the most controversial episodes of Infantino's presidency. The leaked documents reveal that the plan involved transfers of rights intended to span 96 years, according to The Guardian. But FIFA's position, backed by the person familiar with the matter, is that the Forward Enterprise files were not among the material taken — a point the organisation has been at pains to make, because the plan's details are politically explosive.

An earlier version of the Le Temps report had suggested a link between the cyberattack and the leak of documents relating to the plan to open up the World Cup to investors by creating a commercial subsidiary. The newspaper corrected that suggestion, noting that it was not the case. The distinction is important: the documents about the Forward Enterprise plan that have appeared in public came from a different source, at a different time, and the cyberattack is not their origin. The hackers' haul, by contrast, is a separate matter — identity documents, financial records, legal papers, and the ordinary internal paperwork of a global organisation.

Even so, the haul is serious. Passports and identity documents are the raw material of identity theft. Financial records can be used for fraud. Legal papers can be used for blackmail. Sponsorship contracts can be used to embarrass commercial partners. And the fact that the material amounts to less than 0.1 percent of FIFA's total data is, in a sense, beside the point: 12 gigabytes is a large amount of sensitive material by any measure, and the fact that FIFA holds vastly more data does not make the stolen portion any less dangerous to the people whose names are in it.

The worst possible timing

The breach comes at a sensitive time for FIFA and its president, Gianni Infantino, who faces criticism over a failed plan to sell World Cup stakes to private investors and his bid to extend his presidency to 2031. The incident comes months before FIFA's presidential elections, scheduled for March next year. Infantino, who has led world football's governing body since 2016, is seeking to extend his tenure, and the controversy over the Forward Enterprise plan — the attempt to sell a stake in the World Cup's commercial rights to outside investors — has already damaged his standing with the national associations that elect him.

The timing is awkward in another way. The 2026 World Cup, held in the United States, Canada and Mexico, has only just concluded, and FIFA is in the middle of the commercial cycle that follows it — negotiating sponsorships, settling accounts, planning the next tournament. The sponsorship contracts that the hackers claim to have taken are the documents that govern those relationships. If they are published in full, they could embarrass FIFA's commercial partners, reveal the terms on which the World Cup's money is divided, and give rival bidders information they should not have.

There is also the question of who else is in the files. FIFA did not say how many people were affected, and the identity and immigration documents the hackers claim to hold could belong to players, officials, staff, or the many thousands of people who work on FIFA's tournaments. The organisation's statement condemned the publication of personal data in the strongest terms, and warned of the potential consequences for those affected. But it has not said what it will do for them — whether it will offer credit monitoring, identity protection, or any other form of support. That is a question that will be asked again, and more loudly, in the days to come.

A familiar pattern — and a new kind of gang

The FIFA breach fits a pattern that has become depressingly familiar. A major organisation is attacked, a newly formed gang claims responsibility, a ransom is demanded and not paid, and the stolen data appears on a leak site. The same pattern has played out across industries in 2026 — healthcare, manufacturing, telecommunications, and now sport. The gangs change their names, but the business model does not: steal the data, demand the money, publish the data if the money does not come.

What is new about the Handsome Boy Hacking Club is how fast it moved. First observed on 19 November, it had listed four victims within two days — Smith & Wesson, UCHealth, Frontier Communications and FIFA. That is an aggressive pace for a new group, and it suggests either a well-resourced operation or a group that had been active under a different name before. Threat intelligence platforms are still building a picture of the gang, and the fact that its X account has been suspended and its Tor leak site is unreachable does not mean it has gone away. Data-extortion gangs routinely reappear under new names, with new leak sites, and the same stolen data.

The other listings should be treated as claims that have not necessarily been independently verified. FIFA is the only one of the four that has confirmed an attack, and the confirmation came from the organisation itself. Smith & Wesson, UCHealth and Frontier Communications have not, as of this writing, confirmed that they were breached. That is normal: organisations often take days or weeks to confirm a breach, and some never do. But the FIFA case is now the reference point for the group, and it is the case that will be cited when the gang's next victims are named.

The 96-year plan at the centre of the storm

To understand why this breach matters so much to FIFA, it helps to understand the plan that the hackers did not steal — because it is the plan that explains the politics around the organisation. FIFA Forward Enterprise was Infantino's proposal to open up the World Cup to investors by creating a commercial subsidiary, transferring the tournament's commercial rights to outside investors for a period that, according to documents seen by The Guardian, was intended to span 96 years. The plan was abandoned under pressure from many quarters — national associations, sponsors, and critics inside the game who saw it as a sale of football's crown jewel.

The controversy has not gone away with the plan. Infantino faces criticism over the failed attempt, and over his bid to extend his presidency to 2031, and the documents about the plan that have already appeared in public have been damaging enough. The fact that the cyberattack's haul does not include those documents is, for FIFA, a piece of good news inside a bad story — but it is also a reminder of how much sensitive material the organisation holds, and how many people would like to see it. The Forward Enterprise files, the sponsorship contracts, the financial records and the legal papers are all part of the same picture: an organisation that controls billions of dollars of football money, and whose internal workings are of intense interest to a great many people.

The World Cup itself is the largest commercial enterprise in world sport. The 2026 tournament, held in the United States, Canada and Mexico, generated revenues on a scale that dwarfs any other sporting event, and the sponsorship contracts that govern it are among the most valuable documents in sport. The hackers' claim to have taken sponsorship contracts linked to the 2026 World Cup is therefore not a minor detail. Those contracts set out the terms on which the world's biggest brands associate themselves with the world's biggest tournament — the fees, the rights, the exclusivity, the conditions. If they are published, competitors will read them, sponsors will be embarrassed, and FIFA's negotiating position for the next cycle will be weakened.

There is a further dimension. The identity and immigration documents the group claims to hold are the kind of material that can be used to track, impersonate or endanger the people they belong to. FIFA's tournaments bring together players, officials, staff and volunteers from more than 200 countries, and the organisation holds the personal documents of many of them. The publication of passports — even a small number of them — is a serious matter for the individuals concerned, and the fact that FIFA has not yet said how many people are affected, or what it will do for them, is the most troubling gap in its response so far.

Sport's growing target

FIFA is not the first sporting body to be hacked, and it will not be the last. Sport has become a prime target for cybercriminals, for reasons that are easy to see: sporting organisations hold large amounts of personal data, they move large amounts of money, they operate across many jurisdictions, and they are often less well defended than banks or technology companies. The same week that FIFA confirmed its breach, the French news agency AFP reported a cyberattack of its own, shutting down its content platform. The week before, hackers breached the United States Air Force's Cloud One computing platform. The pattern is not confined to sport — but sport, with its global reach and its high profile, is an attractive target.

What makes the FIFA case distinctive is the combination of a brand-new gang, a high-value target, and a method — impersonation — that no amount of spending on firewalls can prevent. The lesson, for FIFA and for every organisation like it, is that the weakest link in the security chain is not the software but the people who use it. Training, verification, and a culture in which nobody hands over access on the strength of a plausible email or a confident voice on the phone, are the defences that matter — and they are the defences that failed here.

The gang's other victims are a reminder of how indiscriminate the new extortion economy has become. A firearms manufacturer, a healthcare provider, a telecommunications company and the governing body of world football have little in common except that they all hold data worth stealing and money worth demanding. The Handsome Boy Hacking Club did not choose FIFA because it cares about football. It chose FIFA because FIFA is rich, famous, and — as it turned out — vulnerable to a phone call.

What happens next

FIFA says it is working closely with the relevant authorities, including law enforcement, and that it is investigating the incident and focusing on determining the scope of the leaked information and the extent of the damage. That investigation will have several strands. The first is forensic: how did the attackers get in, what did they touch, and is there any remaining access that needs to be closed? The second is legal: which jurisdictions' data protection laws apply, and what notifications are required? FIFA is based in Zurich, Switzerland, and the people whose data was taken may live in dozens of countries. The third is reputational: how does an organisation that runs the world's most popular sport explain to its members, its sponsors and its fans that its own house was robbed?

The fourth strand is the one that will matter most to the people in the files. If passports and identity documents have been published on the dark web, the people they belong to are now at risk of identity theft — and they may not know it yet. FIFA has not said whether it will contact those affected individually, or what support it will offer. That is the question that will define the aftermath of this breach, more than the politics of the presidential election or the fate of the Forward Enterprise plan.

For FIFA, the breach is also a test of a claim it has made repeatedly: that it is a modern, professional organisation, run to the standards of a global business. A global business that loses 12 gigabytes of its most sensitive files to a gang that did not exist a month ago — and that got in by pretending to be its own staff — has a problem that goes beyond the files themselves. It has a problem of trust. And trust, in an organisation whose authority rests on the confidence of 211 national associations, is not easily rebuilt.

The hackers, for their part, have already moved on. Their X account is suspended, their leak site is down, and their ransom was not paid. But the data they stole is still out there, and the people whose passports and financial records are in it are still waiting to find out what happens next. The World Cup may be over, but for FIFA, the most difficult match of the year is only just beginning.
