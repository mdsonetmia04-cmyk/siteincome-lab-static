TITLE: Congress Is 1 Yard From Banning Chinese Cars — 100% Tariffs, a December Voice Vote, and the Volvo–Mercedes Loophole LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Congress is close to passing the Connected Vehicle Security Act, banning US sales of cars built with Chinese components — with automakers on board. ---BODY---

The bipartisan bill to ban US sales of cars built with Chinese components is, in the words of one person familiar with the talks, at the "one yard line" — with a Senate voice vote aimed for December. Congress is one yard from banning Chinese cars from the United States — permanently, and by law. The bipartisan Connected Vehicle Security Act, which would bar the sale of vehicles made with components from China and other adversarial countries, is closer to passing than it has ever been, after its Senate sponsors won over the car industry, smoothed out the objections of European automakers, and secured the support of Senate leaders from both parties. The bill's backers are aiming to pass it by a voice vote in the Senate in December, and they are working with House colleagues to fast-track it through that chamber as well. "We are basically done with the bill," Senator Bernie Moreno, the Ohio Republican who co-sponsored it, said in an interview on Monday. One person familiar with the negotiations put it more colourfully: the discussions are at the "one yard line."

The bill, formally the Connected Vehicle Security Act of 2026, would ban US sales of cars made with components from adversaries — principally China and Russia — including the software and hardware that make a modern car "connected": the systems that link a vehicle to the internet, to its manufacturer, and to the world around it. It was introduced on April 29, 2026, by Moreno and Senator Elissa Slotkin, a Michigan Democrat, and it passed the Senate Commerce Committee unanimously on July 22, 2026. On September 22, it was reported out of the committee with an amendment and placed on the Senate legislative calendar. Now, with the auto industry on board and the end of the congressional session approaching, its sponsors believe they can get it across the finish line before Congress adjourns for the year.

Passing the legislation would be another sign of Capitol Hill's hardening position on China — and it would put Congress at odds, in public at least, with President Donald Trump, who has defended doing more business with Beijing and has even mused about allowing Chinese car companies to build plants and sell their vehicles in the United States. The bill's momentum is also a measure of how far the politics of Chinese cars has moved: a ban that would once have been dismissed as protectionism now has the backing of the auto industry, the auto workers' union, the Teamsters, and senators from both parties.

What the bill would ban — and why

The Connected Vehicle Security Act would prohibit the sale in the United States of vehicles made with components from foreign adversaries, including China and Russia. The ban covers not just whole cars, but the parts and software inside them: the connectivity hardware and software that allow a modern vehicle to communicate with its surroundings, receive updates over the air, and transmit data back to its manufacturer.

The national security argument behind the bill is straightforward. A modern car is, in effect, a computer on wheels, fitted with cameras, microphones, GPS, and a permanent internet connection. A vehicle built with Chinese components could, in theory, collect detailed data about where its owner goes, what they say inside the car, and the roads and infrastructure around them — and transmit that data back to China. Slotkin has justified the bill on those grounds, arguing that Chinese vehicles pose a surveillance risk, and that they risk undercutting American automakers with state-subsidised competition.

The economic argument is just as important to the bill's supporters. Chinese automakers such as BYD and Geely have grown rapidly around the world by producing cars at a wide range of price points, backed by the scale of the Chinese domestic market and by industrial policies that American and European manufacturers say amount to unfair competition. If Chinese cars were allowed into the US market, the argument runs, they could undercut American manufacturers on price, cost American jobs, and hollow out an industry that employs millions of people. The United Auto Workers' president, Shawn Fain, has backed the bill. General Motors has issued a statement in support of it, writing that the company "supports policies that protect and strengthen American manufacturing and the global competitiveness of US automakers." The International Brotherhood of Teamsters, which represents about 1.3 million workers, urged lawmakers on November 24 to pass tougher restrictions on Chinese vehicles before Congress adjourns for the year, with its president, Sean O'Brien, arguing that Chinese manufacturers could jeopardise national security and hurt American companies.

The bill would also codify restrictions that already exist in piecemeal form. Chinese vehicles are already effectively blocked from the US market by steep 100 percent tariffs — first imposed by Trump and raised by former President Joe Biden — as well as by Commerce Department restrictions on vehicle components. The Bureau of Industry and Security's rules prohibit sales of covered Chinese- and Russian-linked connected vehicles and software beginning with model year 2027, with restrictions on specified connectivity hardware beginning with model year 2030. But tariffs can be lowered by a future president, and Commerce Department rules can be rewritten by a future administration. A law passed by Congress is harder to undo — and that certainty, supporters say, is what the industry needs to make long-term decisions about where to source parts and where to build cars.

The Volvo and Mercedes problem — and how it was solved

The reason the bill took seven months to reach the one-yard line is that its first draft caught more than its authors intended. The original version would have banned sales of vehicles made by companies in which Chinese interests held more than a 15 percent stake. That threshold sounded reasonable — until the industry pointed out who it would catch. Volvo, the Swedish carmaker, is about 80 percent owned by Geely, the Chinese company that also owns a large stake in Mercedes-Benz. Mercedes-Benz itself has Chinese investors holding approximately 20 percent, including stakes associated with BAIC Group and Geely's chairman, Li Shufu. Under the original 15 percent rule, both Volvo and Mercedes-Benz — two brands that employ thousands of Americans and build cars in American factories — could have been barred from selling vehicles in the United States.

That was not what the bill's sponsors intended, and it was not a result the auto industry, or the senators from the states where those cars are built, would accept. The two senators eased the relevant provisions, loosening the ownership rule so that companies like Mercedes and Volvo, with significant Chinese shareholders, will not be blocked from selling cars in the US. The compromise reflects a careful line: the bill targets vehicles and components from China, not companies that happen to have Chinese investors.

Two other changes were made to win over the industry. The revised bill excludes batteries from the components subject to restrictions, and regulates only battery management systems — the electronics that control how a battery charges and discharges. That change matters because of a specific deal: the technology licensing agreement between the US automaker Ford and the Chinese battery maker CATL, one of the world's largest battery producers, is expected to remain unaffected under the revised language. And the senators decided to include a sunset clause in the vehicle import ban, reflecting concerns among some lawmakers that an indefinite ban on sales of low-cost Chinese electric vehicles could hamper efforts to reduce carbon emissions.

Those changes won over the European and American automakers whose concerns had been the main obstacle to the bill. The Alliance for Automotive Innovation, which represents major manufacturers including Ford, General Motors, Stellantis, Mercedes, and Volvo, told Politico it is "very supportive" of the bill. On September 3, the Alliance had already sent a letter to congressional leaders asking for a permanent ban on connected vehicles, software, and hardware from foreign adversaries. Winning over the industry, and the senators who defend it, greatly improves the odds of the bill passing — because in the Senate, where a single objection can block a voice vote, the support of every interested party matters.

The senators who had to be won over

Two senators in particular illustrate why the negotiations took so long — and why their resolution matters. Senator Rand Paul of Kentucky, whose state is an auto manufacturing hub that has courted more than 10 billion dollars in investment from international automakers, had concerns about parts of the legislation. Senator Katie Britt of Alabama, whose state is home to a Mercedes plant, had concerns of her own. Winning over both, as well as others like them, is crucial to getting the unanimous consent the Senate needs to pass the legislation by voice vote — a necessity given the end-of-year floor time crunch, as lawmakers race to complete votes on must-pass bills before this Congress expires.

A voice vote is the fastest way to pass a bill in the Senate, but it requires that no senator objects. A single objection forces a recorded vote, which takes floor time that, in the final weeks of a congressional session, is in desperately short supply. That is why the sponsors have spent months negotiating with every senator who might object, and why the support of Senate leaders from both parties — including Majority Leader John Thune, the South Dakota Republican who helped advance the measure out of the Senate Commerce Committee earlier this year — is so important. Thune's support signals that the bill has the leadership's blessing, and that floor time will be found for it.

The House is the other half of the equation. Moreno and Slotkin are in talks with House colleagues to ensure the bill can move quickly through that chamber as well. The House has its own version of the politics: members from auto-manufacturing districts, members concerned about China, and members worried about the price of cars for their constituents. The bill's sponsors are working to ensure that whatever passes the Senate can pass the House without a fresh round of negotiations — because with the session ending, there is no time for one.

The tariffs already in place — and why a law is different

It is worth asking why Congress is racing to pass a law banning Chinese cars when Chinese cars are already, for practical purposes, banned. The answer lies in the difference between a tariff and a law, and between an executive action and a statute.

The 100 percent tariffs on Chinese vehicles were imposed by executive action — first by Trump, then raised by Biden — and they can be reduced or removed by a future president with the stroke of a pen. The Commerce Department's restrictions on connected vehicle components are regulations, and regulations can be rewritten. A law passed by Congress and signed by the president, by contrast, can only be changed by another law. For automakers deciding where to invest billions of dollars in factories and supply chains, that permanence matters. As one industry voice put it, codifying the blockade into federal law provides the certainty firms need to make long-term sourcing decisions.

The political context also matters. President Trump has, at various points, defended doing more business with China — defending Nvidia's chief executive, Jensen Huang, amid a Chinese espionage scandal, and musing about allowing Chinese car companies to build plants and sell their vehicles in the US. Congress, by passing a ban, would be asserting its own view: that Chinese cars are a security risk and an economic threat, whatever the president's instincts about deal-making with Beijing. That tension — between a president who sees China as a negotiating partner and a Congress that increasingly sees it as an adversary — is one of the defining features of American China policy, and the car ban is its latest expression.

The bill also arrives at a moment when the politics of car prices is unusually sensitive. Americans continue to worry about affordability, and the campaign around the bill has unfolded against a backdrop of rising prices. Supporters argue that the ban protects American jobs from unfair competition by manufacturers that profit from Chinese industrial practices. Critics — including some lawmakers who pushed for the sunset clause — worry that blocking low-cost Chinese electric vehicles could slow the shift to cleaner cars and keep prices higher than they would otherwise be. The sunset clause is the compromise between those two views: a ban, but not necessarily a permanent one.

BYD, Geely, and the cars Americans cannot buy

For most Americans, the ban is abstract: Chinese cars are not sold in the United States, and few Americans have ever driven one. But in the rest of the world, Chinese cars are everywhere. BYD, the Shenzhen-based manufacturer, has become one of the world's largest makers of electric vehicles, selling cars across Europe, Latin America, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East at prices that European and American manufacturers struggle to match. Geely, which owns Volvo and a stake in Mercedes-Benz, sells cars under its own name and through its subsidiaries in dozens of countries. Chinese automakers have grown globally by producing cars at a variety of price points — from budget city cars to luxury electric sedans — and by moving faster than their Western rivals on batteries, software, and manufacturing scale.

That global success is precisely what worries the bill's supporters. If Chinese manufacturers were allowed into the US market, they argue, they would bring the same advantages that have made them formidable elsewhere: lower costs, faster product cycles, and the backing of the world's largest car market. American manufacturers, already struggling with the transition to electric vehicles, would face a competitor they could not match on price. The bill's supporters do not want to find out whether they are right. They want the door closed before it is opened.

The irony is that the ban also closes the door on the technology that Chinese manufacturers have pioneered. Chinese electric vehicles are widely regarded as among the most advanced in the world — not just in price, but in battery technology, in-car software, and the integration of the car with the phone and the home. By banning them, the United States is choosing to forgo that technology, at least for now, in the name of security and industrial policy. The sunset clause leaves open the possibility that the calculation could change. For now, though, the calculation is clear: the risks outweigh the benefits, and the door stays shut.

The road to December — and what could still go wrong

The path to passage is narrow but visible. The sponsors aim for a voice vote in the Senate in December, with the House moving quickly behind it. The substance of the changes has been resolved, according to the people familiar with the talks; only wording and text remain to be ironed out. The industry is on board. Both parties' Senate leadership have signalled support. The Teamsters, the UAW, and the Alliance for Automotive Innovation are all pushing for passage before Congress adjourns.

What could still go wrong is the calendar. The end of a congressional session is a crush of must-pass bills — government funding, defence authorisation, and the rest — and a bill that is not must-pass can be squeezed out by the sheer press of business. A single senator's objection can force a recorded vote, and a recorded vote takes time that may not exist. The sponsors' strategy — a voice vote, with every potential objector already won over — is designed to avoid exactly that problem. But it depends on nothing going wrong in the final days, and in Congress, something usually goes wrong.

There is also the question of the president's signature. A bill passed by Congress must be signed by the president to become law, and Trump has mused about allowing Chinese car companies into the US market. Would he sign a bill that bans them? The bill's sponsors evidently believe he would — or that, if he vetoed it, Congress could override him. The politics of a veto would be awkward for a president who has made his opposition to China a central theme of his politics. Signing a ban on Chinese cars is, in that sense, the easy choice. But Trump has surprised Congress before, and the bill's fate will not be certain until the ink is dry.

For now, though, the bill is at the one-yard line — closer than any previous attempt to ban Chinese cars has ever come. The negotiations are done. The industry is on board. The leadership is supportive. And if the voice vote happens in December, as its sponsors intend, the United States will have a law — not just a tariff, not just a regulation, but a law — that says Chinese cars cannot be sold on American roads. That is a decision with consequences for the auto industry, for American workers, for the price of cars, and for the relationship between the United States and China. It is also, if it passes, the end of a debate that has been running for years — and the beginning of a new one, about what America gains, and what it gives up, by keeping Chinese cars out.

For American car buyers, the ban's most immediate effect is the absence of a choice they never had. No Chinese car is sold in the United States today, so no dealership will close and no model will disappear from a showroom. What changes is the future: the affordable electric cars that Chinese manufacturers sell in Europe for the equivalent of 20,000 to 30,000 dollars will not be coming to American roads, and American buyers will keep paying the prices American, European, Japanese, and Korean manufacturers charge. Supporters of the bill say that is the price of protecting American jobs and national security. Critics say it is a tax on American consumers, paid in the form of higher car prices and slower adoption of electric vehicles. Both sides agree on one thing: the decision, once written into law, will shape the American car market for a generation.
