TITLE: Israel's Eisenkot Gives Himself 8 Days to Form a Government — as Netanyahu Weighs Serving Second LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Gadi Eisenkot says he will form Israel's next government within the eight days left in his mandate, accusing Likud of stalling unity talks. ---BODY---

Gadi Eisenkot in 2026. The former army chief has eight days left in his mandate to form Israel's next government. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).

Gadi Eisenkot has given himself eight days to change the government of Israel — and he has accused Benjamin Netanyahu of trying to run out the clock. In a public statement on Friday, 27 November 2026, the former army chief who now leads the Yashar party said he intends to form a new government within the eight days remaining in his mandate, and he charged that Netanyahu's Likud party has been conducting coalition negotiations "without any genuine intention of reaching an agreement."

It is the most dramatic moment yet in a political crisis that has gripped Israel since the election of 27 October 2026, an election that ended Netanyahu's long hold on power without giving his rivals a clear majority of their own. Eisenkot, whose Yashar party won 21 of the Knesset's 120 seats, was asked by President Isaac Herzog to form the next government after 67 members of the Knesset recommended him for prime minister, against 46 for Netanyahu. Now, with his mandate running down, he has drawn a line: eight days, no extension, and a government — with Likud or without it.

"I intend to use the mandate I was given to form a new government. Eight days remain, and I intend to establish a government within that time," Eisenkot wrote. He also made clear that he did not intend to seek an extension of his mandate from the president. If he fails, the mandate returns to Herzog, and Israel — a country that has already endured years of political instability, a war in Gaza, a war with Iran, and the deepest social divisions in its history — faces the prospect of yet another election.

At the centre of the standoff is an extraordinary offer that Netanyahu is said to be considering: serving second in a rotation for the premiership, with Eisenkot going first. It would be a humbling comedown for a man who has been prime minister for most of the past seventeen years. Eisenkot's allies say the offer, if it comes, will be a ploy to buy time. Netanyahu's allies say Eisenkot is the one playing a double game. Both sides agree on one thing: the next eight days will decide who governs Israel.

Five meetings, no deal

The breakdown Eisenkot described is, on its face, a story of negotiations that went nowhere. In his statement, he said that the two sides had held five meetings over two weeks, but that Likud had refused to make concrete proposals or compromise on key issues. The issues he listed are the ones that have divided Israeli politics for years — and they go to the heart of what the next government would be.

First, the removal of far-right parties from the government. Netanyahu's current coalition depends on parties of the far right, whose leaders have pushed for the expansion of settlements, the annexation of parts of the West Bank, and policies that Israel's courts and its allies have condemned. Eisenkot has insisted that any unity government must exclude them. Likud, whose own political survival depends on those parties, has refused.

Second, a state commission of inquiry into the 7 October 2023 attack, in which Hamas killed around 1,200 people in southern Israel and took more than 250 hostage — the deadliest day in Israel's history, and the event that led to the war in Gaza. Netanyahu has resisted a full state commission, which would have the power to summon witnesses and apportion blame, including to him. Eisenkot has made it a condition of any deal.

Third, legislation requiring broader military service — the question of whether ultra-Orthodox Jewish men, who have long been exempt from the draft, should be required to serve. The issue has divided Israel for decades, and it has become explosive since 7 October, as the army has fought a long war in Gaza and reservists have served hundreds of days. Eisenkot, a former chief of staff who lost his own son in Gaza, has insisted on a law that spreads the burden. Likud, dependent on ultra-Orthodox parties, has resisted.

Eisenkot said some coalition negotiations had been characterised by a genuine willingness to cooperate, resulting in agreements and the formulation of policy guidelines for a new government. But he accused Likud representatives of entrenching themselves in positions intended to prevent any meaningful progress toward a unity government. "It is convenient to focus the discussion on a rotation agreement for the premiership," he wrote, "but that is only the tip of the iceberg of persistent refusal to address issues at the very heart of our lives."

He was blunter still about what he would not accept. "The notion that we would serve as a rubber stamp for another Netanyahu government after October 7 and everything that has happened here over the past four years," he wrote — and left the sentence hanging, the implication clear. Eisenkot did not enter politics to keep Netanyahu in power. He entered it to replace him.

The rotation offer — and why Eisenkot calls it a ploy

The most surprising element of the crisis is the offer Netanyahu is said to be weighing: a rotation agreement in which Eisenkot would serve first as prime minister, and Netanyahu second. The idea was hinted at on Thursday by Avigdor Liberman, the leader of Yisrael Beytenu, who said Netanyahu was considering letting Eisenkot serve first. Sources in Eisenkot's bloc believe Netanyahu may make a significant new offer as early as the beginning of next week, in an attempt to prevent the government Eisenkot is planning from being sworn in.

Rotation governments are not new in Israel. Netanyahu himself served in one, sharing the premiership with Benny Gantz in 2020, in an arrangement that collapsed within months. The idea has an obvious appeal to a mediator: both men get to be prime minister, and the country avoids an election. But Eisenkot's camp is deeply suspicious. They believe such an offer would be a ploy to buy time — a way for Netanyahu to stay in office, control the agenda, and wait for Eisenkot's mandate to expire.

That suspicion is rooted in experience. Netanyahu is the longest-serving prime minister in Israeli history, and he has survived more political crises than any other Israeli leader by outlasting his rivals. He has formed governments with parties that swore never to sit with him, and he has broken agreements when it suited him. Eisenkot's allies remember all of it, and they do not trust a rotation deal that would leave Netanyahu in the prime minister's office, even as the second man in the rotation, with the power to bring the government down.

There is also a deeper problem. A rotation deal would not resolve the issues Eisenkot listed — the far-right parties, the commission of inquiry, the draft law, the judicial overhaul. It would paper over them. Eisenkot's position is that a government must be built on agreements about what it will do, not on an arrangement about who sits in which chair. "It is convenient to focus the discussion on a rotation agreement," he wrote, and the word "convenient" was doing a lot of work: convenient for Netanyahu, because it avoids the hard questions; convenient for the press, because it is a simple story; but not, in Eisenkot's view, a solution.

The government Eisenkot is building

While the unity talks have stalled, Eisenkot has been building an alternative: a coalition of his own, assembled from the parties that recommended him to the president, with a cabinet already sketched out in unusual detail.

According to reports in the Israeli press, the proposed government would include Avigdor Lieberman as defence minister, Yair Lapid as foreign minister, and Naftali Bennett as finance minister — three former prime ministers and defence ministers, all of them now in Eisenkot's camp. Oded Forer of Yisrael Beytenu would serve as justice minister. Yair Golan, the leader of the Democrats, would serve as public security minister. The lineup also includes Yulia Malinovsky as aliyah and integration minister, Gilad Kariv as culture and sports minister, Efrat Rayten as communications minister, and Naama Lazimi as welfare minister.

Under a coalition agreement reached with Eisenkot, the Democrats would receive four ministerial portfolios and chair several key parliamentary committees, including the Knesset's Constitution, Law and Justice Committee — the committee that would oversee any reform of the judicial system. That detail matters, because the judicial system is at the heart of the crisis.

Eisenkot has also detailed the compromises his party has been prepared to make to secure a unity government, including establishing a joint committee of the parties to advance a broad, agreed-upon reform of the judicial system — essentially compromising on reversing the Netanyahu coalition's judicial overhaul — within a predetermined timetable. The judicial overhaul, pushed by Netanyahu's coalition in 2023, sought to curb the power of the Supreme Court and was the largest domestic crisis in Israel's history before 7 October, bringing hundreds of thousands of protesters onto the streets for months. Eisenkot's offer was to negotiate a reform that both sides could accept, rather than simply repealing the overhaul. Likud, he said, refused to engage.

Naftali Bennett, the leader of the opposition alliance Together and a former prime minister, has said he prefers forming a national unity government, adding that if this proves impossible, his bloc will move forward with a less desirable alternative. That alternative is the government Eisenkot is now assembling — a coalition without Likud, dependent on a patchwork of parties from the centre, the centre-left and the right, and possibly on the support of Arab parties from outside the coalition.

Likud has seized on that last point. In response to Eisenkot's statement, the party accused him of conducting a political double game, claiming he was already assembling an alternative coalition, distributing ministerial portfolios and preparing to rely on Arab parties, which Likud described as supporters of terrorism. It is a familiar attack in Israeli politics, and a measure of how bitter the fight has become.

Who is Gadi Eisenkot?

Gadi Eisenkot was born on 19 May 1960 in Tiberias, a town on the shore of the Sea of Galilee, and grew up in Eilat, the resort city on the Red Sea. He joined the army in 1978 and served in the Golani Brigade, one of the Israel Defense Forces' most storied infantry units. Over the next four decades he rose through every level of the military: commander of the Golani Brigade, commander of the West Bank division, head of military operations, military secretary to the prime minister, and, from 2015 to 2019, Chief of the General Staff — the highest-ranking officer in the IDF, the man in charge of the entire Israeli military.

As chief of staff, Eisenkot was known as a cautious, methodical commander. He developed the military doctrine that bears his name — the idea that Israel should respond to attacks with disproportionate force, to deter its enemies from attacking again — and he oversaw the campaign of airstrikes against Iranian targets in Syria that became known as the "war between the wars". He retired in 2019 and, after a period in business and academia, entered politics in August 2022, joining Benny Gantz's National Unity alliance. He won a seat in the Knesset in the November 2022 election.

His political career was transformed by the war in Gaza. On 7 December 2023, two months after the Hamas attack, his son Gal, a 25-year-old reservist, was killed fighting in Gaza. Eisenkot, who was then serving as a minister without portfolio in the emergency war government, attended his son's funeral and returned to the cabinet the next day. The loss made him, in the eyes of many Israelis, the most credible voice in the country on the war: a father who had paid the same price as thousands of other families, and a general who understood what the war was costing.

He left the war government with the rest of National Unity in June 2024, and in June 2025 he left the party altogether, resigning his Knesset seat the following month. He then founded Yashar — the name means "upright" or "straight" in Hebrew — a new party built around his own reputation: a soldier, a straight-talker, a man who had lost a son and who promised to lead Israel differently. In the election of 27 October 2026, Yashar won 21 seats, behind Likud's 27, but enough — with the support of 67 members of the Knesset — to give Eisenkot the mandate to form a government.

Why Netanyahu is fighting so hard to stay

For Netanyahu, the stakes of the next eight days are existential. He has been prime minister for most of the past seventeen years, and he has survived corruption charges, mass protests, a war, and the deadliest attack in his country's history. He is still facing trial on charges of bribery, fraud and breach of trust — although judges in one of the cases, the so-called Case 4000, have recently urged prosecutors to drop the bribery charge, in what would be a significant victory for him if they agree.

Netanyahu's argument for a unity government is that Israel needs one. Earlier this month he called for the formation of a national unity government, asserting that the country must remain united in the face of what he described as a growing threat from Iran. The threat is real enough: Israel and Iran fought a war earlier this year, the United States is blockading Iranian ports, and on 27 November American aircraft disabled a merchant ship in the Gulf of Oman that was trying to run the blockade. Netanyahu's case is that a country facing Iran, and still fighting in Gaza, cannot afford a narrow, partisan government.

But his critics hear something else in that argument: a man who lost an election trying to stay in power by any means. The election gave Likud 27 seats, the largest single party, but only 46 members of the Knesset recommended Netanyahu for prime minister, against 67 for Eisenkot. In a parliamentary system, that is a defeat, however it is dressed up. Netanyahu's response — to stall the unity talks, to float a rotation deal, to accuse Eisenkot of a double game — looks to his critics like a man who will not accept the result.

There is also the matter of what a government without Netanyahu would do. A state commission of inquiry into 7 October would investigate the failures that led to the deadliest day in Israeli history, and it would almost certainly find that Netanyahu, as prime minister, bore responsibility. A law requiring ultra-Orthodox men to serve in the army would break the alliance with the religious parties that has sustained Netanyahu for years. A reversal of the judicial overhaul would undo the project that defined his last government. For Netanyahu, losing power is not just a political defeat. It is the loss of the protections that power provides.

The election that nobody won

The crisis began on 27 October 2026, when Israelis voted in an election that produced a result almost nobody expected — and nobody quite won. Likud, Netanyahu's party, finished first, with 27 seats. Yashar, Eisenkot's party, finished second, with 21. But the arithmetic of the Knesset, Israel's 120-seat parliament, is what matters, and the arithmetic said that Netanyahu could not form a government: only 46 members recommended him to the president, while 67 recommended Eisenkot.

The new Knesset was sworn in on 10 November, and President Herzog held his consultations with the parties over the following days. On 5 November, after two days of consultations, the picture was clear: 67 members for Eisenkot, 46 for Netanyahu. Three days later, Herzog announced that he would ask Eisenkot to form the government, giving him the standard 28-day mandate. The eight days that remain are the last stretch of that mandate.

The election itself was a verdict on four years of crisis: the judicial overhaul and the protests it provoked, the 7 October attack and the war that followed, the war with Iran, and a cost of living that has squeezed Israeli families for years. Voters did not give any party a majority, and they did not give Eisenkot a mandate to govern alone. What they gave him was a chance: the chance to build a coalition from the parties that had, for the first time in years, agreed on one thing — that it was time for a change.

What happens in the next eight days

Eisenkot's mandate expires in eight days. In that time, three things could happen. The first is a unity government, built on a rotation deal or on a genuine agreement about the issues that divide the two sides. The second is a government without Likud, sworn in on the votes of Eisenkot's bloc, possibly as a minority government dependent on outside support — a possibility that has been openly discussed in the Israeli press, with reports that Eisenkot aims to hold a vote to swear in a minority government next week. The third is failure: no government, the mandate returned to the president, and Israel heading for another election, its fifth in seven years.

A minority government would be fragile, and Eisenkot knows it. It would depend on parties that disagree with each other on almost everything except the desire to replace Netanyahu, and it would need the support — or at least the abstention — of Arab parties that have never been part of an Israeli government. But it would be a government, and it would end the deadlock. The alternative — another election — would leave Israel without a functioning government for months, at a time when it is fighting a war in Gaza, facing Iran, and rebuilding its economy.

The next few days will be decisive. Sources in Eisenkot's bloc believe Netanyahu may make a significant new offer as early as the beginning of next week, in an attempt to prevent the government Eisenkot is planning from being sworn in. Whether that offer is genuine, or a ploy, will be judged by what it contains: not just who serves first in a rotation, but what the government would actually do about the far-right parties, the commission of inquiry, the draft law and the courts.

Eisenkot has staked his reputation on a simple promise: a government within eight days. He is a soldier who became a politician by promising to be straight with the Israeli public, and his statement on Friday was written in that voice — direct, unadorned, and uncompromising. "Eight days remain, and I intend to establish a government within that time." In eight days, Israel will know whether he meant it — and whether the country that has been governed by Benjamin Netanyahu for most of the past two decades is finally ready for someone else.
