TITLE: Nvidia Brings Back Jensen Huang as CEO — Shares Surge 31% as Trump Claims Credit and US Blacklists Megaspeed LABELS: Business, Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Nvidia's board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO on Monday; shares surged 31%, the Nasdaq jumped 6.5%, and the US blacklisted Megaspeed the same day. ---BODY---

Jensen Huang in 2026. Photo: Anders Eidesvik / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)

Nvidia's board of directors voted unanimously on Monday to bring Jensen Huang back as the company's president and chief executive, ending one of the most extraordinary corporate crises in the history of the technology industry and sending the chipmaker's shares soaring by more than 30 percent in a single day.

The decision, announced on November 23, 2026, came after a weekend of intense pressure from the White House and followed talks between the board and Huang himself, along with consultations with senior United States officials. The Nasdaq Composite jumped 6.5 percent on Monday as investors welcomed the return of the man who built Nvidia from a graphics-chip startup into the world's most valuable supplier of artificial intelligence hardware.

Huang had resigned earlier in November after reports of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman whom United States officials believe is a Chinese spy. His departure stunned Wall Street, wiped billions of dollars from Nvidia's market value and raised urgent questions about the leadership of a company at the very centre of the global AI race. His return, barely two weeks later, was just as startling — and it immediately became a political story as well as a business one, with President Donald Trump claiming credit for the reinstatement and the administration taking parallel action against a Singaporean firm accused of smuggling Nvidia chips to China.

The board's unanimous vote

Nvidia's board voted unanimously to reinstate Huang after a lengthy meeting, according to reports of the decision. Directors spoke with Huang and consulted senior US officials before reaching their conclusion, and the company said the investigation into the allegations behind his dismissal would continue even as he resumed his duties.

Trump, who had weighed in publicly on the affair, said Huang's return posed no risk to national security — a statement that carried weight because the central concern around the resignation was never simply the affairs themselves, but the identity of one of the women involved. According to reports that preceded Huang's resignation, one of the women was believed by US officials to be a Chinese intelligence operative, raising the possibility that the chief executive of America's most strategically important chip company had been compromised.

Before the board's decision, Huang reportedly admitted to additional affairs and expressed regret during the lengthy board meeting, according to reports published ahead of the reinstatement. The board's willingness to bring him back despite those admissions — and despite the continuing investigation — suggested that directors concluded the company's need for his leadership outweighed the reputational and security questions that remained unresolved.

The reinstatement followed what several reports described as a weekend pressure campaign from the White House, led by Vice President JD Vance. The administration's involvement was itself remarkable: it is rare for a US president and vice president to intervene so directly in the leadership of a publicly traded company, and rarer still for that intervention to be aimed at restoring a chief executive rather than removing one.

A 31 percent surge and a $15 million gain for Trump

The market's reaction was immediate and dramatic. Nvidia's shares surged more than 30 percent on Monday after the reinstatement was announced, and the Nasdaq Composite rose 6.5 percent — one of its strongest single-day gains of the year. The rally reflected not only relief that Huang was back, but also the market's judgement that no other executive could plausibly lead Nvidia through the current moment in the AI boom.

One of the more unusual details of the day was the effect on President Trump's own finances. According to ethics filings and market data, Trump's Nvidia holdings rose by as much as $15 million in value on Monday as the shares surged. The figure, reported on November 24, underscored the unusual entanglement between the president's personal investments, his public advocacy for Huang's return and the market-moving power of his statements.

Trump claimed credit for the reinstatement, telling reporters that he had convinced Nvidia to bring Huang back. The claim drew scrutiny in Washington, where critics questioned the independence of Nvidia's board and asked whether a corporate governance decision of this magnitude should have been shaped by political pressure. Congress, meanwhile, pressed ahead with its own inquiry into Nvidia's sales of chips to China — an investigation that the reinstatement did nothing to resolve.

In Taiwan, where much of Nvidia's hardware is manufactured, the chip sector welcomed Huang's comeback. Industry figures there described him as irreplaceable, a view that reflected his singular role in building the relationships — with Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company above all — that underpin Nvidia's dominance in AI accelerators.

The Megaspeed blacklist

On the same day Huang returned, the US Commerce Department blacklisted Megaspeed International, a Singaporean cloud computing company accused of sending AI chips to China. The decision, which followed months of bipartisan pressure, was announced on Monday and immediately linked in coverage to the Huang affair, because Megaspeed sits at the centre of the alleged chip-smuggling network that formed part of the backdrop to his resignation.

Megaspeed was accused of funnelling advanced AI chips to Chinese buyers in violation of US export controls. The blacklisting means American companies are now barred from supplying the firm without a licence, effectively cutting it off from the US technology ecosystem. The move was presented by the administration as evidence that it was taking the China chip question seriously even as it restored the chief executive whose company makes the chips in question.

The timing raised obvious questions. If the administration was concerned enough about chip smuggling to blacklist a major firm, why was it simultaneously pushing for the return of a chief executive whose personal conduct had raised counterintelligence concerns? The administration's answer, implicit in Trump's statement, was that Huang's return posed no risk to national security — a judgement that his critics in Congress and in the intelligence community have not necessarily shared.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has also said the US is seeking answers on Nvidia's sale of chips to China, and German Foreign Minister Johann Wadephul said he was shocked by Trump's push to reinstate the Nvidia chief. The international reaction reflected a broader unease: America's allies depend on Nvidia hardware too, and they watched the saga unfold with a mixture of relief at the market stability and concern about the governance of a company on which so much now depends.

Who is Jensen Huang?

Jensen Huang, 63, co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and served as its chief executive for more than three decades, transforming it from a maker of graphics cards for video games into the dominant supplier of the processors that power artificial intelligence. Under his leadership, Nvidia's chips became the essential infrastructure of the AI revolution, used by every major technology company to train and run large AI models.

Huang became one of the most recognisable figures in the technology industry, known for his black leather jacket, his showmanship at product launches and his close relationships with the leaders of the world's biggest technology companies. He was also a central figure in the geopolitics of AI: Nvidia's most advanced chips are subject to US export controls designed to prevent China from acquiring them, and Huang himself had repeatedly warned that those controls could backfire by pushing China to develop its own alternatives.

His resignation earlier in November was reported to have followed the discovery of extramarital affairs, including one with a woman US officials believe is a Chinese spy. The reports did not allege that Huang had passed secrets or technology to China, and no charges have been brought against him. But the combination — the chief executive of the world's most important AI chip company, a suspected foreign intelligence operative and the most sensitive technology rivalry of the era — was enough to force his departure.

The board's decision to bring him back, with the investigation still open, suggests that directors weighed those risks against the risk of leaving Nvidia leaderless at a moment when the company faces intense competition, enormous demand and a fast-moving policy environment. It is a gamble that the market, at least on Monday, endorsed emphatically.

The investigation continues

The investigation into the allegations behind Huang's dismissal continues, and its outcome remains the largest open question hanging over the company. Nvidia has not disclosed the scope of the inquiry, who is conducting it or when it will conclude. Until those questions are answered, Huang's return will be shadowed by uncertainty — about what investigators may yet find, about whether new information could force another reversal and about how the episode will be judged by regulators and shareholders.

Congress's separate inquiry into Nvidia's chip sales to China adds another layer of scrutiny. Lawmakers have been pressing the company for answers about how its most advanced processors reached Chinese buyers despite export controls, and the Megaspeed blacklisting is likely to intensify those questions rather than settle them. The administration's own position is complicated by the fact that it intervened to restore Huang while simultaneously sanctioning a firm accused of smuggling his company's chips.

For Nvidia's customers — the cloud providers, AI laboratories and governments that depend on its hardware — the immediate concern is stability. The company's product roadmap, its relationships with manufacturers in Taiwan and its ability to navigate export controls all depend on steady leadership. Huang's return provides that, at least for now. Whether it provides it for the long term will depend on what the continuing investigation finds and on whether the board's judgement about the security risks proves correct.

What to watch next

Several things will determine how this story develops. The first is the outcome of the investigation into the allegations that led to Huang's resignation. Any new findings — particularly any evidence of compromised information or technology — would reopen the crisis immediately.

The second is Nvidia's next earnings report, the company's first since the turmoil. Investors will be watching for any sign that the leadership crisis disrupted sales, delayed product launches or damaged customer relationships. The 31 percent surge on Monday suggests the market expects a clean bill of health, but earnings will provide the first hard evidence.

The third is the export-control picture. The Megaspeed blacklisting, the congressional inquiry and the administration's broader technology rivalry with China all point in the same direction: tighter scrutiny of how advanced AI chips move around the world. Huang has long argued that overly strict controls hurt American companies without stopping China; his return puts that argument back at the centre of the policy debate.

And the fourth is the political dimension. Trump's personal financial gain from the share surge, his claim of credit for the reinstatement and the administration's direct role in a corporate governance decision are all likely to draw further scrutiny — from Congress, from ethics watchdogs and from investors who want to know whether Nvidia's board acted independently. The board's unanimous vote is, on its face, a strong signal of independence. Whether it is seen that way in Washington will depend on what emerges in the coming weeks.

A company, a crisis and a comeback

The Huang saga has been, in the space of less than a month, a story about personal conduct, national security, corporate governance, market power and presidential politics — all converging on a single company. Nvidia is not just another large firm: its chips are the physical foundation of the artificial intelligence systems that governments and companies around the world are racing to build. Who runs it matters far beyond its shareholders.

That is why the board's decision to reinstate Huang, the market's euphoric response and the administration's involvement all carry such weight. The events of Monday resolved the immediate crisis, but they left the deeper questions — about security, about governance and about the relationship between political power and corporate power — unanswered. Those questions will shape not only Nvidia's future, but the future of the AI industry it dominates.

The AI chip race behind the drama

The turmoil at Nvidia unfolded against the backdrop of the most intense technology rivalry in the world: the race to control the supply of advanced AI chips. Nvidia's processors are the single most important input into the construction of large AI systems, and the company's ability to sell them — and to whom — has become a matter of national policy in Washington and in Beijing alike.

Under US export controls, Nvidia's most advanced chips cannot be sold to Chinese buyers. The controls, which have been tightened repeatedly over several years, are designed to slow China's progress in artificial intelligence by denying it access to the hardware on which that progress depends. But the controls have also created a vast grey market, in which chips are routed through third countries and intermediary firms to reach buyers they were never meant to reach.

Megaspeed, the Singaporean firm blacklisted on the same day Huang returned, sits at the centre of that grey market — at least according to the US government. The firm was accused of funnelling advanced AI chips to Chinese buyers, and its blacklisting followed months of bipartisan pressure in Congress. The case against it, and the broader question of how effectively export controls can be enforced when the incentive to evade them runs into the billions of dollars, is one of the most consequential policy questions in the technology world.

Huang himself has been a prominent critic of the controls, arguing that they hurt American companies without stopping China. His argument is that restricting sales pushes Chinese buyers to develop their own alternatives, accelerating the rise of domestic competitors that will eventually challenge Nvidia on its own terms. It is an argument that has found a sympathetic hearing in parts of the administration, which is eager to support American exporters, and a hostile one in Congress, where the priority is slowing China's AI development at any cost.

The tension between those two positions — commercial against strategic — is the central fault line in US technology policy, and Huang's return puts it back in the spotlight. As chief executive of the company whose products are at the centre of the dispute, he is both the most informed advocate for loosening the controls and the most interested one. His critics will argue that his views cannot be separated from his company's bottom line; his supporters will argue that no one understands the market better.

Nvidia's place in the world

To understand why a single chief executive's fate could move the Nasdaq by 6.5 percent in a day, it helps to grasp the scale of Nvidia's position. The company designs the graphics processing units — GPUs — that have become the standard hardware for training and running AI models. Every major AI laboratory, every large cloud provider and a growing number of governments depend on Nvidia's chips, and there is, for the most demanding applications, no ready substitute.

That dominance was built over three decades, and it was built under Huang's leadership. He bet early that GPUs, originally designed for video games, could be repurposed for general computation, and then bet again that artificial intelligence would become the largest computing market in history. Both bets paid off on a scale that few in the industry anticipated, turning Nvidia from a niche hardware company into one of the most valuable firms in the world.

The company's importance now extends well beyond the technology industry. Its chips are a strategic asset, fought over by governments. Its supply chain — centred on manufacturing in Taiwan — is a geopolitical vulnerability. Its product roadmap shapes the plans of every company building AI systems. And its share price has become a barometer for the entire AI boom: when Nvidia rises, the market takes it as a sign that the boom is intact; when it falls, the market worries that the boom is faltering.

That is why the leadership crisis mattered so much, and why its resolution mattered even more. A company of Nvidia's importance cannot afford a vacuum at the top, and the market's 31 percent surge on Monday was, in part, a sigh of relief that the vacuum had been filled by the one person the market believes can do the job. Whether that belief is justified — and whether the board's decision will look wise in a year, or reckless — is a question that only time, and the continuing investigation, can answer.

For now, the world's most important chip company has its chief executive back, its shares have recovered, and the AI boom rolls on. The questions that the crisis raised — about security, about governance, about the entanglement of business and politics — remain open. They will not be resolved by a single board vote, however unanimous, or a single day's trading, however euphoric.
