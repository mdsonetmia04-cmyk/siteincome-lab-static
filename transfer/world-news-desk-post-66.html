TITLE: Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie Just Ended Their $164 Million Winery War — 10 Years After the Split LABELS: Entertainment SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie settled their four-year Chateau Miraval winery lawsuit on Nov 25, ending a decade of legal war ---BODY---

Angelina Jolie at the 2024 Toronto International Film Festival. Jolie and her former husband Brad Pitt announced on November 25, 2026, that they have settled their long-running legal battle over Chateau Miraval, the French winery they once owned together. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0.

Brad Pitt and Angelina Jolie have ended their war. After ten years of separation, four years of lawsuits, a divorce that took eight years to finalise, and a legal fight that dragged some of the darkest allegations of their marriage into public court filings, the two biggest movie stars of their generation have settled their dispute over Chateau Miraval, the French winery they once owned together. The announcement came on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, in a rare joint statement from the former couple, and it was, by the standards of this particular saga, remarkably quiet. There was no victory lap, no press conference, no leak to a friendly outlet. Just two sentences, issued through their representatives, and a request that the world stop speculating. "We have settled out of court in the best interests of our family and will not be commenting further," the statement read. "Any comments in media stories attributed to 'sources' are in fact not coming from either of us, or anyone close to us." With that, one of the longest, bitterest and most closely watched celebrity legal battles of the decade came to an end — not with a verdict, but with a handshake behind closed doors.

The settlement closes a lawsuit that Pitt filed in February 2022, and that Jolie answered with a countersuit eight months later, and that has, for the last four years, been the last open front in a divorce that has dominated Hollywood gossip since the couple separated in September 2016. The divorce itself was finalised in December 2024, eight years after Jolie filed, but the winery fight lived on, and it lived on because it was never really about wine. It was about money, about control, about a $164 million sale, about a non-disclosure agreement, and about allegations of abuse that were first made public only because of this very lawsuit. It was, in the end, a fight about everything that had gone wrong between two people who had once been the most famous couple on the planet, and its ending, quiet and private, is the closest thing to a clean break that either of them has had in a decade.

The winery at the centre of it all

Chateau Miraval is a 1,200-acre estate in the hills of Provence, in the south of France, and it is, by any measure, a remarkable property. Pitt and Jolie bought a controlling interest in it in 2008, when they were still the golden couple of Hollywood, and they married there, in a small private ceremony, in August 2014, with their six children in attendance. The estate, which produces the rosé wine that has become one of the most successful celebrity wines in the world, was, for both of them, more than an investment. For Pitt, it became a passion project, a business he built up over years, pouring money and attention into the vineyard, the winemaking and the brand. For Jolie, it was a family home, a place where the children spent summers, a piece of France that belonged, in some sense, to all eight of them. That is why the fight over it was so bitter, and why it lasted so long: it was not a fight over a line item on a balance sheet, but over a place that had meant something to both of them, and to their children.

The numbers tell part of the story. In his most recent court filing, made in June 2026, Pitt contended that by 2016 he had funded roughly 70 percent of the couple's investment in Miraval, while Jolie had put up the remaining 30 percent. The estate itself, and the wine business built on it, had grown enormously in value under Pitt's stewardship, and the rosé, in particular, had become a global brand, sold in dozens of countries and praised by wine critics who had, at first, been sceptical of a celebrity wine. The business was, in short, worth fighting over, and both sides knew it. But the fight was never only about the valuation. It was about who got to decide what happened to it, and about whether Jolie had the right to sell her half to someone Pitt had never approved, and about what Pitt had demanded in return for buying her out himself.

The $164 million sale that started the war

The dispute began in 2021, when Jolie sold her stake in the winery to Tenute del Mondo, the wine division of the Stoli Group, a spirits company controlled by the Russian-born billionaire Yuri Shefler, the man behind Stolichnaya vodka. The sale, which was reported to be worth $164 million, was, in Pitt's telling, a betrayal. He sued Jolie in February 2022, claiming that she had deliberately damaged the wine company they had bought together in 2008 by selling her half to a "stranger" with a bad reputation — something the couple had, according to the original court filing, explicitly and mutually agreed never to do. Jolie, he claimed, knew and intended that Shefler and his affiliates would try to control the business Pitt had built and undermine his investment in Miraval, and the sale was, in his words, "vindictive" and "unlawful".

Jolie's side told a very different story. She denied that she needed Pitt's approval to sell her stake, and she said that she had, in fact, tried to sell her share back to Pitt first, and that the negotiations had fallen apart for a reason that had nothing to do with money. In her countersuit, filed in October 2022, she claimed that Pitt had demanded, as a condition of buying her out, that she sign a non-disclosure agreement that would have contractually prohibited her from speaking outside of court about Pitt's physical and emotional abuse of her and their children. The NDA, in her telling, was not a standard business document but a gag order, an attempt to buy her silence about what had happened on a private flight in September 2016, the flight that had ended their marriage. She said she had declined to sign it, and that she had sold to Shefler instead, and that Pitt's lawsuit was, in her words, a "vindictive war" waged against her for refusing to be silenced.

The flight, the allegations and the investigation

The allegations that surfaced in Jolie's countersuit were the most serious ever made against Pitt, and they were, until that filing, largely unknown to the public. Jolie and her lawyers said that during the September 2016 flight, Pitt had been physically and emotionally abusive towards her and towards their children, and that the incident had been reported to the FBI and to the Los Angeles County Department of Children and Family Services. Both agencies investigated, and neither brought criminal charges against Pitt, who has denied all of the allegations. Jolie did not press charges. But the descriptions of the alleged abuse, which were set out in detail in her cross-complaint, became public for the first time through this lawsuit, and they transformed a business dispute into something much darker, and much harder to settle.

That is, in the end, what made the Miraval case so unusual, and so difficult to resolve. Most celebrity business disputes are about money, and money can be divided. This one was about a story that neither side could afford to have told in open court, and about a set of allegations that, once made, could never be unmade. Pitt's lawyers insisted that the abuse claims were a smear, deployed to gain leverage in a commercial negotiation. Jolie's lawyers insisted that the NDA demand was proof that Pitt was trying to cover up what had happened. The truth, as in so many divorces, is known only to the two people who were there, and to their children, and it will, now that the case has settled, never be tested in court. Both sides have moved to dismiss their claims against each other, and no judge will ever rule on the allegations that Jolie raised in her counter-filing.

A divorce that took eight years

To understand why the settlement matters, it helps to remember how long this has all taken. Pitt, 62, and Jolie, 51, were together for twelve years, and married for two, before Jolie filed for divorce in September 2016, citing irreconcilable differences. The divorce was not finalised until December 2024, eight years later, after years of fighting over custody of their six children, over the division of their assets, and over the terms on which they would, eventually, stop being married to each other. The winery lawsuit, filed in 2022, ran in parallel with the divorce for two years, and it outlived the divorce by almost two more. For a decade, in other words, the two of them have been in court, or in negotiations, or in the newspapers, fighting over the remains of a relationship that ended in 2016. The settlement, on November 25, 2026, is the first time in ten years that there is no open legal case between them.

That is a significant fact, and it is worth pausing on it, because the public has, for a decade, watched this couple's private life become a public spectacle, and has, for a decade, been invited to take sides. The tabloids chose Pitt, or Jolie, or neither, and the internet chose both, and the story became, over time, less a story about two people and more a story about the way fame works, about the way a marriage between two of the most famous people in the world becomes, when it ends, a kind of public property, fought over in the press as much as in the courts. The joint statement, with its pointed warning that anonymous "sources" are not speaking for either of them, is a rebuke to that whole machinery, and a reminder that the two people at the centre of it have, for years, been unable to control the story of their own lives.

What the settlement does — and does not — resolve

The terms of the settlement were not disclosed, and neither side has said what, if anything, changed hands. What is known is that both sides have moved to dismiss their claims against each other, and that the litigation over the estate is over. What is not over, at least not yet, is Pitt's separate pursuit of Shefler. According to reports, Pitt is still pursuing claims against the Stoli Group billionaire, seeking a court order that would allow him to buy back Jolie's former shares at the same valuation at which she sold them, and asking for unspecified damages. The settlement between Pitt and Jolie does not, in other words, resolve the question of who owns Miraval now, or whether Shefler's purchase will stand, or whether Pitt will, in the end, regain control of the half of the estate that Jolie sold. It resolves the fight between the former spouses. The fight over the winery itself may continue, in a different courtroom, with a different defendant.

That distinction matters, because it is a reminder that the settlement is, in the end, a personal one, not a commercial one. Pitt and Jolie have agreed to stop suing each other. They have not agreed on what happens to the business, and they have not, so far as anyone knows, agreed on a price for anything. The practical effect is that the estate, and the wine, and the brand, remain in the hands of the people who hold them now, and the litigation that will decide their future will be fought, if it is fought at all, between Pitt and Shefler, not between Pitt and Jolie. For the former couple, the significance is simpler: they are done with each other, in court, at last.

Two careers, two very different decades

The settlement also lands at a moment when both Pitt and Jolie are, professionally, in very different places from where they were when the fight began. Pitt, 62, has spent the last few years rebuilding his public image and his career, with a string of well-received films and a production company, Plan B, that continues to make some of the most acclaimed films in Hollywood. Jolie, 51, has spent the same years working as a director, as a humanitarian, and as an actress in films like "Maria", in which she played the opera singer Maria Callas, a performance that earned her some of the best reviews of her career. Both of them have, in other words, moved on, professionally, even as their legal fight continued, and both of them have, in recent years, spoken, in interviews, about the toll that the decade of litigation has taken, and about their desire to be done with it.

That desire is, perhaps, the most human part of this story, and the part that is easiest to overlook in the coverage of the money and the allegations. Ten years is a long time to be fighting with someone you once loved, and four years is a long time to be suing them, and eight years is a long time to be getting divorced. The children, who were between eight and fifteen when their parents separated, are now adults, or nearly so, and they have spent their entire adolescence watching their parents fight in public. The joint statement's phrase — "in the best interests of our family" — is, in that light, not a piece of legal boilerplate but a statement of fact, and a recognition that the family, whatever its shape now, has paid a price for this fight, and that the price has been high enough.

Hollywood's most expensive divorce, by the numbers

It is worth, at the end, setting out the numbers, because they are the numbers that define a decade of legal warfare, and because they deserve to be seen in one place. Pitt and Jolie bought a controlling interest in Chateau Miraval in 2008, and they married there in 2014. They separated in September 2016, and Jolie filed for divorce the same month. The divorce was finalised in December 2024, eight years later. Jolie sold her stake in the winery in 2021, reportedly for $164 million, to Tenute del Mondo, the wine division of the Stoli Group, controlled by the Russian-born billionaire Yuri Shefler. Pitt sued in February 2022, claiming she had broken an agreement that neither would sell without the other's approval. Jolie countersued in October 2022, claiming Pitt had demanded a non-disclosure agreement that would have barred her from speaking about alleged abuse. Pitt contended that by 2016 he had funded roughly 70 percent of the couple's investment in Miraval, and Jolie the remaining 30 percent. The settlement was announced on November 25, 2026, ten years after the separation, four years after the lawsuit, and two years after the divorce was finalised. The terms were not disclosed. Both sides have moved to dismiss their claims. Pitt, 62, and Jolie, 51, have said they will not comment further, and have asked that anonymous "sources" not be treated as speaking for them.

Those are the numbers, and they are, in their way, a kind of epitaph for a marriage that was, for a time, the most famous in the world, and for a divorce that became, over a decade, a kind of public institution. The winery, the wine, the $164 million, the NDA, the allegations, the eight-year divorce and the four-year lawsuit — all of it is now, if the settlement holds, behind them. What remains is the family, and the children, and the two careers, and the question of what Pitt and Jolie do next, now that they are, at last, no longer fighting each other in court. The answer, for the moment, is that they are not saying, and that, after ten years of noise, the silence is itself a kind of news.

The children, and the price of a public divorce

There is one group of people whose names appear, again and again, in the coverage of this settlement, and whose voices have, almost entirely, been absent from it: the six children. They were between eight and fifteen years old when their parents separated in September 2016, and they have spent the whole of their adolescence, and, for the eldest, the beginning of their adult lives, watching their parents fight in public, in court filings, in tabloid headlines, and in the kind of anonymous briefings that the joint statement, on Wednesday, specifically warned against. The eldest are now adults. The youngest are in their late teens. And they have, by all accounts, borne the cost of this decade more heavily than anyone else in the story, which is why the settlement's phrase — "in the best interests of our family" — carries a weight that goes beyond the legal language in which it is written.

It is worth remembering, too, that the children have not been silent. In recent years, several of them have, in small and public ways, distanced themselves from their father, and one of them has, in a legal filing of her own, asked to drop the Pitt surname. Those are not the actions of children who have been untouched by a decade of litigation, and they are not actions that can be undone by a settlement, however welcome that settlement may be. The damage, if that is the right word, is done, and it will take longer than a press release to repair. But the settlement, at the very least, removes one of the reasons for the fighting, and it removes the prospect of a trial in which the family's private life would, once again, have been laid out in public, for the newspapers, and for the internet, to pick over. For the children, that alone is a kind of relief, and it is, perhaps, the most important thing the settlement achieves.

What the tabloids got wrong

There is one more thing worth noting about the settlement, and it is the sentence that has, so far, received the least attention. "Any comments in media stories attributed to 'sources' are in fact not coming from either of us, or anyone close to us." That is, by the standards of celebrity statements, an unusually blunt rebuke, and it is a rebuke that is aimed, squarely, at the machinery that has covered this divorce for a decade. For ten years, the story of Pitt and Jolie has been told, in large part, through anonymous sources, through "friends of" and "insiders" and "people close to" one side or the other, and those sources have, time and again, been used to plant stories, to shape narratives, and to wound the other side, all without anyone having to put their name to anything. The joint statement is, in effect, a declaration that neither side is playing that game any more, and that the stories that appear, from now on, attributed to anonymous sources, should be treated with scepticism. It is a small sentence, and it is, in its way, a radical one, because it asks the press, and the public, to stop believing the very thing that has sustained the coverage of this divorce for a decade.

What happens to Miraval now

The question that remains, and that the settlement does not answer, is what happens to Chateau Miraval itself. The estate, and the wine business built on it, are, by all accounts, thriving, and the rosé, in particular, has become a global brand, sold in dozens of countries and praised by critics. But the ownership is, as of today, split between Pitt and the Stoli Group, and the legal question of whether Shefler's purchase will stand, and whether Pitt will be allowed to buy back the shares at the price Jolie sold them for, is still open. If Pitt succeeds, he will regain control of the whole estate, and the business he built will be his again. If he fails, he will remain a co-owner with a man he has described, in court filings, as a "stranger" with a bad reputation, and the future of the winery will be decided, in part, by people who were not there when it was built.

Either way, the settlement between Pitt and Jolie is a turning point, and it is a turning point that will be remembered, in Hollywood, as the end of one of the longest and most bitter celebrity divorces in memory. It is also, for the two people at its centre, a kind of freedom, and a kind of relief. They have spent a decade in court, and they have spent a decade in the newspapers, and they have spent a decade being asked, in interviews, about a fight that neither of them, in the end, wanted to keep fighting. Now they do not have to. The statement said they would not comment further, and they have, so far, kept their word. The silence, after ten years of noise, is the news, and it is, for once, good news.
