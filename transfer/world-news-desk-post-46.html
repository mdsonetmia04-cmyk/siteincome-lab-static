TITLE: Senate Is Investigating JD Vance Over a $2 Billion Venezuela Oil Deal — the $300,000 Donor, the Montana Meeting and the December 14 Deadline LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: The Senate is investigating whether Vice President JD Vance helped donor John Addison and Vitol win a $2B Venezuela oil deal. Documents due Dec 14. ---BODY---

Vice President JD Vance. A Senate committee is investigating whether he used his office to help a major political donor secure a lucrative contract to trade Venezuelan oil. Photo: Official White House portrait, March 2026, Wikimedia Commons.

A United States Senate committee is investigating whether Vice President JD Vance used his office to help a major political donor win one of the most lucrative oil contracts in recent American history. The investigation, launched by the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee, centres on a meeting that may — or may not — have taken place at an ultra-exclusive ski resort in Montana, a phone call from the Energy Secretary two days after American troops captured Venezuela's president, and a deal to sell 50 million barrels of Venezuelan crude worth about $2 billion. At the heart of the story is a friendship between the Vice President and a commodities trader named John Addison, who gave $300,000 to Mr Vance's Senate campaign in 2022 and whose family donated $7 million to groups supporting Donald Trump's 2024 presidential campaign. The committee has demanded documents from Mr Vance's office by December 14, 2026. Mr Vance's office says the meeting at the centre of the investigation never happened. The Senate, it seems, intends to find out for itself.

The investigation was set in motion by a letter from Senator Maggie Hassan, a Democrat from New Hampshire, who will become chair of the Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee in January, when the new Congress is seated. In the letter, reviewed by Reuters, Ms Hassan wrote that she was acting with "serious concern that you may have used your position as Vice President to help a major political donor secure lucrative business opportunities involving Venezuelan oil". The letter was sent after an exclusive Reuters report, published on November 11, detailed a meeting between Mr Vance and Mr Addison a week before the United States invaded Venezuela in January and captured the country's authoritarian leader, Nicolás Maduro. The Senate committee has now formally questioned the official statements made by the Trump administration about the Venezuela oil trade, and will ask Mr Vance's office for a number of documents to be submitted by December 14.

The story begins, according to the Reuters report, in December 2025, at the Yellowstone Club in Montana — an ultra-exclusive ski and golf resort where membership costs millions and the guest list is among the most private in America. Mr Vance was on a trip to the club that month, and, according to a person familiar with the matter, he met there with John Addison, a gasoline trader with Vitol, the world's largest independent energy trading company. Mr Addison was described by Reuters as "an old friend and early financier of his political career". The report also cited flight records that put Mr Addison's private jet and the vice-presidential plane at Bozeman Yellowstone International Airport at the same time. According to the Reuters source, Mr Vance and Mr Addison talked about Venezuela — and more specifically, about how fast Venezuelan crude could be traded in the event that the United States took control of the country's oil industry. The meeting took place, the report said, a week before the United States invaded Venezuela in January and captured Mr Maduro.

The invasion, the phone call and the $2 billion deal

On January 3, 2026, American forces invaded Venezuela and captured President Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, in an operation that left close to a hundred people dead, according to the authorities in Caracas. The invasion put the United States in control of the world's largest proven oil reserves — and raised an immediate question: who would sell Venezuela's oil, and on what terms? The answer came with remarkable speed. Mr Addison has confirmed to Reuters that on January 5 — two days after the operation that ousted Mr Maduro — the United States Energy Secretary, Chris Wright, called him at his desk in Houston with what the report described as an irresistible offer. Soon after, Vitol and Trafigura, the two giant commodities trading houses, received special licences from the Trump administration to trade Venezuelan crude.

The terms of the deal were extraordinary. Reports from earlier this year said that Vitol and Trafigura had struck an agreement with the White House to sell 50 million barrels of Venezuelan crude, valued at some $2 billion at the time, soon after the invasion. Both companies have declined to disclose the terms of their agreements with PDVSA, Venezuela's state oil company, and the Energy Department has said that Vitol was chosen because it is the world's largest energy commodity trader. But the scale of the profit is now part of the Senate's inquiry: according to the letter from Senator Hassan, Vitol has obtained hundreds of millions of dollars in profits from marketing Venezuelan oil, with profit margins that in recent years have reached four times the operating margins reported by the company. In other words, the contract was not merely large — it was, by the standards of the commodities business, extraordinarily lucrative.

The timeline is what makes the story so sensitive. A meeting in December, in which the Vice President and a Vitol trader reportedly discussed how fast Venezuelan crude could be traded if the United States took control of the oil industry. An invasion on January 3. A phone call from the Energy Secretary on January 5, offering the trader a licence to sell that oil. And a deal, within weeks, to sell 50 million barrels worth $2 billion. Each step, taken alone, can be explained. Taken together, they raise the question the Senate is now asking: did the Vice President's friendship with a major donor shape who got to sell Venezuela's oil?

What the Senate wants — and what the Vice President's office says

Senator Hassan's letter demands a detailed record of all communications and documentation exchanged between Mr Vance, Vitol and Mr Addison, as well as other papers and messages aimed at analysing possible conflicts of interest in the process by which the government decided to award the licence to the oil company. The documents are due by Monday, December 14, 2026. The letter also sets out the financial ties that make the inquiry so pointed: Mr Addison provided $300,000 to Mr Vance's campaign for the Senate in 2022, and both he and his wife donated $7 million to groups that supported Mr Trump's 2024 presidential campaign. Ms Hassan, who will chair the committee from January, wrote that as a member of the committee and its next chair, she is examining whether current legislation makes it possible to adequately prevent, disclose and remedy conflicts of interest in which senior officials of the executive branch may incur, and whether it is necessary to adopt new legislative measures to strengthen that regulatory framework.

Mr Vance's office has issued a formal denial. His press secretary, Luke Schroeder, said in a statement: "The alleged airport meeting never happened." The office has referred to the episode as "the alleged airport meeting", and both the office of the Vice President and Mr Addison have denied the Reuters report. Mr Addison, for his part, told Reuters: "And anyone who says so is absolutely making it up because I've never told a single soul that was the case." The Energy Department has said that Vice President Vance and Secretary Wright never discussed Venezuelan oil sales between themselves, and that Vitol was chosen because it is the world's largest energy commodity trader. Vitol and Trafigura have said they had no advance knowledge of the military operation.

The denials, however, have not ended the matter — and in some ways they have sharpened it. The Senate committee has questioned the official statements made by the Trump administration about the Venezuela oil trade, and the investigation will now test those statements against documents. The flight records cited by Reuters — placing Mr Addison's private jet and the vice-presidential plane at Bozeman Yellowstone International Airport at the same time — are a matter of public record, and they are the kind of evidence that is difficult to explain away. The December 14 deadline gives the committee a fixed point: either Mr Vance's office produces the communications the committee has asked for, or it refuses, and the refusal itself becomes part of the story.

Vitol, Trafigura and the business of Venezuelan oil

To understand why this contract matters so much, it helps to understand who Vitol is. Vitol is the world's largest independent energy trader, a private company based in Switzerland and London that buys and sells more oil than many countries produce. It does not drill wells or own refineries in the traditional sense; it trades — buying crude where it is cheap, selling it where it is dear, chartering tankers, storing oil, and profiting from the difference. In a normal market, that is a low-margin business, measured in cents per barrel. In a disrupted market — a war, a sanctions regime, a sudden change of government — the margins can be enormous, because the trader who can move oil when nobody else can is worth a fortune. Venezuela, with the world's largest proven reserves and an oil industry hollowed out by years of mismanagement and sanctions, is exactly that kind of market.

Trafigura, the other company named in the deal, is Vitol's great rival, and the two firms dominate the global trade in oil, metals and other commodities. Both received special licences from the Trump administration to trade Venezuelan crude, and both have declined to disclose the terms of their agreements with PDVSA. That secrecy is normal in the commodities business, where trading terms are closely guarded commercial secrets. But it is also what makes the Senate's inquiry so difficult — and so important. Without the documents, the public cannot know how the licences were awarded, what the traders paid, what they earned, or whether anyone in the administration steered the business toward a friend. The committee's demand for documents is, in effect, a demand to see inside a deal that has so far been conducted behind closed doors.

The human cost of the underlying events should not be lost in the financial detail. The operation that captured Mr Maduro on January 3 left close to a hundred people dead, according to the authorities in Caracas, and up to 100 people by other accounts. The invasion was, and remains, one of the most consequential American military actions of the decade — a decision to remove the leader of a sovereign country by force, and to take control of its oil industry. The question of who profited from that decision, and how, is not a footnote to the invasion. It is central to how history will judge it. If the Senate finds that the Vice President helped a donor win the contract, the invasion will be remembered differently than if the contract was awarded on the merits. That is why the investigation matters, and why it will be followed so closely.

The politics: a Democratic committee, a Republican vice president

The politics of the investigation are as charged as the facts. The committee is acting now, in the lame-duck session, but its real power will arrive in January, when the Democrats take control of the House and Ms Hassan takes the chair of the Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee. The Democrats won a historic House majority in the midterms — at least 259 seats, the largest since 1990 — and the Senate investigation of Mr Vance is one of the first signs of how they intend to use their new power. The committee can demand documents, hold hearings, subpoena witnesses and publish findings. It cannot bring criminal charges, but it can refer matters to the Justice Department, and it can do something almost as powerful: it can put the facts on the public record.

The administration's response, so far, has been denial and dismissal. The Vice President's office has said the meeting never happened. The Energy Department has said the Vice President and the Energy Secretary never discussed Venezuelan oil sales. Vitol has said it had no advance knowledge of the operation. Those denials may prove to be entirely accurate — and the Senate's inquiry may end with a finding that the contract was awarded fairly, on the merits, to the world's largest energy trader, and that the Vice President's friendship with Mr Addison played no role at all. That is a possible outcome, and it should be stated plainly. But the denials have not stopped the investigation, because the committee has evidence — the flight records, the timing, the money — that it believes deserves an answer in documents rather than in statements.

There is also a broader question at stake, and it is the question Ms Hassan put at the centre of her letter: whether the current law is adequate to prevent, disclose and remedy conflicts of interest among senior officials of the executive branch. The federal ethics rules were written for a world in which senior officials disclosed their finances, recused themselves from matters affecting their donors, and kept their distance from the businesses of their friends. The Venezuela oil deal tests those rules in a new way — not because anyone has been found guilty of anything, but because the facts, as reported, sit so close to the line. A Vice President, a donor, a meeting at a private ski club, a phone call two days after an invasion, a $2 billion contract: if the rules permit all of that without disclosure, then the rules may need to change. That is the legislative question the committee will consider, whatever it finds about Mr Vance.

John Addison: the trader at the centre of the story

John Addison is not a household name, but in the world of oil trading he is a significant figure. A gasoline trader with Vitol, he was, according to Reuters, an old friend of Mr Vance and an early financier of his political career. The $300,000 he gave to Mr Vance's 2022 Senate campaign was a substantial contribution, and the $7 million he and his wife gave to groups supporting Mr Trump's 2024 campaign put them among the significant donors of that election. Mr Addison left Vitol about three months after the January phone call from the Energy Secretary, according to one account — a departure that has itself become part of the story, though its reasons have not been publicly explained.

Mr Addison's own words are the most direct denial in the record. "And anyone who says so is absolutely making it up because I've never told a single soul that was the case," he told Reuters, in response to the report that he and Mr Vance had discussed Venezuelan crude at the Yellowstone Club. It is a categorical denial, and it deserves to be reported as such. But it is also a denial of a conversation, not of the underlying facts that the Senate is investigating: the flight records, the phone call from Secretary Wright on January 5, the licence, the $2 billion deal and the hundreds of millions in profits. Those facts are, for the most part, not in dispute. What is in dispute is what they mean — and whether they add up to a favour, a coincidence, or something in between.

What happens next: December 14 and beyond

The immediate calendar is clear. Mr Vance's office has until Monday, December 14, 2026, to produce the documents Senator Hassan has requested: communications between the Vice President or his staff and Mr Addison or Vitol, and the other papers and messages the committee has asked for. What happens on that date will set the course of the investigation. If the documents are produced, the committee will examine them and decide whether they support the administration's account or contradict it. If they are withheld, the committee will have to decide whether to escalate — and in January, with a new chair and a new House majority behind her, Ms Hassan will have considerably more leverage than she has today.

Beyond December 14, the investigation will unfold against a crowded political calendar. The new Congress is seated in January, the Democrats take control of the House, and Ms Hassan takes the chair of the committee. The administration, meanwhile, is dealing with the aftermath of a midterm defeat, a Supreme Court ruling that struck down its tariffs, and a series of other investigations — including the question of whether the Nvidia chief executive, Jensen Huang, faced a federal probe, which the Attorney General declined to clarify this week. The Vance investigation will not be the only story in Washington in January. But it may be the most personal, because it goes to the integrity of the second-highest office in the land.

Why the documents matter more than the denials

There is a reason the Senate is asking for paper rather than accepting statements, and it is not partisanship. In an investigation of this kind, a press statement is a claim; a document is evidence. An email, a calendar entry, a flight manifest, a call log — these are the records that can confirm or refute a timeline in a way that no spokesperson can. The flight records cited by Reuters are a good example: they are not an accusation, they are a fact, and they place two aircraft at the same airport at the same time. The committee's job is to gather the rest of the record — the communications between Mr Vance's office and Mr Addison, the records of the Energy Department's decision to license Vitol, the terms of the deal with PDVSA — and to see whether the whole picture supports the denials or undermines them. That is slow, unglamorous work, and it is the only work that can settle a question like this one.

It is also worth noting what the committee is not doing. It has not accused Mr Vance of a crime. It has not subpoenaed him. It has not held a hearing, named a witness, or published a finding. It has asked for documents, by a date, and it has explained why it is asking. That restraint matters, because the credibility of the investigation will depend on it. If the documents show that the Vice President played no role in the award of the contract, the committee will have to say so — and the administration will be entitled to say that it was right all along. If the documents show otherwise, the consequences will be serious, and they will be serious precisely because the investigation was conducted carefully. Either way, the documents are the point. December 14 is the date to watch.

For the public, the lesson of this story so far is a simple one: the facts matter, and they are still being established. The meeting may have happened or it may not have. The flight records may prove a meeting or merely a coincidence of travel. The phone call from the Energy Secretary may have been the routine business of government or the delivery of a favour. The $2 billion contract may have been awarded on merit or on friendship. The Senate's job is to find out which of those things is true — and to put the evidence on the record so that the public can judge for itself. Until December 14, and until the documents arrive, the honest position is to report what is known, to report what is denied, and to wait for the evidence. That is what this article has done. The next chapter belongs to the committee, the documents and the Vice President's office — and to the question, now formally asked, of whether the second-highest office in the United States was used to help a friend get rich.
