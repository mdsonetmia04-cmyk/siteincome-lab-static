TITLE: Puerto Rico's Supreme Court Just Cancelled Luma's Power Contract in a 5-4 Vote — 3.2 Million People Ask Who Keeps the Lights On LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Puerto Rico's Supreme Court voted 5-4 to cancel Luma Energy's contract to run the island's grid, giving officials one year to find a new operator. ---BODY---

Governor Jenniffer Gonzalez-Colon of Puerto Rico. Her government sued to cancel Luma Energy's contract to run the island's power grid, and on Wednesday the island's Supreme Court ruled 5-4 in her favour — plunging 3.2 million people into a year of uncertainty over who will keep their lights on. Photo: United States House of Representatives, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Puerto Rico's Supreme Court has narrowly ruled in favour of cancelling the government's contract with Luma Energy, the private company that has run the island's fragile power grid for the past five years. The 5-4 ruling, running to more than 200 pages and made public on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, plunges the United States territory into further uncertainty, as millions of residents wonder who will take over from Luma while power outages persist across the island and crews continue to repair a grid that was razed by Hurricane Maria in 2017.

The court ruled that the contract was never valid, because a key supplemental agreement between the government and Luma never received approval from the board of the island's public-private partnership agency. The decision gives the government one year to put a new operator in place — and it requires Luma to keep the lights on in the meantime, participating in an "orderly transfer" of the operation over the next twelve months. Luma has ten days to ask the court to reconsider its ruling.

Governor Jenniffer Gonzalez-Colon, who made cancelling the Luma contract one of her priorities and sued the company nearly a year ago, welcomed the decision. The island's financial oversight board warned that it creates enormous uncertainty, with no clear successor in sight. And two dissenting judges warned of "monumental collateral consequences". The governor's answer was blunt: "There will be no chaos."

The 5-4 ruling, in 200 pages

The case turned on a question that sounds technical but proved decisive: whether the government's agreements with Luma had been properly approved. When Puerto Rico privatised its power grid after Hurricane Maria, it gave Luma Energy — a consortium based in Canada and Texas — a 15-year deal to take over the power lines, promising that the company would rebuild the grid stronger and improve a service that was already notoriously unreliable. Luma took over in June 2021.

But the deal was not a single document. It was a series of agreements, including a supplemental agreement that extended the arrangement — and, the court found, that supplemental agreement never received the approval it needed from the board of the Puerto Rico Public-Private Partnerships Authority, the agency that oversees such deals. Because that approval was missing, the court held, the contract was never valid. The ruling was 5-4, with the majority concluding that the procedural flaw was fatal, and the dissenters arguing that the consequences of voiding the contract would be catastrophic.

The ruling runs to more than 200 pages, and it is not a simple document. It deals with the intricacies of Puerto Rican administrative law, the powers of the partnerships authority, and the history of a deal that was negotiated in the chaotic aftermath of a hurricane that had destroyed the island's infrastructure. But its conclusion is simple enough to state: the company that has run Puerto Rico's power grid for five years was never legally entitled to do so, and the government now has a year to find someone who is.

The dissenters' warning

Not all of the court's judges agreed. Judge Angel Colon Perez, who voted against the cancellation, asserted that the final ruling stemmed from "an excessive eagerness to satisfy a capricious request from the leader currently in power" — an apparent reference to the governor. The charge was extraordinary: that the court had bent to political pressure, and that the legal reasoning was a pretext for a decision made for political reasons.

Judge Roberto Feliberti Cintron, who was part of the majority, sharply denied the allegations. But the dissent did not stop there. Two dissenting judges warned of "monumental collateral consequences" — a phrase that captures the scale of what the ruling sets in motion. Puerto Rico's power grid is not a normal utility. It is a system that was already broken before Luma arrived, that was destroyed by a hurricane, and that has been the subject of one of the largest rebuilding efforts in American history. Pulling the operator out of it, even over a year, is not a routine administrative change. It is a gamble with the electricity supply of 3.2 million people.

The dissenters' concern is not abstract. The island's grid is still being rebuilt, nine years after Maria. Outages are a fact of daily life. Businesses run generators. Hospitals keep backup power ready. Schools close when the grid fails. Any disruption in the handover — any gap between Luma's departure and a successor's arrival — would be felt immediately, in homes, hospitals and businesses across the island. The court gave the government a year. A year sounds like a long time. In the world of power grids, it is not.

Luma's five years

To understand why the ruling has divided the island, it helps to understand what Luma inherited — and what it did with it. When Hurricane Maria flattened Puerto Rico's already rickety power grid in September 2017, it did not merely damage the system. It destroyed it. The storm knocked out power to the entire island, and some residents waited almost a year for electricity to return. The grid that Maria destroyed had been crumbling for decades under the management of the Puerto Rico Electric Power Authority, known as PREPA, a public utility weighed down by debt, corruption and neglect.

The island's government decided to privatise. In 2021, it handed the transmission and distribution system — the poles, the wires, the substations, the business of getting electricity from power plants to homes — to Luma Energy, a consortium formed by the Canadian company ATCO and the American company Quanta Services. The deal was for 15 years. Luma promised to rebuild the grid stronger, to modernise it, and to improve a service that Puerto Ricans had come to expect would fail them.

Five years later, the verdict on Luma's performance depends on who you ask. The company says it inherited a system in ruins and has made real progress: thousands of miles of lines repaired, hundreds of substations rebuilt, and a grid that is stronger than the one it took over. Its critics — and they are many, including the governor — say the opposite: that outages remain constant, that prices have reached record highs, and that the company has failed to deliver on the promises that justified privatisation in the first place.

The numbers at the centre of the dispute are stark. When the lawsuit was filed in December 2025, Governor Gonzalez said Luma had nearly $11 billion in federal funds at its disposal to rebuild the grid, but had only been able to recover about $550 million in reimbursements from the United States Federal Emergency Management Agency. The gap between those two figures — $11 billion available, $550 million recovered — became the governor's central charge: that Luma had been given the money to rebuild the grid and had not spent it, or had not spent it fast enough, while Puerto Ricans sat in the dark.

$11 billion and $550 million

The federal money is the key to understanding both the case and the anger behind it. After Hurricane Maria, the United States government committed billions of dollars to rebuilding Puerto Rico's power grid — one of the largest disaster-recovery investments in American history. The money was there. The question was whether it was being used.

The governor's argument was that it was not. Luma, she said, had nearly $11 billion in federal funds at its disposal and had recovered only some $550 million from FEMA — roughly five cents on the dollar. The implication was clear: the company was sitting on the money while the grid remained broken, and Puerto Ricans were paying the price in outages and in bills.

Luma's defenders tell a different story. They say the federal reimbursement process is slow by design, that the money is tied to specific projects with specific approvals, and that the $550 million figure reflects what has been reimbursed so far — not what has been spent, or what is in the pipeline. They point to the work that has been done: lines rebuilt, substations modernised, vegetation cleared from rights of way. And they note that the grid Luma inherited was not merely damaged but effectively destroyed, and that rebuilding it was always going to take a decade or more, not five years.

Both accounts contain elements of truth, which is why the argument has never been settled. What is not in dispute is that the grid is still unreliable, that outages are still frequent, and that Puerto Ricans still pay some of the highest electricity prices in the United States. Those facts, more than any legal argument, explain why the governor's campaign to cancel the contract found such a ready audience — and why the court's ruling was greeted with street celebrations in San Juan, where crowds waved Puerto Rican flags and held signs reading "Fuera Luma!" — "Out with Luma!"

Who takes over?

The hardest question the ruling raises is also the simplest: if not Luma, then who? The court gave the government one year to find a new operator, and the governor has said the work of finding one will start immediately. But there is no obvious successor waiting in the wings. Running Puerto Rico's power grid is not a job that companies line up for. The system is fragile, the regulatory environment is complex, the politics are poisonous, and the history of the last five years is a warning to any company thinking of taking it on.

The island's financial oversight board — the body created by Congress to manage Puerto Rico's debt crisis — has warned that the decision creates enormous uncertainty, with no clear successor and no clear plan for the transition. The board's concern is financial as well as operational: the grid's rebuilding is funded by federal money, and the flow of that money depends on stable management and credible plans. A year of uncertainty could slow the rebuilding further, or complicate the financing of the work that remains.

There is also the question of what happens to Luma's employees. The company employs thousands of people on the island — line workers, engineers, customer service staff — and the transition will have to account for them. The court said Luma was legally obliged to participate in the "orderly transfer" of the operation over the next year, which means the company must keep the lights on and help hand the system over, even as it loses the contract it fought to keep. Luma has said it disagrees with the ruling and is weighing all of its legal options, including asking the court to reconsider within the ten days allowed.

The most likely outcomes are all imperfect. The government could find another private operator, but the search will take time, and any new company will face the same problems Luma faced. It could return the grid to public management, but the public utility that ran it before — PREPA — was bankrupt, and its record was worse than Luma's. Or it could try a hybrid model, splitting the system between public and private hands. None of these options is quick, and none of them is certain to work better than the arrangement the court has just voided.

What it means for 3.2 million people

For the people of Puerto Rico, the ruling is both a victory and a risk. It is a victory because Luma had become, for many islanders, the symbol of everything that was wrong with the grid: the outages, the bills, the sense that a private company was profiting from a public disaster while the public sat in the dark. The street celebrations in San Juan were real, and they reflected a genuine feeling that the island had taken back control of something that belonged to it.

But it is also a risk, because the thing the island has taken back is a broken grid, and the year ahead will be a year of transition — a year in which the company that runs the grid is also the company that has just lost the right to run it, a year in which a new operator must be found, vetted and installed, and a year in which the federal money that funds the rebuilding must keep flowing through a system in flux. The governor has promised there will be no chaos. That is a promise that will be tested every day for the next twelve months, every time the lights go out.

The story of Puerto Rico's power grid is, in the end, a story about a disaster that never really ended. Hurricane Maria struck in September 2017. It killed nearly 3,000 people — a toll that was not fully acknowledged for almost a year — and it destroyed the island's infrastructure. Nine years later, the grid is still being rebuilt, the money is still being argued over, and the question of who should run it is still being fought in court. The Supreme Court's ruling is the latest chapter in that story, and it is not the last. The transition year will bring its own disputes, its own delays and its own outages. And the people who will live through it are the same people who have lived through all of it: 3.2 million Americans, on an island in the Caribbean, waiting for the lights to stay on.

The road from Maria to Luma

The privatisation of Puerto Rico's power grid was born out of failure — not one failure, but a long accumulation of them. For decades, the Puerto Rico Electric Power Authority, PREPA, had been the island's power company: a public utility that was also, in practice, a political machine. It borrowed heavily, maintained poorly, and employed generously. By the time Hurricane Maria struck in 2017, PREPA was already effectively bankrupt, carrying some $9 billion in debt, and its infrastructure was so degraded that engineers warned a major storm could destroy it. Maria did exactly that.

The storm made landfall on September 20, 2017, as a Category 4 hurricane, with winds of 155 miles per hour. It knocked out power to the entire island — all 3.2 million people, in a single day. What followed was the longest blackout in American history. Some residents waited eleven months for electricity to return. Hospitals ran on generators. Businesses closed. People died — not only in the storm itself, but in the months that followed, from lack of power for medical equipment, from heat, from the slow collapse of the systems that keep a modern society alive. The official death toll, revised after a study by George Washington University, was estimated at 2,975 people.

In the aftermath, the island's government concluded that the public utility could not be fixed — that PREPA was too indebted, too politicised and too broken to rebuild the grid it had allowed to crumble. The decision to privatise was controversial from the start. Supporters said only a private company, with access to capital and expertise, could rebuild a system that had been destroyed. Critics said the island was handing a public necessity to a private company that would put profit before service, and that the people who had suffered through Maria would end up paying higher bills for the same unreliable power.

The contract with Luma was signed in 2020 and took effect in June 2021. From the beginning, it was shadowed by protest. Puerto Ricans marched against it, unions opposed it, and politicians — including the woman who would become governor — campaigned against it. Gonzalez-Colon made cancelling the contract a central promise of her campaign for governor, and when she took office, she moved quickly: the lawsuit to nullify the contract was filed in December 2025, less than a year after her inauguration. The court's ruling is, in that sense, the fulfilment of a campaign promise — and a measure of how deeply the anger at Luma runs in Puerto Rican politics.

The year ahead

The twelve months the court has given the government will be among the most consequential in the island's recent history. In that year, officials must find a new operator, negotiate a new contract, secure the approvals that were missing from the last one, and manage a transition from a company that has just been told it was never legally entitled to the job. Each of those steps is difficult on its own. Together, they amount to rebuilding the governance of the island's electricity system while the system itself continues to operate — and continues, some days, to fail.

The first test will come quickly. Luma has ten days to ask the court to reconsider its ruling, and the company has said it is weighing all of its legal options. A motion for reconsideration is a long shot — courts rarely reverse themselves — but it would, at a minimum, add weeks of uncertainty to a process that already has too little time. Beyond that, Luma could seek other legal remedies, and the transition could become entangled in litigation even as the clock runs down on the one-year deadline.

The second test will be the search for a successor. The government will need to run a procurement process, evaluate bidders, and negotiate terms — all while the oversight board watches the finances and the public watches the lights. Any new operator will inherit the same fragile grid, the same federal funding pipeline, and the same political pressures that shaped Luma's tenure. The lesson of the last five years is that running Puerto Rico's power grid is a job that punishes its holder, whoever that holder is. The next operator will know that, and will price its bid accordingly — or will not bid at all.

And then there is the money. The nearly $11 billion in federal funds, and the $550 million recovered so far, will remain the central argument of the next year. The governor will press the case that the money must be spent faster. The new operator will argue that the spending is constrained by federal rules. The oversight board will watch the debt. And the people of Puerto Rico will watch their bills and their blackouts, measuring the promises of the transition against the reality of their daily lives.
