TITLE: Dell Just Doubled Its Sales to $54.9 Billion — and Its AI Backlog Hit a Record $132.3 Billion LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Dell revenue doubled to $54.9 billion as AI server sales jumped 291% — backlog hit a record $132.3 billion and guidance rose to $202 billion. ---BODY---

Michael Dell, the founder and chief executive of Dell Technologies. Dell reported record quarterly revenue of $54.9 billion on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, more than double the figure of a year earlier. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Dell Technologies has just reported the most extraordinary quarter in its forty-two year history. The Texas computer maker, founded by Michael Dell in his university dormitory room in 1984, announced on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, that its revenue for the third quarter of fiscal 2027 had more than doubled to a record 54.9 billion dollars, up 103 percent from a year earlier, and far above the 49.32 billion dollars that Wall Street had expected. Its adjusted earnings per share rose 265 percent to 9.45 dollars, against the 6.59 dollars that analysts had forecast, and its GAAP earnings per share rose 287 percent to 8.82 dollars. The company raised its forecast for the full year for the third time this year, lifting its revenue guidance to 202 billion dollars from 192 billion, and its board approved a further 25 billion dollars of share buybacks. Behind the numbers lies a transformation: Dell, the company that the world knew as a maker of personal computers, is now, by its own account, a different company — one whose profit and growth are driven by the infrastructure of artificial intelligence, and whose order book for AI servers has swollen to a record 132.3 billion dollars.

The results stunned even a market that has grown accustomed to spectacular numbers from the technology sector. Dell's revenue of 54.94 billion dollars was not merely a record; it was more than double the 27 billion dollars the company reported in the same quarter a year earlier, and it exceeded the consensus forecast by more than 5 billion dollars — a margin of error that, in a company of Dell's size, is almost unheard of. The company's shares rose about 5 percent in extended trading after the results were announced, a gain that would have been larger still had the broader market not been falling on the same day. Dell's stock has been one of the hottest in the S&P 500 this year, according to Morningstar, and Tuesday's results gave investors another reason to believe that the rally has further to run. The question now is not whether Dell is benefiting from the AI boom. It is whether any company, in any industry, is benefiting from it more.

The numbers that stunned Wall Street

The headline figures, taken from Dell's own earnings release, are worth setting out in full, because they are the foundation of everything that follows. Revenue for the quarter ended October 30 was 54.9 billion dollars, up 103 percent year over year. Diluted earnings per share were 8.82 dollars, up 287 percent. Non-GAAP diluted earnings per share were 9.45 dollars, up 265 percent. The company's Infrastructure Solutions Group, the division that houses its server and storage operations, reported record revenue of 42.0 billion dollars, up 198 percent year over year. Within that division, revenue from AI-optimised servers was 22.0 billion dollars, up 291 percent year over year, while revenue from traditional servers and networking was 15.0 billion dollars, up 235 percent. The company's personal computer business, by contrast, grew by only 3 percent, to 12.89 billion dollars, compared with growth of 20 percent in the previous quarter — a sign that customers are prioritising spending on servers and storage over new laptops and desktops.

The scale of the beat against expectations is what caught Wall Street's attention. Analysts, according to data compiled by LSEG, had expected revenue of 49.32 billion dollars and adjusted earnings of 6.59 dollars a share. Dell delivered 54.94 billion dollars and 9.45 dollars — beating the revenue forecast by more than 11 percent and the earnings forecast by more than 43 percent. In a market where a beat of a few percent is enough to move a stock, a beat of this size is a sensation. The company's cash flow was equally strong: it generated record year-to-date cash flow from operations of 10.8 billion dollars, and ended the quarter with 16.3 billion dollars in cash and investments, after a 2 billion dollar sequential increase. Its core leverage ratio — a measure of how much debt it carries relative to its earnings — stood at just 0.7 times, a conservative figure that gives the company ample room to invest, to acquire, or to return money to shareholders.

The 132.3 billion dollar backlog

The most important number in Dell's earnings release was not its revenue, or its earnings, or its forecast. It was its backlog. Dell closed the third quarter with a record 132.3 billion dollars in AI server backlog — orders that customers have placed and that Dell has not yet delivered — after logging 60.2 billion dollars in new orders during the three-month period. The backlog is, by itself, about two-thirds of the revenue that Dell expects to generate in the entire fiscal year, and it is the clearest evidence that the demand for AI infrastructure is not slowing down. It is also, in the company's telling, a carefully vetted backlog: every order, Dell says, is rigorously checked for customer quality and for the customer's access to capital, land and power before it is accepted, and checked again before it is shipped.

That vetting matters, because the AI infrastructure boom has raised questions that go beyond any single company's results. The data centres being built to train and run AI models are enormously expensive, and they are being financed, in part, by debt. If some of the companies placing the orders cannot pay for them — if their business models do not generate the revenues they have promised — then the orders will be cancelled, and the backlog will melt away. Dell's insistence on vetting its customers is, in part, a response to that concern: a signal to investors that its backlog is real, that its customers can pay, and that its orders will turn into revenue. The company's chief operating officer, Jeff Clarke, made the point explicitly in the earnings release, saying that the company had ended the quarter with a record 132.3 billion dollar AI backlog after booking 60.2 billion dollars of orders, "each rigorously vetted for customer quality and access to capital, land and power."

Clarke also explained why Dell believes it can convert that backlog into revenue faster than its competitors. "Customers turn that backlog into revenue sooner because we test the complete rack before it ships and stand it up within six hours of delivery, which shortens time-to-first-token," he said — "time-to-first-token" being the industry's term for how quickly a customer can start using an AI system after it is delivered. The claim is a technical one, but its commercial logic is simple: in a market where every week of delay costs money, the company that can deliver fastest wins the most business. Dell says that more than 7,500 customers are now deploying its AI Factories — its term for the integrated systems of servers, storage and software that it sells to companies building AI infrastructure — and that demand "continues to accelerate and broaden globally."

A company transformed

The results confirm a transformation that has been under way at Dell for several years, but that has accelerated dramatically in the last twelve months. "We expect record full-year revenue of 202 billion dollars, up 78 percent. We are now a different company, with Infrastructure Solutions Group driving the vast majority of our profit and growth," said Jeff Clarke, Dell's vice chairman and chief operating officer. The numbers bear him out. The Infrastructure Solutions Group, which sells the servers and storage that power data centres, generated 42.0 billion dollars of revenue in the quarter — more than three-quarters of the company's total — and grew by 198 percent. The personal computer business, which was once the whole of Dell, generated 12.89 billion dollars and grew by 3 percent. The company that Michael Dell founded to sell personal computers is now, overwhelmingly, a company that sells the infrastructure of artificial intelligence.

The transformation is not merely a matter of revenue. It is a matter of identity, and of strategy. Dell has spent years building the capabilities that the AI boom requires: the ability to design and manufacture servers at scale, the supply chain to source the chips and the components, the engineering to test whole racks before they ship, and the services organisation to install them in data centres around the world. Those capabilities, built over decades, are now the company's most valuable assets. The AI-optimised server business, which generated 22.0 billion dollars in the quarter — up 291 percent from a year earlier — is the fastest-growing part of the company, and it is the part that Wall Street now values most. The traditional server and networking business, at 15.0 billion dollars and up 235 percent, is growing almost as fast. The personal computer business, for so long the company's core, is now a sideshow — profitable, but no longer the engine of growth.

The forecast raise — the third this year

Dell did not merely report a strong quarter. It raised its forecast for the full year, for the third time in 2026. For fiscal 2027, the company now expects revenue of 202 billion dollars, up from its prior target of 192 billion, and above the 194.48 billion dollars that Wall Street had expected, according to data compiled by LSEG. It raised its forecast for AI-optimised server revenue to 78 billion dollars from 74 billion — a figure that represents growth of 216 percent from the prior fiscal year, and more than three times the amount delivered in the year before that. And it raised its forecast for adjusted earnings per share to 29.68 dollars from 25.50 dollars — an increase of more than 16 percent in a single quarter. For the fourth quarter, which ends in January, the company guided revenue to 56.3 billion dollars, up 69 percent from a year earlier, and adjusted earnings to 8.40 dollars a share, up 116 percent.

The forecast raises matter because they signal confidence — not merely in the current quarter, but in the quarters that follow. A company raises its forecast when it believes that the demand it is seeing is durable, that its backlog will convert into revenue, and that its customers will keep ordering. Dell has now raised its forecast three times in a single year, and each raise has been larger than the last. The first raises were modest. This one — a 10 billion dollar increase in the revenue forecast, and a 4 billion dollar increase in the AI server forecast — is anything but modest. It is a statement that the company expects the AI infrastructure boom to continue, and to accelerate, into 2027. It is also a statement that Dell expects to capture a growing share of that boom, as customers turn to the company that can deliver complete, tested systems at scale.

25 billion dollars more for buybacks

Dell also announced that it would return more money to its shareholders. The company returned a record 4.9 billion dollars to shareholders in the third quarter, through a combination of share repurchases and dividends, and its board approved an additional 25 billion dollars in share repurchase authorisation. In the quarter, the company repurchased 9 million shares at an average price of 488 dollars a share. The buyback authorisation, which gives the company permission to buy back its own stock in the market, is a signal of confidence: a company buys back its shares when it believes they are undervalued, and when it has more cash than it needs to invest in its business. Dell, with 16.3 billion dollars in cash and investments and record cash flow from operations of 10.8 billion dollars so far this year, has cash to spare.

The buyback also matters for the stock. When a company buys back its shares, it reduces the number of shares in circulation, which increases the earnings attributable to each remaining share. For investors, the combination of a rising earnings forecast and a shrinking share count is a powerful one: it means that earnings per share can grow faster than earnings themselves. Dell's chief financial officer, David Kennedy, made the point in the earnings release, saying that "the team executed exceptionally well, driving record revenue of 54.9 billion dollars, record EPS and a record 4.9 billion dollars returned to shareholders." He added that the company had "continued to run the company more efficiently as we've grown, and that is driving higher profitability, even as AI servers become a larger part of our business." The message was clear: Dell is not merely growing. It is growing profitably, and it is sharing the profits with its owners.

The market's verdict

The market's reaction to Dell's results was positive, though it was muted by the broader sell-off in technology stocks on the same day. Dell's shares rose about 5 percent in extended trading after the results were announced, a gain that reflected the scale of the beat and the size of the forecast raise. The rise came on a day when the Nasdaq fell 1.48 percent and Nvidia, the company most closely associated with the AI boom, fell 5.86 percent — a contrast that underlined the difference between Dell's story and Nvidia's. Nvidia's fall was driven by political questions about its chief executive and by profit-taking after a 31 percent surge. Dell's rise was driven by numbers: by revenue that doubled, by earnings that beat expectations by 43 percent, and by a backlog that reached a record.

Dell's stock has been one of the hottest in the S&P 500 this year, according to Morningstar, and the company's business has more than doubled within a year's time. The question for investors is whether the rally can continue. The bull case is straightforward: the backlog is real, the demand is accelerating, the forecast keeps rising, and the company is returning billions of dollars to shareholders. The bear case is that the AI infrastructure boom will eventually slow, that the backlog will prove less durable than it appears, and that the margins on AI servers — which are lower than the margins on traditional servers, because the chips that power them are so expensive — will compress the company's profitability over time. Dell's own commentary addresses the bear case directly: the vetting of orders, the testing of racks, the focus on efficiency. But the debate will continue, and it will be settled not by earnings releases but by the orders that arrive, or fail to arrive, in the quarters ahead.

The AI infrastructure gold rush

Dell's results are, in the end, a window onto the largest capital spending boom in the history of technology. Companies around the world are spending hundreds of billions of dollars to build the data centres that will train and run artificial intelligence models, and Dell is one of the principal beneficiaries. The company's AI-optimised server revenue of 22.0 billion dollars in a single quarter is, by itself, larger than the entire annual revenue of many technology companies. Its backlog of 132.3 billion dollars is larger than the annual revenue of most of the companies in the S&P 500. And its customer base — more than 7,500 companies deploying Dell AI Factories — spans the globe, from the largest cloud providers to the smallest enterprises that are building their first AI systems.

The boom has transformed the economics of the server industry. A decade ago, a server was a commodity: a box of standard components, sold at a thin margin, to a customer who cared mostly about price. Today, an AI server is a complex system: a rack of specialised chips, connected by high-speed networking, cooled by liquid, and integrated with software that manages the training of models with billions of parameters. The companies that can build those systems — that can source the chips, assemble the racks, test them before they ship, and install them in data centres within hours — command a premium that the commodity server makers never could. Dell's claim that it can stand up a rack within six hours of delivery is, in this context, not a boast. It is a competitive advantage, and it is one of the reasons its backlog has grown so large.

The risks Wall Street is still watching

For all the strength of Dell's results, the risks are real, and they are worth setting out plainly. The first is concentration. The AI server business is dominated by a small number of very large customers — the cloud providers and the AI companies that are building the biggest data centres — and if one of those customers were to slow its spending, Dell would feel it immediately. The company's insistence that it vets its orders for customer quality is, in part, an acknowledgement of that risk: it is a promise that its customers can pay, but it is also a reminder that the question has been asked. The second risk is margin. AI servers are less profitable, per dollar of revenue, than traditional servers, because the chips that power them — principally Nvidia's — account for so much of the cost. As AI servers become a larger share of Dell's business, the company's overall margins could come under pressure, even as its revenue grows.

The third risk is the PC business. Dell's personal computer revenue grew by only 3 percent in the quarter, to 12.89 billion dollars, compared with growth of 20 percent in the previous three months. The slowdown suggests that customers — both businesses and consumers — are prioritising spending on AI infrastructure over new computers, and that the PC refresh cycle that drove growth earlier in the year is fading. The PC business is still profitable, and it still generates more than 12 billion dollars a quarter, but it is no longer growing, and it is no longer the engine of the company. If the AI boom were to slow, Dell would not have a fast-growing PC business to fall back on. The fourth risk is the boom itself. Capital spending booms in technology have a history of ending badly, and the sums now being spent on AI infrastructure are without precedent. Dell's backlog is a promise, not a guarantee, and promises in this industry have been broken before.

What it means for the AI trade

Dell's results also matter for the broader debate about the AI trade — the question of whether the spending on artificial intelligence is a bubble or a transformation. On Tuesday, the market gave two different answers to that question. Nvidia, the company that makes the chips, fell 5.86 percent. Dell, the company that builds the servers that hold the chips, rose 5 percent. The divergence is instructive. Nvidia's fall was driven by politics and by profit-taking. Dell's rise was driven by orders and by a backlog. The two companies are, in a sense, two sides of the same trade: Nvidia sells the intelligence, Dell sells the infrastructure that houses it. But the market treated them differently on Tuesday, and the difference suggests that investors are beginning to distinguish between the companies that make the AI boom possible and the companies that are most exposed to its risks.

The distinction may prove durable. The AI infrastructure boom is, at its core, a construction boom: a boom in building data centres, in laying fibre, in installing servers, in providing power. The companies that build the infrastructure — Dell, the server makers, the data centre operators, the power companies — are selling picks and shovels to the gold rush, and the picks and shovels are selling well. The companies that are digging for gold — the AI model makers, the application builders, the companies that must turn AI into revenue — face a harder question, and their answers are not yet clear. Dell's results suggest that the infrastructure boom is real, that the orders are real, and that the money is real. They do not, by themselves, prove that the AI models will generate the returns that would justify the spending. But they do prove that, for the moment, the spending is happening, and that Dell is capturing more of it than almost anyone else.

The bottom line

Dell Technologies reported, on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, the strongest quarter in its history. Revenue more than doubled to a record 54.9 billion dollars, up 103 percent from a year earlier, against expectations of 49.32 billion. Adjusted earnings per share rose 265 percent to 9.45 dollars, against expectations of 6.59. AI-optimised server revenue rose 291 percent to 22.0 billion dollars. The company booked 60.2 billion dollars of new AI orders in the quarter and ended it with a record backlog of 132.3 billion dollars. It raised its full-year revenue forecast to 202 billion dollars, its AI server forecast to 78 billion, and its adjusted earnings forecast to 29.68 dollars a share — the third forecast raise of the year. It returned a record 4.9 billion dollars to shareholders and won approval for a further 25 billion dollars of buybacks. And its shares rose about 5 percent in extended trading, even as the broader technology market fell.

The results confirm what Jeff Clarke said in the earnings release: Dell is now a different company. The maker of personal computers has become the builder of the infrastructure of artificial intelligence, and the transformation has been, by every measure, a success. The question now is whether the transformation can continue — whether the backlog will convert into revenue, whether the orders will keep coming, and whether the AI boom will prove to be a durable shift in the economy rather than a passing fever. Dell's answer, delivered in the form of a record quarter, a record backlog and a raised forecast, is that it will. The market, for the moment, believes it. The next quarter will test whether that belief is justified.
