TITLE: 6 Dead, Including Two Boys Aged 8 and 10 — Cessna Plunged 10,000 Feet a Minute Into the Gulf Off Mississippi's Horn Island LABELS: US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A Cessna carrying four adults and two boys aged 8 and 10 plunged into the Gulf off Horn Island, Mississippi. Divers found all six in the fuselage ---BODY---

Horn Island, Mississippi, in the Gulf of Mexico. The wreckage of a Cessna 210 carrying six people was found two miles north-east of the island, and divers recovered all six bodies from inside the fuselage. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Six people are dead, including two boys aged eight and ten, after the small plane carrying them home from a fishing trip plunged into the Gulf of Mexico off the Mississippi coast on Tuesday afternoon. The single-engine Cessna was descending at 10,000 feet a minute — a fall so steep that air traffic controllers lost it from their screens in seconds — when it vanished from radar about halfway through a flight from New Orleans to Fort Myers, Florida. A four-hour search across the darkening waters of the Mississippi Sound ended in the early hours of Wednesday morning, when divers found all six bodies still inside the wrecked fuselage, two miles north-east of Horn Island, a remote barrier island off Pascagoula. The identities of the victims had not been released by Wednesday morning, and investigators from the National Transportation Safety Board and the Federal Aviation Administration were beginning the long work of finding out why a routine flight home ended in one of the worst small-plane disasters of the year — on the eve of the busiest Thanksgiving travel week American aviation has seen in 16 years.

The flight had begun, by all accounts, as an ordinary end to an ordinary trip. The six people aboard had spent time in Venice, Louisiana, a tiny fishing community at the very end of the highway in the Mississippi Delta, a place locals call the "end of the world" because the road simply stops there, and a launching point for charter-fishing trips into the Gulf. According to the Times, the aircraft took off from an airstrip at Buras, near Venice, at 1.29 p.m. local time on Tuesday, November 24. It made a brief stop at Lakefront Airport in New Orleans, and then set course for home — Page Field airport in Fort Myers, Florida — departing Lakefront at about 2.04 p.m. The flight should have taken a little over two hours, a straight run east across the Gulf coast, over the Mississippi Sound and the Florida Panhandle, and down the Florida peninsula. It lasted 34 minutes.

At about 3.45 p.m. Eastern time, the regional air-traffic control tower in Mobile, Alabama, reported that it had lost contact with the aircraft, and alerted the United States Coast Guard. The Federal Aviation Administration told the Coast Guard that the plane had been descending at 10,000 feet a minute when communications dropped — a rate of descent so extreme that it left almost no time for a mayday call, a recovery, or a controlled ditching. Ten thousand feet a minute is more than 110 miles an hour straight down. A commercial airliner in a normal descent comes down at around 2,000 to 3,000 feet a minute. The Cessna was falling, in effect, out of the sky. The National Transportation Safety Board, in a preliminary account issued on Tuesday night, put the time of the crash at about 2.38 p.m. local time — consistent with a flight that had been airborne for barely half an hour when it went down.

What happened in those 34 minutes is now the central question of the investigation, and at this stage there is no public answer. The Coast Guard has not said whether the pilot reported any problem before contact was lost, and the NTSB has not said what caused the crash. What is known is the sequence that followed, and it was a textbook multi-agency response that still ended in the worst possible outcome. The Coast Guard launched a search by air and sea. The Mississippi Department of Marine Resources sent marine patrol officers and a dive crew. The Pascagoula Police Department joined the search. The Federal Bureau of Investigation assisted. A United States Air Force C-130 Hercules joined the hunt from above, sweeping the waters where the plane had last been tracked. The search area was the Mississippi Sound off Horn Island, a long, low barrier island that shelters the coast, and the water there is shallow, turbid, and full of shifting sand — hard water to search, and harder still in the dark.

About two hours after the crash was reported, searchers found the first wreckage: the cabin of the aircraft, three life jackets, and a life raft, floating near the island. There was no sign of the passengers. The cabin had separated from the rest of the aircraft — the Coast Guard did not specify whether other parts, the wings and the tail, were found scattered nearby — and the fact that the life jackets and the raft were in the water, unused, told its own story about how fast the end had come. The search went on into the night. Then, in the early hours of Wednesday, November 25, divers from the Mississippi Department of Marine Resources located the remains of all six people inside the fuselage of the aircraft, submerged about two miles north-east of Horn Island. The search was called off. Every person aboard was dead.

"This is a heartbreaking outcome, and our deepest sympathies are with the families and loved ones who are facing an unimaginable loss," said Commander Trevor Blount, the Coast Guard Sector Mobile search and rescue mission coordinator, in a statement issued after the search ended. Joe Spraggins, the executive director of the Mississippi Department of Marine Resources, said his marine patrol officers and dive crew had worked with "the utmost care, professionalism and respect" to recover the victims' remains from the wreckage. The Coast Guard did not release the victims' identities, and by Wednesday morning they had still not been made public — a delay that is normal while next of kin are notified, and agonising for the families waiting for official word.

The six aboard — and the trip they were coming home from

The six people on the plane were four adults and two children, two boys aged eight and ten, according to the Coast Guard. Beyond that, little has been confirmed. The plane was heading home to Florida, and its registration pointed to Cape Coral, on Florida's Gulf coast, near Fort Myers. The Federal Aviation Administration registry lists the aircraft as owned by Rhonda Culmer of Cape Coral, and USA Today, citing the FAA registration, reported that the plane was registered to EAB Services, based in Cape Coral. The Times reported that the six-seat Cessna, manufactured in 1972, was owned by a flight instructor at the Fort Myers Flying Club. The overlapping accounts point to the same corner of south-west Florida — a flying club, a small aviation business, a family aircraft used for trips like this one — but the precise ownership, and who was flying the plane, had not been confirmed by the authorities on Wednesday morning.

What is clearer is where they had been. Venice, Louisiana, where the trip began, is a place that exists for one reason: fishing. It sits at the end of Louisiana Highway 23, where the Mississippi River meets the Gulf, and it is the jumping-off point for charter boats heading out to the oil rigs and the deep water, where anglers chase tuna, marlin and red snapper. The community is small, remote and used to visitors who arrive by road and leave by boat or by air — and to the small airstrip at Buras, a few miles up the highway, that serves the charter trade. The six had spent time there, according to the Times, and were on their way home to Florida when the flight ended. It was the kind of trip thousands of American families make every year — a long weekend, a fishing trip, a flight home in a private plane — and it is precisely that ordinariness that makes the crash so shocking. There was nothing unusual about the journey. There was nothing, so far as anyone has said, unusual about the weather or the aircraft or the plan. And then, 34 minutes into a routine flight, the plane was falling at 10,000 feet a minute.

The two boys, aged eight and ten, are the detail that has fixed itself in the public mind, and it is the detail that makes the loss hardest to comprehend. They were children on a family trip, coming home from a fishing holiday, in a plane with four adults who were, presumably, their parents or relatives. Their names have not been released. Their ages are all that is known about them. In a week when millions of American families are travelling to be together for Thanksgiving, the deaths of two children on a flight home have a particular cruelty — and the Coast Guard's careful, formal statement of sympathy, and the Marine Resources department's emphasis on the care taken in recovering the bodies, suggest something of how the rescuers themselves experienced the night. Divers who go down to a wrecked fuselage to bring out the dead, including children, do not forget it. The search lasted four hours. The recovery, and the grief, will last far longer.

Horn Island — the lonely shore where the search ended

Horn Island, where the wreckage was found, is one of the strangest and loneliest places on the American Gulf coast. It is a barrier island about 13 miles long, a ribbon of sand, marsh and pine forest lying off the Mississippi coast, south-west of Pascagoula, and part of the Gulf Islands National Seashore — which means it is federally protected, undeveloped, and reachable only by boat. There are no roads on Horn Island, no towns, no permanent residents. There are sea oats, slash pines, alligators in the lagoons, and, in the summer, nesting sea turtles and shorebirds. It was once home to a quarantine station and a military installation, and to the artist Walter Anderson, who rowed out to the island alone for weeks at a time in the 1940s and 1950s and painted its wildness. Today it is a place for day-trippers, campers and fishermen — and, on Tuesday night, for a search fleet.

The island's geography matters to the story. The Mississippi Sound, the stretch of water between the barrier islands and the mainland, is shallow — often only a few feet deep in places, and rarely more than 20 feet — and its waters are murky with sediment from the rivers. A plane that goes down there does not sink into a deep, cold abyss; it comes to rest on a sandy, shifting bottom, in water that is hard to see through and hard to search. The Coast Guard said the wreckage was found about two miles north-east of the island, in the Sound itself, and that the fuselage was submerged — which is consistent with a shallow-water impact in which the aircraft broke apart on hitting the surface and then settled on the bottom. The life jackets and the life raft found floating nearby suggest the cabin broke open on impact. For the divers, working in dark, turbid water, in the middle of the night, to find six bodies inside a submerged fuselage, is painstaking and dangerous work — and the four hours the search took is, in that light, remarkably fast.

The island is also a reminder of how much of the American coast is still wild, and how thin the margin is between a routine flight and a disaster in a place with no help nearby. A plane that goes down two miles off Horn Island is not going down next to a hospital or a fire station. It is going down next to an uninhabited sandbar, in water that has to be searched by boat and by divers, in the dark. The nearest help is in Pascagoula, on the mainland, and the Coast Guard station that ran the search is in Mobile, Alabama, across the state line. The response was fast and large — the Coast Guard, the state marine resources department, the local police, the FBI, an Air Force C-130 — and it still took four hours to find the fuselage, and it still found no survivors. Speed, in this case, was not the problem. The problem was the fall itself.

The plane — a 1972 Cessna 210L, a workhorse of American skies

The aircraft was a Cessna 210L Centurion, a six-seat, single-engine plane with the tail number N29002, manufactured in 1972 — which makes it 54 years old. That sounds old, and it is, but in general aviation it is not unusual. The Cessna 210 is one of the most successful light aircraft ever built: a high-wing, retractable-gear, single-engine workhorse that has been flying since the late 1950s, used for everything from family transport to charter work, air taxi, and survey flying. Thousands are still in service, many of them older than the pilots who fly them, maintained under a regime of mandatory inspections and airworthiness directives that is supposed to keep an old airframe safe for as long as it is properly looked after. Age alone tells you almost nothing about why a plane crashed. A well-maintained 54-year-old Cessna is, in principle, as safe as a new one. A poorly maintained one is not. The NTSB will examine the maintenance records, the engine, the airframe and the avionics before it says anything about the aircraft's condition — and it will be months before it does.

The 210L is a capable aeroplane. It cruises at around 160 to 170 knots, carries six people, and has the range to fly from New Orleans to Fort Myers without refuelling. It is a single-engine aircraft, which means that if the engine stops, there is no second engine to keep it flying — the pilot must glide to a landing, and over water, that means ditching. But a descent rate of 10,000 feet a minute is not what an engine failure looks like. An engine failure in a Cessna 210 produces a glide, a controlled descent of perhaps 1,000 feet a minute, with time to radio a mayday and pick a landing spot. A descent of 10,000 feet a minute suggests something else entirely: a loss of control, a structural failure, a catastrophic event in the air, or a pilot incapacitated. The NTSB has not speculated, and it would be wrong to do so here. But the number itself — 10,000 feet a minute, reported by the FAA to the Coast Guard — is the single most alarming fact in the whole story, and it is the fact the investigators will be working backwards from.

There is another question the investigation will have to answer: what, if anything, was recorded. A 1972 Cessna is not required to carry a flight data recorder or a cockpit voice recorder — the black boxes that are mandatory on airliners are not required on small private aircraft — and it is unlikely that this one had them. What it will have had is a transponder, which sends the aircraft's position and altitude to air traffic control, and which is how the FAA knew the descent rate. It may have had modern avionics, a GPS, and an autopilot, depending on how it had been upgraded over 54 years of service. The NTSB will recover the wreckage, examine the engine and the propeller for signs of power at impact, examine the flight controls, and look at the maintenance history, the pilot's qualifications and recent flying, the weather along the route, and the air-traffic control recordings. A preliminary report is usually published within a few weeks; a final report, with a probable cause, can take a year or more.

The investigation — what happens next

The National Transportation Safety Board announced on Tuesday night, at 9.39 p.m. Eastern time, that it was investigating a crash involving an aircraft near Horn Island, and it asked any witnesses, or anyone with surveillance video or information about the crash, to contact the NTSB at witness@ntsb.gov. That is standard practice, and it is also a sign of how little is yet known: the NTSB is asking the public for help because the aircraft itself may not be able to tell the story. The FAA will also investigate, as it always does, but the NTSB leads the inquiry into the cause, and it has the final word.

The first phase of the investigation is recovery. The wreckage — the fuselage, and whatever remains of the wings, the tail and the engine — has to be brought up from the shallow water off Horn Island and taken to a facility where it can be examined piece by piece. In a water crash, that is harder than it sounds: salt water corrodes, currents scatter debris, and the impact itself may have broken the aircraft into fragments that have to be found and mapped. The investigators will look for the classic signs: whether the engine was producing power when it hit the water, whether the propeller was turning, whether the flight controls were intact, whether there was a fire, whether the fuel system was compromised. They will check the aircraft's logbooks, the pilot's logbook, and the maintenance records. They will interview the people who saw the plane take off from Buras and from Lakefront, the air traffic controllers in Mobile who were talking to it, and anyone who saw or heard it in its final minutes.

The second phase is analysis. The NTSB will reconstruct the flight from the radar and transponder data, second by second, and compare it with the weather, the aircraft's performance, and the pilot's actions. It will consider the possibilities one by one — mechanical failure, weather, pilot incapacitation, loss of control, structural failure — and eliminate them until only one remains, or until the evidence runs out. Sometimes the evidence does run out: in a water crash with no recorders, the probable cause is sometimes listed as undetermined. But the NTSB's record in small-plane crashes is thorough, and the families of the six victims will, in time, get an answer — or as close to one as the wreckage allows.

There is a third, less visible part of the process, and it is the one that matters most to the families: the identification of the victims and the return of their remains. The Coast Guard did not release the victims' identities on Tuesday night or on Wednesday morning, and that is because the next of kin had to be notified first — a process that is handled by local law enforcement and the coroner, and that takes time, especially when the victims come from more than one family and more than one state. The bodies were recovered from the fuselage, and they will have been taken to a medical examiner for identification and autopsy. It is a slow, formal process, and it is the last thing the families will want to wait for, and the first thing that has to be done properly.

A Thanksgiving week shadowed by loss

The crash happened on the eve of Thanksgiving, in the middle of the busiest travel week of the American year. According to AAA, an estimated 81.5 million Americans were expected to travel at least 50 miles from home during the Thanksgiving holiday period, and the Federal Aviation Administration was anticipating more than 417,000 flights over the nine-day holiday period — the busiest Thanksgiving for flight operations in 16 years. The airports were full, the roads were full, and the skies were full, and the six people on the Cessna were part of that great annual migration: a family, coming home for the holiday, in a small plane, on a route they had probably flown before.

That context is part of why the crash has resonated. Thanksgiving travel is, for most Americans, the most familiar journey of the year, and a plane crash on the way home for Thanksgiving feels like a violation of something safe. It is also a reminder of the difference between the two kinds of flying that Americans do in this week. The 417,000 flights the FAA was expecting are mostly commercial airliners, flown by two professional pilots, maintained by airlines, tracked by radar from gate to gate, and equipped with every safety system modern aviation can provide. The Cessna 210 was none of those things. It was a private flight, in a 54-year-old single-engine aircraft, flown by a private pilot, over open water, at a time of year when the weather over the Gulf can change quickly. That does not make it unsafe — millions of such flights happen every year without incident — but it does make it a different kind of flying, with a different margin for error, and a different outcome when something goes wrong.

The crash is also a reminder of the scale of general aviation in America, and of its risks. The United States has more than 200,000 general aviation aircraft — small planes, flown for business, for pleasure, for training, for charter — and they fly millions of hours a year. The safety record of general aviation has improved enormously over the decades, but it remains, per hour flown, far less safe than commercial aviation. The NTSB investigates hundreds of small-plane crashes a year, and the causes are depressingly familiar: loss of control in flight, controlled flight into terrain, engine failure, weather, pilot error, mechanical failure. The Horn Island crash will be added to that list, and in a year or so the NTSB will add a probable cause to it, and the families will have something to hold on to. But for now, the cause is unknown, and the only certainty is the loss.

What we still do not know

There is a great deal that is still unknown, and it is worth being honest about it. We do not know the names of the six people who died, or how they were related to each other, or who was flying the plane. We do not know what the pilot said to air traffic control, if anything, in the final minutes. We do not know what the weather was like along the route at the time of the crash, or whether it played any part. We do not know whether the aircraft had any mechanical problem, or whether the pilot had any difficulty, or whether something happened in the cabin. We do not know why the plane was descending at 10,000 feet a minute, or whether the pilot was conscious, or whether anyone aboard knew what was happening. The NTSB will answer some of these questions, in time. Some of them may never be answered.

What we do know is the outline of a tragedy, and it is enough. Six people, four adults and two boys aged eight and ten, took off from New Orleans on a Tuesday afternoon, on their way home to Florida after a fishing trip in Louisiana, in a small plane that had flown the route before. Thirty-four minutes later, the plane was falling out of the sky at 10,000 feet a minute. Four hours later, divers found all six of them in the fuselage, two miles off Horn Island, in the dark water of the Mississippi Sound. The Coast Guard called it a heartbreaking outcome. It was. And in a week when 81.5 million Americans are travelling to be with the people they love, six of them will not be coming home — and two of them were children, aged eight and ten, who had gone fishing and were on their way back for Thanksgiving. The investigation will go on, the wreckage will be raised, the reports will be written, and the families will be told what happened. But nothing the investigation finds will change the fact that a family trip ended, on a Tuesday afternoon in November, two miles off a lonely barrier island in the Gulf of Mexico, and that six people — including two little boys — are gone.
