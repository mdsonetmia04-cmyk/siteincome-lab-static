TITLE: Judge Upholds Mamdani's Rent Freeze for 1 Million Apartments — Then Calls His Campaign Promise 'Reckless and Irresponsible' LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A judge upheld Mamdani's rent freeze for 1 million New York apartments — but called the mayor's campaign promise 'reckless and irresponsible' ---BODY---

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani. A Manhattan judge has upheld his rent freeze for nearly one million rent-stabilized apartments — while calling the mayor's campaign promise to deliver it "reckless and irresponsible". Photo: The White House via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.

New York City's rent freeze will stand. A Manhattan judge ruled on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, that Mayor Zohran Mamdani's signature housing policy — a freeze on rents for nearly one million rent-stabilized apartments — is legal and will remain in place, handing the 35-year-old mayor the biggest court victory of his young administration. But the ruling came with a sting that may hurt almost as much as a defeat: the judge called the mayor's campaign promise to freeze rents "reckless and irresponsible", said he had no power to make it, and found "troubling" evidence that the board that voted for the freeze was not as independent as it claimed to be.

The decision, a 20-page ruling by Justice Brendan T. Lantry of the New York State Supreme Court, dismissed a lawsuit brought by seven landlords who argued that the freeze was the product of a "sham process with a predetermined outcome". Lantry threw out the entire challenge, ruling that the city's Rent Guidelines Board had acted within its authority when it voted 7-1 in June to freeze rents on both one-year and two-year leases. The freeze, he wrote, "has a rational basis and must therefore be upheld as valid".

For the more than two million New Yorkers who live in rent-stabilized apartments — nearly half of the city's 2.3 million rental homes — the ruling means certainty for the holiday season and beyond. The freeze applies to leases that began on or after October 1, 2026, and runs through September 30, 2027. It has already been in effect for weeks, and Wednesday's ruling means it will now run its full course unless the landlords win on appeal.

"Today, Judge Lantry affirmed what we knew all along: the RGB had the authority to deliver for the more than two million New Yorkers who call rent-stabilized apartments home," Mamdani said in a statement after the ruling. "This holiday weekend, more than two million New Yorkers can breathe a little easier."

But the landlords are not giving up, and the judge's own words have given them plenty to work with. "Despite the economic realities and uncontroverted factual record that this unprecedented rent freeze will have devastating consequences for small landlords throughout our city, the judge deferred to the RGB's ultimate decision," said Randy Mastro, the landlords' lawyer. "Therefore, we are continuing to review the ruling and considering next steps." An appeal, in other words, is very much on the table.

A freeze the mayor promised — but could not order

The central drama of the case was a question of power: who actually decides what New Yorkers pay in rent? The answer, under New York law, is not the mayor. It is the Rent Guidelines Board, a nine-member panel that votes every year on how much landlords may raise the rent on the city's roughly one million rent-stabilized apartments. The mayor appoints the board's members, but he does not control its votes — or so the law intends.

Mamdani, however, campaigned on a promise to freeze the rent. It was the centrepiece of his affordability platform, the pledge that carried him from the New York State Assembly to City Hall in one of the biggest upsets in the city's political history. He repeated it at rally after rally, and he appointed six of the board's nine members in February, choosing people he believed were sympathetic to tenants. When the board voted 7-1 on June 26 to freeze rents on both one-year and two-year leases — the first freeze covering both lease terms since the board was founded in 1969 — Mamdani marked the occasion with a cake reading "Happy Rent Freeze!"

The landlords argued that the whole exercise was rigged from the start. Their lawsuit, Kenilworth Holdings LLC v New York City Rent Guidelines Board, filed in the New York State Supreme Court, alleged that the board had run a "sham process with a predetermined outcome", that Mamdani had effectively dictated the result by appointing loyalists to the board, and that the freeze was therefore not a genuine decision at all.

Justice Lantry gave that argument a closer look than the landlords might have expected — and he found parts of it persuasive. He wrote that a New York City mayor "has no authority to unilaterally raise, 'freeze' or otherwise adjust the rent" of stabilized units. He examined an email in which the board's chairperson sent a draft to one of Mamdani's aides, and he described the communications between the board's chair and City Hall as "troubling", saying they "cast a dark shadow" on the board's purported independence. And he delivered the line that will be quoted for weeks: "The court recognizes that political candidates routinely comment on prevalent policy issues while running for an elected position, but to guarantee a rent freeze to millions of New Yorkers without the authority to do so, is irresponsible."

But in the end, the judge drew a sharp line between bad politics and bad law. The board's role, he wrote, is quasi-legislative, which means its orders stand wherever a rational basis exists. The landlords, he ruled, had failed to show that the rent freeze was unreasonable, arbitrary or capricious. The freeze had a rational basis, the board had acted within its authority, and the decision "must therefore be upheld as valid". The rebuke, in other words, was real — but it did not change the outcome.

One million apartments, two million New Yorkers

The scale of the freeze is what makes it such a large prize in New York politics. Rent-stabilized apartments account for about one million of the city's 2.3 million rental units — roughly 40 percent of all rented homes — and they house about a quarter of all New Yorkers, more than two million people. For those tenants, the freeze means that when their lease comes up for renewal between October 1, 2026, and September 30, 2027, the rent will not go up at all, whether they sign for one year or two.

The average monthly rent for a regulated apartment was $1,599, according to the board's 2025 study — a figure that sounds modest only in comparison with the rest of the New York market, where the median rent for a newly leased apartment is $3,950, according to the listings agency StreetEasy. For a tenant in a stabilized apartment, a year without an increase is worth hundreds of dollars; for a family renewing for two years, the saving runs into the thousands. Multiplied across a million apartments, the freeze moves an enormous amount of money from landlords' balance sheets to tenants' pockets.

That is precisely why the landlords fought it so hard, and why they say the consequences will be severe. Small property owners, they argue, face rising costs of their own — property taxes, insurance, maintenance, water and sewer charges — and a year with no increase in income while costs keep rising pushes them towards the edge. Ann Korchak, who leads the group Small Property Owners of New York, said the outcome was terribly disappointing and warned that the freeze "will push more small rent-stabilized property owners to the edge of the cliff". The landlords' uncontested factual record, as Mastro put it, showed "devastating consequences" for small owners across the city.

The tenants' answer is that their own costs have risen just as fast, and their incomes have not kept up. At the public hearings in the run-up to the June vote, tenants demanded a freeze — or even a rent decrease — saying that wages were not keeping up with inflation and soaring bills. The board, which by law must weigh factors including wages, inflation, maintenance costs, taxes and landlords' incomes, heard both sides in a weeks-long, noisy annual ritual that determines how much landlords can charge. In the end, seven of the nine members voted for zero.

The only dissenter was Arpit Gupta, an economist appointed to the board in 2022 by Mamdani's predecessor, Mayor Eric Adams. The rest of the board — including the six members Mamdani had appointed in February — voted for the freeze. One of the two landlord representatives on the board, Christina Smyth, an appointee of Adams, resigned before the vote, saying the outcome had been predetermined by the mayor. "The rebuilt board was required to deliver a rent freeze," she said.

The mayor who promised the impossible

To understand why this ruling matters so much, it helps to understand who Zohran Mamdani is — and how improbable his rise has been. Mamdani, born on October 18, 1991, in Kampala, Uganda, to the film director Mira Nair and the political scientist Mahmood Mamdani, both of Indian descent, became the 112th mayor of New York City on January 1, 2026. At 35, he is one of the youngest people ever to hold the office. He is the city's first Muslim mayor and its first Asian American mayor. He represented the 36th district in the New York State Assembly from 2021 to 2025, and he won the Democratic primary in June 2025 by defeating the former governor Andrew Cuomo in an upset that stunned the city's political establishment — then beat Cuomo again in the November general election, when Cuomo ran as an independent, along with the Republican Curtis Sliwa.

Mamdani ran as a democratic socialist, a member of the Democratic Party and the Democratic Socialists of America, on a platform built around a single word: affordability. His five promises were a rent freeze on stabilized apartments, fare-free city buses, universal childcare, city-owned grocery stores, and a $30 minimum wage by 2030, paid for by tax increases on corporations and on people earning more than $1 million a year. The rent freeze was the promise he repeated most often, the one his supporters chanted at rallies, the one that made his campaign feel, to its supporters, like a movement rather than a candidacy.

It was also, as Justice Lantry has now pointed out, a promise he had no power to keep on his own. The mayor does not set rents. The Rent Guidelines Board does. Mamdani could appoint the board's members, and he did — six of nine in February — but he could not order them how to vote. The landlords' case was, in essence, that he had done exactly that, through appointments and through back-channel contact between City Hall and the board's chair. The judge found the contact troubling. But he also found that the board's decision was lawful anyway.

That distinction — between a process that looks compromised and a decision that is legally valid — is the heart of the ruling, and it is why both sides are claiming a kind of victory. Mamdani has his freeze. The landlords have a judge's finding that the mayor's promise was reckless, that the board's independence was shadowed, and that the freeze "may cause negative collateral consequences", as Lantry put it. The judge, for his part, emphasised that his decision was not a vote for or against the wisdom of the freeze — only a ruling that it was legal.

A second court win in a month

Wednesday's ruling is the second legal victory for Mamdani this month. Earlier in November, a state appellate court reversed a lower court's September ruling that the mayor's pied-à-terre tax rollout had been mishandled — a separate fight over a tax on wealthy owners of second homes that they leave empty for much of the year. Together, the two rulings suggest that the courts, for now at least, are not going to be the venue where Mamdani's opponents stop his agenda.

That agenda extends well beyond the rent freeze. In January 2026, Mamdani and Governor Kathy Hochul announced a plan to expand free childcare across the city. In June, the Rent Guidelines Board approved the freeze. His administration has named the first site for a planned network of city-owned grocery stores. And his housing plan, called Block by Block, aims to build 200,000 affordable homes and preserve another 200,000 over the next decade — a scale of public housing ambition that New York has not attempted in generations.

The rent freeze, though, is the policy that defines him, the one his supporters point to when they say he kept his word, and the one his critics point to when they say he is wrecking the city's housing market. It is also, as the judge's ruling makes clear, a policy with a built-in expiry date. The freeze runs through September 30, 2027. What happens after that — whether the board votes for another freeze, a small increase, or a large one — will be decided by the same nine-member board, in the same noisy annual ritual, in the summer of 2027.

What the freeze means for landlords

For landlords, the ruling is a defeat with a silver lining: a judge has now said, on the record, that the process behind the freeze was flawed, that the mayor overpromised, and that the consequences for small owners could be severe. That language matters, because it will be quoted back in the next fight — whether that fight is an appeal of this ruling, a challenge to a future freeze, or a political campaign against the mayor's housing agenda.

The landlords' core argument has always been economic rather than legal. Rent-stabilized buildings, they say, are not the luxury towers of Manhattan. Many are small, older buildings owned by families, not corporations, and their owners' margins are thin. Property taxes rise every year. Insurance premiums have soared. The cost of repairs, of boilers, of roofs, of compliance with the city's ever-growing list of building regulations, keeps climbing. A year with no rent increase does not freeze those costs. It freezes only the income.

The result, the landlords warn, is deferred maintenance: repairs that are put off, boilers that are not replaced, facades that are not fixed, apartments that slowly deteriorate. The history of New York housing gives that warning some force. In the 1970s, when rent controls and a fiscal crisis squeezed landlords hard, thousands of buildings were abandoned, and whole blocks of the Bronx burned. Nobody is predicting a return to the 1970s. But the landlords argue that the direction of travel is the same: a policy that squeezes owners without helping them maintain their buildings will, over time, produce worse housing, not better.

The tenants' advocates answer that the landlords' warnings have been made before every freeze, and the catastrophe has never arrived. New York has frozen rents before — three times under former Mayor Bill de Blasio between 2015 and 2021, for one-year leases — and each of those freezes survived a similar legal challenge from owners. The buildings did not collapse. The city did not burn. What happened, the advocates say, is that two million people kept a little more of their money, and the landlords, most of whom own buildings that have appreciated enormously in value, absorbed a year of flat income.

What happens next

The immediate question is whether the landlords will appeal. Mastro's statement — "we are continuing to review the ruling and considering next steps" — is the language of a lawyer who has not yet decided, but the landlords have every incentive to try. An appeal would go to the Appellate Division, and from there, potentially, to the Court of Appeals, New York's highest court. The process would take months, and the freeze would remain in effect while it played out. The landlords would need to persuade an appellate court that Justice Lantry got the law wrong — a harder argument than persuading a trial judge that the process looked bad.

The longer question is what happens in the summer of 2027, when the Rent Guidelines Board votes again. If Mamdani seeks a second freeze, the landlords will return to court with Lantry's own words in hand: the "reckless and irresponsible" promise, the "troubling" communications, the "dark shadow" over the board's independence. They will argue that the pattern has repeated, and that the court should look harder the second time. Mamdani, for his part, will argue that the freeze worked — that it kept two million people in their homes, that it did not destroy the housing stock, and that the voters who elected him on a promise of affordability expect him to keep it.

For now, though, the freeze stands, and the politics are simple. Mamdani won the case. He also got a public scolding from a judge — a former chairman of the Staten Island Republican Party, as it happens — who upheld his policy while questioning his honesty in promising it. It is the kind of ruling that gives both sides something to celebrate and something to fear, and it is the kind of ruling that guarantees the fight over New York's rents is far from over.

What is not in doubt is what the ruling means for the two million New Yorkers in rent-stabilized apartments this Thanksgiving weekend. Their rent will not go up when their lease renews. For a city where the median newly leased apartment costs $3,950 a month, and where the average regulated rent is $1,599, that is not a small thing. It is, as Mamdani put it, a reason to breathe a little easier — at least until September 30, 2027.
