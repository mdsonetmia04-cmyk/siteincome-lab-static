TITLE: At 81, Rodrigo Duterte Faces Trial for 85 Murders — The ICC Case That Could Put a Former President Away for Life LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Rodrigo Duterte goes on trial at The Hague on Nov 30, charged with crimes against humanity over 85 murders in his drug war. He faces life in prison. ---BODY---

Rodrigo Duterte, the former president of the Philippines, goes on trial at the International Criminal Court in The Hague on 30 November 2026, charged with crimes against humanity. Photo: Office of the President of the Philippines, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

On Monday morning, 30 November 2026, an 81-year-old man will be brought into a courtroom in The Hague and asked to enter a plea. His name is Rodrigo Roa Duterte. He was, for six years, the president of the Philippines. He is charged with crimes against humanity — murder and attempted murder — arising from a war on drugs in which, by the estimates of human rights groups, between 12,000 and 30,000 people were killed. If convicted of any of the three counts against him, he could spend the rest of his life in prison.

The trial, before Trial Chamber III of the International Criminal Court, is one of the highest-profile cases the court has heard in its more than two decades of existence, and the biggest since the administration of United States President Donald Trump imposed sanctions that could imperil the court's ability to function. It is also a first: Duterte is the first former Asian head of state to stand trial before the international tribunal, and only the second former national leader to appear before it for crimes allegedly committed while in power, after Laurent Gbagbo of Ivory Coast, who was acquitted in 2019.

For the families of the dead, the opening of the trial is a moment they have waited years for — some of them a decade. Llore Pasco, a mother who lost her two sons in the drug war, is travelling to The Hague to attend the trial, which will open on her 73rd birthday. More than 500 alleged victims will be represented in court. For Duterte's supporters, who have called for his release, it is a political prosecution. For his daughter Sara Duterte, now serving as the Philippines' vice president, it is a family crisis played out on a world stage. For the court itself, it is a test of whether international justice can function while the world's most powerful country tries to dismantle it.

The charges: 85 murders, three counts, eight years

Duterte is accused of the crimes against humanity of murder and attempted murder, under article 7(1)(a) of the Rome Statute, the treaty that founded the ICC. The charges cover three counts, spanning the period from 1 November 2011 to 16 March 2019 — the years when he was mayor of Davao City and then, from 2016, president of the Philippines.

Count 1 covers murders in Davao City during the mayoral period, between 2013 and around June 2016, allegedly committed by the Davao Death Squad — a group prosecutors say Duterte founded and led. Count 2 covers the murders of so-called "high-value targets" in locations across the Philippines during the presidential period, between around July 2016 and July 2017. Count 3 covers murder and attempted murder in barangay clearance operations — the neighbourhood-level anti-drug sweeps — in locations across the Philippines during the presidential period, between around July 2016 and September 2018.

The charges name 85 murders and two attempted murders. Prosecutors say those victims are only a sample of a far larger toll. The Philippine government itself puts the death toll at around 6,000 — the Philippine National Police reported 6,200 deaths during anti-drug operations, and law enforcement agencies acknowledged 6,248 deaths. United Nations agencies, the Philippine Commission on Human Rights, the Supreme Court and domestic human rights groups put the number of killings by police and unidentified assailants at between 20,000 and 30,000. A pre-trial document from the ICC prosecutor in September 2021 estimated that the total number of civilians killed in connection with the so-called war on drugs between July 2016 and March 2019 "appears to be between 12,000 and 30,000". The ICC's warrant cites 43 specific cases as representative examples of a much broader pattern.

The prosecution's case, as outlined in the court's documents, is that Duterte created, funded and armed death squads responsible for thousands of extrajudicial killings between 2011 and 2019. Pre-Trial Chamber I found reasonable grounds to believe that he was individually responsible as an indirect co-perpetrator for the crime against humanity of murder, and, in its decision confirming the charges on 23 April 2026, found sufficient evidence to establish substantial grounds to believe he was criminally responsible for the three counts — as an indirect co-perpetrator, and/or for ordering or inducing, and/or for aiding and abetting the commission of the crimes.

Duterte denies the charges. His lawyers have told the judges he will plead not guilty, and have argued: "This is not a crimes against humanity case and, as such, does not belong before this court." His supporters have made a stranger argument in the run-up to the trial: that the court should determine whether Duterte's "kill" remarks were just a Visayan way of emphasis, misread by outsiders in Manila and abroad. It is a measure of how little room the defence has that it is reduced to arguing about dialect.

The road to The Hague: arrest at the airport, a year in detention

Duterte was arrested on 11 March 2025 at Ninoy Aquino International Airport in Manila, on his return from Hong Kong. The arrest was carried out on the basis of a warrant issued by the ICC on 7 March 2025. He was detained at Villamor Air Base the same day, and by 12 March he had been surrendered to the custody of the International Criminal Court. He has been held since then at the ICC Detention Centre in Scheveningen, in The Hague, in a detention unit for war crimes suspects inside a Dutch prison.

His initial appearance before the court took place on 14 March 2025. The confirmation of charges hearing ran from 23 to 27 February 2026, and the charges were confirmed on 23 April 2026. On 24 April, the ICC Presidency constituted Trial Chamber III to hear the case. On 8 October 2026, the chamber made public its decision that Duterte is fit to stand trial, and adopted measures to facilitate his participation. In September 2026, he made his first physical pre-trial appearance before the court.

The trial will open on 30 November 2026 before Trial Chamber III, composed of Presiding Judge Joanna Korner, Judge Keebong Paek and Judge Nicolas Guillou. The schedule is precise. Duterte will be asked to enter a plea on Monday. Prosecutors will then present their opening arguments, followed by the legal representatives of the victims. The prosecution has been granted six hours for its opening statement and the victims' representatives three hours, delivered across four hearing sessions per day: 10:00–11:00, 11:30–12:30, 13:30–14:30 and 15:00–16:00, The Hague local time. The first prosecution witness is scheduled to take the stand immediately after the opening presentations, marking the start of the evidentiary proceedings. The prosecution is expected to call about 100 witnesses during the trial, according to ICC spokesman Oriane Maillet — earlier estimates put the figure at between 60 and 70 — and the chamber has already laid down the initial schedule for evidentiary hearings in 2027. The trial is expected to run well into 2027, and may last several years, depending on its complexity and the challenges that arise.

The arrest made Duterte the first former Asian leader to be taken into ICC custody, and the first former head of state to be surrendered to the court by his own country's government. The Philippines had withdrawn from the Rome Statute — the withdrawal took effect in March 2019 — but the ICC retains jurisdiction over crimes committed while the country was a member. The Marcos administration, after arresting and surrendering Duterte in March 2025, announced renewed efforts to advance domestic accountability for the drug war. That, in the end, is what the ICC is for: it is a court of last resort, stepping in only when domestic authorities are unwilling or unable to investigate and prosecute crimes under its jurisdiction.

The drug war: what happened in the Philippines

To understand the trial, it is necessary to understand what happened in the Philippines between 2016 and 2022. Duterte, born on 28 March 1945, had been mayor of Davao City for most of the previous three decades, a period during which the city became notorious for the Davao Death Squad — a group accused of killing suspected criminals, many of them poor, many of them young, with impunity. He was elected president in 2016 on a promise to kill drug dealers and users, and he began his presidency on 30 June 2016 by launching a nationwide "war on drugs".

What followed was a campaign of killings on a scale that shocked even a country accustomed to political violence. Police reported thousands of deaths in anti-drug operations. But the killings were not only carried out by police in operations. Human rights groups documented a pattern in which unidentified assailants — often riding in pairs on motorcycles, often wearing masks — shot suspected drug users and dealers in their homes, on the streets, in front of their families. The victims were overwhelmingly poor. They included children as young as three years old. The government's own records put the death toll at about 6,000. Human rights groups estimate 12,000 to 30,000.

Duterte himself encouraged the killings in public, in language that became infamous around the world. He told police to kill drug suspects, promised them protection, and said he would be "happy to slaughter" drug addicts. He compared himself to Hitler and said he would kill millions of drug users. His supporters said he was speaking in hyperbole, in the rough idiom of a provincial mayor. His critics said he was giving orders. The ICC's case is, in essence, that the second reading is correct: that the killings were not a tragic excess of a legitimate law enforcement campaign, but a policy — designed, directed and protected from the top.

The killings did not stop when Duterte left office in 2022. Human Rights Watch, in a report published on 24 November 2026, said Philippine authorities have failed to investigate the extrajudicial killings since Duterte was transferred to the ICC, and have failed to deliver meaningful justice for the victims. As of 7 November, there had been at least 1,342 public reports of drug-related killings under President Ferdinand Marcos Jr., according to the independent monitoring of the Dahas project. A Justice Department task force formed in November 2024 identified cases involving Duterte-era killings, but department data showed only four had reached trial and three more were under preliminary investigation. The Commission on Human Rights said its investigations from 2016 to 2023 led to 25 court filings, 37 cases referred to the Ombudsman and 42 to the Justice Department, with no further progress reported. It is reinvestigating 493 Duterte-era cases and aims to resolve 140 by the end of 2026. Convictions of officers implicated in killings have been rare.

The ICC's reach extends beyond Duterte himself. In November 2025, the court issued a sealed arrest warrant for Ronald dela Rosa, Duterte's former police chief and the architect of the drug war's operational campaign. The warrant was unsealed in May 2026; Dela Rosa has since gone into hiding. His case is a reminder that the trial in The Hague is not only about one man, but about an entire system — and that the system has not yet been dismantled.

A court under attack: the American campaign against the ICC

The trial opens as the court itself is under the most sustained attack in its history. The United States, which is not a member of the ICC, has waged an ongoing campaign to force its closure. On 9 October 2026, the Trump administration sanctioned the ICC, escalating its attack on the court and adding to existing sanctions on ICC judges and prosecutors, three Palestinian groups and a United Nations human rights expert. Washington has imposed what Reuters described as crippling financial sanctions, called on member states to withdraw from the court, and vowed to dismantle the ICC "brick by brick".

The sanctions matter because the ICC depends on the cooperation of states to function — to arrest suspects, to protect witnesses, to transfer funds, to enforce its orders. Financial sanctions on the court's officials and on the institutions that work with it make all of that harder. They also send a message to every government considering whether to cooperate: that helping the court carries a cost. The Duterte trial will test the court's ability to conduct a high-profile trial in those conditions. It is, in the words of Reuters, one of the highest-profile cases to come before the court in its more than two decades of existence — and it arrives at the moment when the court's survival is most in doubt.

Four serving national leaders currently face ICC arrest warrants: Israel's Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, Russia's President Vladimir Putin, and others. The court's docket is, in effect, a list of the world's most intractable conflicts. Duterte's case is different in one important respect: he is in custody. Netanyahu and Putin are not, and are unlikely to be. The trial in The Hague is therefore not only a test of the court's ability to try a former president — it is a test of whether the court can try anyone at all, when the most powerful country on earth is trying to close it down.

Human Rights Watch, in its report this week, argued that the Trump administration's sanctions reaffirm the ICC's critical role as a court of last resort. The argument is that a court that can be sanctioned into silence is not a court at all, and that the attempt to silence it proves its necessity. Whether that argument persuades anyone in Washington is another matter. What is certain is that the trial will be watched in Washington as closely as it is watched in Manila — and for the opposite reason.

The families: 500 victims, one birthday, and a decade of waiting

For the families of the dead, the trial is not about geopolitics. It is about the people they lost, and about a decade of waiting for someone, anyone, to say that what happened was wrong. More than 500 alleged victims will be represented in court, through their legal representatives, who have been granted three hours for their opening statement. The prosecution is expected to call about 100 witnesses. The first will take the stand immediately after the opening presentations on Monday.

Llore Pasco's story has become, in the reporting of the trial, emblematic of what is at stake. She lost her two sons in the drug war. She is travelling to The Hague to attend the trial, which will open on her 73rd birthday. She will sit in a courtroom in the Netherlands, thousands of miles from the Philippines, and listen to a court decide whether the man who ordered the campaign that killed her sons is guilty of crimes against humanity. For those who have suffered the most egregious abuses of the war on drugs, Reuters quoted one observer as saying, "this will be a welcome moment in their quest for justice."

That quest has been long and, for the most part, fruitless. In the Philippines, the families have waited for domestic justice that has not come. The police officers who carried out the killings have, with rare exceptions, not been prosecuted. The officials who ordered them have not been investigated. The task forces and commissions have produced reports and referrals, but few trials. The ICC is, for many families, the last resort — literally, in the language of international law, and figuratively, in the language of grief.

There is also the question of what the trial means for the Philippines itself. Duterte remains a powerful political figure. His daughter Sara is vice president. His supporters, who have called for his release, remain a significant political force. The trial will be followed intensely in the Philippines, where the drug war remains divisive — defended by those who say it made the streets safer, condemned by those who count the dead. The outcome will not settle that argument. But it will establish, in a court of law, whether the killings were crimes against humanity or, as the defence argues, something else entirely.

What to expect from the trial — and what it will decide

The trial is expected to run well into 2027 and may last several years. The prosecution will present its case first, calling witnesses — survivors, family members, former police officers, experts — to establish the pattern of killings and Duterte's responsibility for them. The defence will then present its case, arguing that the charges do not amount to crimes against humanity, that the court lacks jurisdiction, and that Duterte did not order the killings. The judges — Korner, Paek and Guillou — will decide. If convicted of any of the three counts, Duterte could face life in prison. The ICC has no death penalty.

Three questions will define the trial. The first is factual: did Duterte create, fund and arm the death squads, and did he order or induce the killings? The prosecution says yes, and says it has the witnesses and the documents to prove it. The defence says no. The second is legal: do the killings amount to crimes against humanity — a widespread or systematic attack on a civilian population — or were they, as the defence argues, a law enforcement campaign that went wrong? The distinction matters, because it is the difference between murder as a crime and murder as a crime against humanity, and it is the difference between a domestic matter and an international one. The third is institutional: can the ICC conduct a fair, credible trial of a former head of state while under sanctions from the United States? The answer to that question will shape the future of international justice long after Duterte's case is over.

There is one more thing worth saying about this trial, and it is the simplest. For ten years, the families of the dead in the Philippines have been told that nobody would ever be held responsible — that the killings were too many, the evidence too thin, the politics too hard, the court too weak. On Monday, in a courtroom in The Hague, a former president will be asked to plead to 85 murders. Whatever the verdict, that moment matters. It is the moment when the question of who killed 12,000 to 30,000 people stopped being a matter of history and became a matter of law.
