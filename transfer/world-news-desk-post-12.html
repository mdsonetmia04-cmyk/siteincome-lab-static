TITLE: Raskin Expands Investigation Into Donald Trump Jr.'s 1789 Capital Fund — Records Demanded From Four Companies LABELS: Politics, US SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Jamie Raskin expanded his probe into Donald Trump Jr.'s 1789 Capital fund, demanding records from four companies that won federal contracts. ---BODY---

Jamie Raskin, official portrait 2019. Photo: Leah Herman, U.S. House of Representatives / Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

Congressman Jamie Raskin is expanding his investigation into Donald Trump Jr.'s venture capital fund, 1789 Capital — and the move, announced on Monday evening, is the clearest signal yet of the oversight storm that is coming to Washington in January. Raskin has requested records from four companies that received investments from the fund and later benefited from federal contracts or policies, a demand that connects the President's son's business to the administration's decisions in a way that invites serious scrutiny. The investigation is part of a wide-ranging oversight agenda that Democrats are planning for their takeover of Congress in January, when the party's historic House majority — at least 258 seats, its largest since 1992 — will give it the subpoena power it has lacked for years.

The probe is about more than one fund or four companies. It is about the question that has shadowed the Trump presidency from its first day: where does the family's business end and the government's business begin? And it is about what a Democratic Congress, armed with a majority this large and a leader in Raskin who has made oversight his signature, intends to do about it. Here is what the investigation is, who is running it, and what comes next.

The investigation: four companies, one fund, one question

The facts, as reported, are straightforward. Raskin, on Monday evening, requested records from four companies that received investments from 1789 Capital — the venture capital fund associated with Donald Trump Jr. — and that later benefited from federal contracts or policies. The request is an expansion of an investigation that Raskin had already begun into the fund, and it sharpens the inquiry considerably. It is no longer a general examination of a venture fund. It is a targeted demand for documents from specific companies, about specific investments, and about specific government decisions that followed them.

The question at the heart of the investigation is the oldest one in the ethics of public office: did the companies benefit from federal contracts or policies because of their merits, or because of their connection to the President's family? It is a question that cannot be answered by assertion. It can only be answered by documents — by the records of the investments, by the timelines of the contracts, by the communications between the companies, the fund, and the government. That is what Raskin is demanding, and that is why the request matters.

The four companies have not been publicly named in the reporting of the request, and their identities may emerge as the investigation proceeds. What is known is the pattern that Raskin is examining: investment from a fund tied to the President's son, followed by benefit from the federal government. If the pattern is innocent — if the investments were ordinary venture bets and the contracts were won on merit — then the documents will show it. If the pattern is not innocent, the documents will show that too. Either way, the records are the point, and the demand for them is the story.

The investigation is also notable for its timing. It comes in the weeks between the Democrats' midterm victory and their takeover of the House in January, when the party is preparing its oversight agenda and signaling its priorities. Raskin's move is the first major investigative step of the new majority, and it sets the tone for what follows: an aggressive, document-driven examination of the administration, its finances, and its connections.

Who is Jamie Raskin

To understand the investigation, it helps to understand the investigator. Jamie Raskin — Jamin Ben Raskin — was born on December 13, 1962, in Washington, D.C. He graduated from Harvard College in 1983 and from Harvard Law School in 1987, and he spent the early years of his career as a lawyer and a scholar: an assistant attorney general in Massachusetts, then a professor at the American University Washington College of Law, where he taught from 1990 to 2017. He is the author of books on constitutional law and American democracy, and he built a reputation, long before he entered Congress, as one of the country's leading voices on the Constitution.

Raskin entered politics in Maryland, serving in the state senate from 2007 to 2016, where he was majority whip from 2012 to 2016. He was elected to the United States House of Representatives in 2016, taking office on January 3, 2017, as the representative for Maryland's 8th congressional district — a seat he has held through the 115th Congress and the four succeeding Congresses. In the House, he quickly became one of the Democratic Party's most prominent constitutional voices, and in 2021 he was appointed lead manager for the House's impeachment proceedings against President Donald Trump — the lawyer who presented the case for conviction to the Senate.

That role made Raskin a national figure, and it defined his approach to oversight: legal, methodical, and grounded in the Constitution. He is not a showman. He is a professor who became a prosecutor, and his investigations read like legal briefs — careful, documented, and built to withstand scrutiny. As the top Democrat on the House Oversight Committee, he has spent the years in the minority building the cases he intends to bring when his party returns to power. The 1789 Capital investigation is one of those cases.

Raskin's background matters for another reason. He is a constitutional scholar who has written about the relationship between money and power in American politics, and about the dangers of corruption in a democracy. The investigation into a venture fund tied to the President's son is, for Raskin, not a partisan exercise. It is the application of a principle he has defended his entire career: that public office must not be used for private gain, and that the American people have a right to know when it is.

1789 Capital: the fund at the center

The fund at the center of the investigation is 1789 Capital, a venture capital fund associated with Donald Trump Jr., the President's eldest son. Venture capital funds invest in early-stage companies, providing money in exchange for equity, and hoping that some of those companies will grow into major successes. It is a legitimate and common form of finance. But when the person behind the fund is the son of the sitting President, the ordinary business of venture investing takes on a different character — because the companies the fund backs may also be companies that seek business from the federal government, and the government is run by the fund manager's father.

That is the conflict of interest at the heart of the investigation, and it is not a new one. The Trump family's business interests have been a subject of scrutiny since the President first took office, and the question of whether the family's companies benefit from the presidency has been raised repeatedly by ethics experts, journalists, and members of Congress. What is new is the specificity of Raskin's inquiry: four companies, identified by name in his request, that received investments from the fund and then benefited from federal contracts or policies.

The pattern Raskin is examining is the classic shape of a corruption investigation. It has three elements: an investment, a benefit, and a connection between them. The investment is documented. The benefit — the federal contracts and policies — is documented. The connection is what the investigation must establish, and it is what the records Raskin has demanded are meant to show. Did the companies receive their contracts because they were the best bidders? Or did the fund's connection to the President's family play a role? The documents will tell.

It is worth noting what the investigation is not. It is not an accusation that a crime has been committed. Raskin has not charged anyone with wrongdoing, and the request for records is a standard tool of congressional oversight, not a criminal indictment. It is, however, a serious escalation — a move from general inquiry to targeted document demands — and it signals that Raskin believes the pattern he has found is worth pursuing to its conclusion.

The oversight agenda: what January will bring

The 1789 Capital investigation is one piece of a much larger puzzle. Democrats are planning a wide-ranging oversight agenda for their takeover of Congress in January, and the scale of the planned agenda reflects the scale of the majority they won. With at least 258 seats — the largest Democratic majority since 1992 — the party will control every committee in the House, and every committee will have subpoena power. The oversight that follows will be the most extensive in modern congressional history.

The agenda, as it has been described, will cover the administration, its appointees, its contracts, and its finances. It will examine the decisions that the administration made, the people who benefited from them, and the processes by which they were made. It will include investigations of the kind Raskin is conducting — document-driven, legally grounded, and focused on specific transactions — and it will include the public hearings that turn those investigations into national events.

The oversight agenda is also a political choice. Democrats believe that the administration's conduct in office is a winning issue — that voters who gave them a historic majority did so, in part, because they want the President checked, and that the party's job is to do the checking. The investigations are the mechanism by which that checking happens, and they will dominate the news for months after the new Congress convenes.

But the oversight agenda carries risks as well. Investigations consume time, attention, and political capital. They can make a majority look more interested in the past than in the future, and more interested in scoring points than in governing. The leadership of the new majority — Speaker-designate Hakeem Jeffries and his team — will have to balance the oversight agenda against the legislative agenda, and to decide how much of the House's time goes to investigating and how much goes to legislating. The Raskin investigation is the first test of that balance, and the way it is handled will set the pattern for everything that follows.

The Nvidia connection: another front in the same fight

The 1789 Capital investigation is not the only front on which Democrats are challenging the administration's relationship with business. In recent days, Democrats have reacted with alarm to President Trump's successful intervention on behalf of Nvidia, pressing the company to reinstate Jensen Huang as chief executive — an intervention that, according to reports, gives Democrats another target for 2028. Democrats, lacking a unified AI policy, have criticized the move as a threat to national security and as evidence of corruption, and they have highlighted the President's financial stake in Nvidia as a reason for concern about corporate influence and accountability.

The Nvidia episode and the 1789 Capital investigation are different stories, but they share a theme: the relationship between the President, his family, and the companies that do business with the government. In the Nvidia case, the question is whether the President used his office to benefit a company in which he holds a financial stake. In the 1789 Capital case, the question is whether companies backed by the President's son's fund benefited from the government. Both questions go to the same principle — that public office must not be used for private gain — and both are now on the Democrats' oversight agenda.

The two stories also illustrate the breadth of the Democrats' approach. They are not focusing on a single scandal or a single company. They are examining a pattern — a pattern of business and government intersecting in ways that benefit the President, his family, and the companies connected to them. It is a pattern that can only be established through documents, and that is why the oversight agenda is built around document demands, subpoenas, and hearings. The evidence, if it exists, is in the records. And the records are what Raskin and his colleagues are demanding.

What the fund and the family say

As with any investigation at this stage, the response from the subjects has been limited. The companies that received Raskin's request have not, in the public reporting, issued detailed responses. The fund has not publicly addressed the specific allegations. And Donald Trump Jr. has not commented on the expanded investigation in the reporting available.

That silence is normal for an investigation at this stage. Congressional document requests are the beginning of a process, not its end, and the subjects of such requests typically respond through lawyers and through the formal process of compliance — or non-compliance — rather than through public statements. What matters is what happens next: whether the companies produce the records, whether they resist, and whether the investigation must escalate to subpoenas to compel compliance.

If the companies cooperate, the investigation will proceed on the documents. If they do not, Raskin and the Democrats will face a choice: to subpoena the records, and if the subpoenas are defied, to enforce them through the courts or through contempt proceedings. That escalation is a familiar one in congressional oversight, and it is one that the new majority, with its large majority and its control of the committees, is well positioned to pursue.

The administration's broader response to the Democrats' oversight agenda has been confrontational. The President has blamed the Democrats' midterm victories for a $6 trillion slide in the stock market, and he has shown no inclination to cooperate with the investigations that are coming. That confrontation will define the next two years, and the 1789 Capital investigation is where it begins.

What happens next

The immediate next step is the response to Raskin's request. The four companies have been asked to produce records, and their response — cooperation, delay, or resistance — will determine the investigation's next phase. If the records are produced, Raskin's team will review them, and the investigation will proceed to the next stage: interviews, further document requests, and, eventually, a public accounting of what was found.

The longer-term next step is January, when the new Congress convenes and the Democrats' oversight agenda begins in earnest. Raskin's investigation will be one of the first items on that agenda, and it will be joined by others — investigations into the administration's contracts, its appointees, and its finances, conducted by committees across the House. The subpoena power that Democrats have lacked will return, and with it the ability to compel the testimony and documents that oversight requires.

For the American public, the investigation is a test of a simple proposition: that no one, not even the President's family, is above scrutiny, and that the records of government business belong to the people. Whether the investigation finds wrongdoing or exonerates the companies involved, the act of demanding the records is itself a statement about how democracy works — and about what a majority of 258 seats intends to do with its power.

The ethics rules — and why they matter

The investigation into 1789 Capital rests on a body of law and ethics that has governed American public office for more than two centuries. At its foundation is the Constitution itself. The Emoluments Clause — Article I, Section 9 — prohibits federal officeholders from accepting gifts, payments, or titles from foreign governments without the consent of Congress. It was written by the Founders, who had watched European monarchs buy influence with gifts and who were determined that the American republic would not be for sale.

The modern ethics framework goes further. Federal ethics laws prohibit government officials from participating in matters in which they have a financial interest. The STOCK Act, passed in 2012 with overwhelming bipartisan support, prohibits members of Congress and executive branch officials from using nonpublic information for private profit, and requires them to disclose their financial transactions. The Office of Government Ethics, established in 1978, exists to advise officials on how to avoid conflicts of interest — and to identify them when they arise.

Presidents have historically addressed these concerns by placing their assets in a blind trust — an arrangement in which an independent trustee manages the President's holdings without the President's knowledge or involvement, eliminating the possibility that presidential decisions could be influenced by personal financial interest. President Trump, in both of his terms, did not place his business empire in a blind trust. Instead, he transferred management of his companies to his sons, while retaining ownership — an arrangement that ethics experts have criticized from the beginning, because it leaves the President with a direct financial interest in businesses that interact with the government he leads.

The scrutiny of presidential family business is not new, and it is not partisan. Jimmy Carter placed his peanut farm in a blind trust when he took office, and still faced questions about it. The Clinton Foundation's fundraising was the subject of years of congressional investigation. The Bush family's business connections were examined throughout both Bush presidencies. What is new, in the case of 1789 Capital, is the specificity of the inquiry — the identification of four companies, the documentation of their investments, and the documentation of the federal benefits that followed. It is an investigation built not on suspicion, but on records, and the records are what will decide it.

That is why the ethics rules matter, and why the investigation matters. The rules exist because the American people have a right to know that their government acts in their interest, not in the interest of the President's family or of the companies connected to it. The investigation is the mechanism by which that right is enforced. And the documents Raskin has demanded are the evidence by which it will be judged.
