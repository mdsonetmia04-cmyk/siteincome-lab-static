A federal appeals court has revived his bid to move his New York hush-money case to federal court, opening a new path to challenge his conviction on 34 felony counts — and freezing his state appeal in the meantime. Photo: The White House, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

A federal appeals court has handed President Donald Trump a new pathway to erase the criminal conviction that made him the first United States president in history to be found guilty of a crime. On Monday, November 23, 2026, a three-judge panel of the 2nd United States Circuit Court of Appeals revived Trump's bid to move his New York hush-money case to federal court, where he could seek to have the conviction dismissed on presidential immunity grounds. The ruling also set in motion a halt to Trump's simultaneous appeal through New York's state courts — freezing a process that had already reached arguments before a mid-level appeals court earlier this month.

The decision was only two pages long. But its consequences could be enormous. If the case lands in federal court, Trump will argue that the verdict should be discarded because state prosecutors relied on evidence that would have been shielded by presidential immunity in a federal forum. If he succeeds, the conviction — 34 felony counts of falsifying business records, returned by a Manhattan jury in May 2024 — could be wiped from the books entirely. And if he fails, the ruling still buys him time: the state appeal that was moving toward a decision has now been stopped in its tracks, potentially for years.

Trump's legal team welcomed the ruling immediately. In a written statement provided to The Associated Press, a spokesperson for the president's legal team said Trump "appreciates the 2nd Circuit's prompt and correct ruling, as he continues to win against Democrat weaponization of our Justice System". The Manhattan District Attorney's Office, which prosecuted the case, declined to comment.

The two-page order that changed everything

The panel's order was short, but it was decisive. In two pages, the three judges of the 2nd Circuit reversed a lower court ruling that had kept the case in New York state court, and directed a federal judge in Manhattan to consider whether the proceedings could be brought under federal jurisdiction. The appeals court said the late United States District Judge Alvin Hellerstein had "erred in holding that removal is categorically unavailable in a state criminal case" after a conviction — the legal question at the heart of the dispute.

The process at issue is known as removal: the transfer of a case from state court to federal court. Trump has sought removal of his hush-money case three times, arguing that because the charges relate to his conduct as president — or to evidence connected to his presidency — the case belongs in federal court, where presidential immunity would apply. Each time, Hellerstein, a senior federal judge in Manhattan's Southern District of New York, denied the request. Most recently, in August 2026, Hellerstein rejected Trump's third attempt, finding that the president's arguments were insufficient to justify moving the case out of state court.

The 2nd Circuit disagreed. The panel held that Hellerstein had erred not only in ruling that removal was categorically unavailable after a conviction, but also in denying that Trump had good cause to make two attempts at moving the case — once after his indictment, and again after his trial. The court ordered a lower federal judge to promptly rule on whether the case should indeed be transferred from New York state court to federal jurisdiction. No deadline was given, but the instruction to rule promptly was unmistakable.

The ruling came a week after the panel — Judges Raymond J. Lohier Jr., John M. Walker Jr. and José A. Cabranes — spent more than an hour questioning Trump's lawyer and a state prosecutor on the matter. The speed of the decision, and its brevity, suggested a court that had already made up its mind.

The three judges

The panel that issued the ruling was made up of three judges with long careers on the federal bench. Judge Raymond J. Lohier Jr. was appointed to the 2nd Circuit by President Barack Obama in 2010. Judge John M. Walker Jr., a cousin of former President George H.W. Bush, was appointed by President George H.W. Bush in 1989 and has served on the court for more than three decades. Judge José A. Cabranes was appointed by President Bill Clinton in 1994. The panel was not, in other words, a group of judges hand-picked for sympathy to the president. It was a cross-section of the federal judiciary — and it ruled unanimously in Trump's favour.

That matters, because the 2nd Circuit has now sided with Trump twice in his quest for federal court intervention in this case. The first time, the court sent the case back to Hellerstein for reconsideration. The second time — this week — it went further, ruling that Hellerstein's reasoning was wrong as a matter of law. The pattern suggests a court that has grown increasingly sympathetic to the argument that a former president's criminal case, intertwined as it is with questions of presidential immunity, deserves a federal forum.

Trump's lawyer, Robert Giuffra Jr., had urged the judges to rule swiftly. Without federal court intervention, Giuffra argued, the president "will likely have to wait years" for state appellate courts to rule before he can take the matter to the United States Supreme Court. The panel appears to have agreed — and its order for a prompt ruling from the lower court reflects that urgency.

Hellerstein's three denials

To understand the significance of Monday's ruling, it helps to understand what it reversed. Judge Alvin Hellerstein was a senior federal judge in Manhattan who had presided over the removal question since Trump's first attempt to move the case in 2023. A World War II veteran who had served on the federal bench since 1998, Hellerstein was known for his independence and his impatience with what he saw as overreaching legal arguments.

He denied Trump's removal requests three times. Each time, he found that the case — which concerned a payment made before Trump took office, and business records kept by a private company — did not arise from Trump's official duties as president, and therefore did not belong in federal court. His most recent denial, in August 2026, was his third. Hellerstein died in October 2026 at the age of 92, and his death left the removal question without the judge who had decided it three times running.

The 2nd Circuit's ruling does not merely disagree with Hellerstein's conclusions. It holds that his central legal premise was wrong — that removal is not, as he had held, categorically unavailable in a state criminal case after a conviction. That is a significant legal statement, and it opens a door that Hellerstein had kept firmly shut. It also means the question will now be decided by a different federal judge, one who has not already ruled on it three times.

The immunity argument

If the case is transferred to federal court, Trump's argument will be straightforward — and it rests on one of the most consequential Supreme Court decisions of the past decade. In July 2024, the Supreme Court ruled that a former president has broad immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts taken while in office. The ruling, in a case called Trump v. United States, held that presidents enjoy absolute immunity for actions within their core constitutional authority, and presumptive immunity for other official acts.

Trump's lawyers will argue that the Manhattan prosecutors secured his conviction by relying on evidence that would have been shielded by that immunity in federal court — including testimony and records connected to his presidency. If the federal court agrees, the verdict could be discarded. The argument has been made before, in various courts, and it has not yet succeeded. But it has never been made in a federal forum that has already sided with Trump twice on the question of jurisdiction.

The stakes are difficult to overstate. The hush-money conviction is the only criminal conviction ever returned against a sitting or former American president. Trump was given no punishment — an unconditional discharge — but the conviction itself stands as a historical fact, and as a legal vulnerability. Erasing it would remove the only criminal judgment against him, and would reshape how history records his first term and the years that followed.

The state appeal, frozen

Monday's ruling did more than open a new front in the case. It also froze the one that was already moving. Along with his federal court fight, Trump has been waging a traditional appeal through New York's state court system, which included arguments before the mid-level Appellate Division on November 5, 2026. That court has yet to rule — and under Monday's decision, it may be prevented from doing so indefinitely.

Giuffra, Trump's lawyer, sent a letter to the Appellate Division on Monday notifying that court of the 2nd Circuit ruling, including its request "that no further proceedings occur in the state courts" while jurisdiction is being settled. The state appeal, in other words, is now on hold — and it could stay on hold for a very long time. The federal court must first decide whether the case belongs in federal jurisdiction. If it does, Trump will argue the verdict should be discarded on immunity grounds. If the federal court then rules against him, the case could return to the 2nd Circuit, and eventually to the Supreme Court. Each step takes months. The state court, meanwhile, waits.

The delay is itself a victory for Trump. His legal strategy in the hush-money case has always been, in part, a strategy of time: delay the proceedings, exhaust the appeals, and let the political calendar do what the courts have not. The conviction was returned in May 2024. More than two years later, it has not been finally resolved — and Monday's ruling means it may not be resolved for years to come.

The case itself: 34 counts, $130,000

The case stems from a $130,000 payment made shortly before the 2016 presidential election to adult film performer Stormy Daniels, whose real name is Stephanie Clifford. Daniels has said she had an affair with Trump in 2006 — a claim Trump denies. Prosecutors said the payment was made to keep her from speaking publicly about the alleged affair in the final weeks of the campaign, when it might have damaged Trump's chances of winning the presidency.

The charges were not about the payment itself, but about how it was recorded. Prosecutors said Trump falsified business records to conceal the payment, disguising reimbursements to his former lawyer Michael Cohen as legal expenses. In May 2024, a Manhattan jury found Trump guilty on all 34 felony counts of falsifying business records. The verdict made him the first United States president — sitting or former — to be convicted of a crime.

Trump has denied any wrongdoing from the start, and he has said the case was a politically motivated prosecution brought by a Democratic district attorney, Alvin Bragg, to damage him. His supporters have made the same argument, and the case has become a central exhibit in the debate over whether the American justice system has been weaponised against political opponents. His critics, meanwhile, note that the jury heard weeks of evidence, including from Cohen and from Daniels herself, and returned a unanimous verdict on every count.

The sentence, when it came, was the lightest the law allowed: an unconditional discharge, meaning no prison time, no fine and no probation. The judge who imposed it, Juan Merchan, said the sentence reflected the extraordinary circumstances of the case — a president-elect, days from taking office, convicted of a crime that would ordinarily carry a prison sentence. The conviction, however, remained on the record. That is the record Trump is now trying to erase.

What the ruling means for the presidency

Monday's decision is about one case, but its implications reach further. The question it answers — whether a state criminal case against a president can be moved to federal court after a conviction — has never been squarely decided before, and the 2nd Circuit's answer will now shape how future cases are handled. If the ruling stands, it will be harder for state prosecutors to keep a president's criminal case in state court, and easier for a president to invoke federal jurisdiction — and with it, federal immunity.

The ruling also lands in the middle of a broader fight over the limits of presidential power. Since returning to office, Trump has tested those limits repeatedly — in his use of executive orders, in his confrontation with the courts over birthright citizenship, in his deployment of federal power against universities, law firms and media companies. The courts have pushed back in some cases and deferred in others. The hush-money ruling is the latest entry in that ledger, and it is one of the most favourable to the president so far.

It is also a reminder of how much of Trump's legal fate now rests in the hands of the federal judiciary — and, ultimately, of the Supreme Court. The 2nd Circuit's ruling is not the final word. The Manhattan District Attorney's Office can seek further review. The federal judge who must now decide the removal question could rule against Trump. And if the case reaches the Supreme Court, the justices who decided the immunity question in 2024 will be asked to decide, in effect, whether that decision requires the erasure of a state jury's verdict. None of those outcomes is certain. What is certain is that the conviction that was supposed to be settled in 2024 is now, in late 2026, very much alive — and very much in play.

What happens next

The next step belongs to the federal judge in Manhattan who has been directed to rule promptly on whether the case should be transferred to federal jurisdiction. That judge — who has not yet been named in public reporting — will have to decide a question that Hellerstein answered three times, but that the 2nd Circuit has now said he answered wrongly. If the judge orders removal, Trump will then argue in federal court that the verdict should be discarded on immunity grounds. If the judge declines removal, Trump can appeal that decision back to the 2nd Circuit — the same court that has now ruled in his favour twice.

Meanwhile, the state appeal sits frozen. The Appellate Division heard arguments on November 5 and has not ruled. Giuffra's letter has asked it not to rule — or, at least, not to proceed — while jurisdiction is being settled. The state court's docket, in other words, is now hostage to the federal courts' timetable. And the federal courts' timetable, as Trump's own lawyer has acknowledged, could run for years.

For Trump, the ruling is a win on two fronts: a new path to erasing the conviction, and a halt to the appeal that was closest to deciding it. For the Manhattan District Attorney's Office, it is a setback — the second time the 2nd Circuit has intervened in the case, and the first time it has done so in terms that could lead to the conviction's dismissal. For the American public, it is another chapter in a legal saga that has now run for more than three years, through an indictment, a trial, a conviction, a sentencing and three failed removal attempts — and that, with Monday's two-page order, has just begun again.

The conviction was historic. The attempt to erase it may prove historic too. A jury of twelve New Yorkers found a president guilty of 34 felonies. Whether that verdict survives will now be decided not in the courtroom where it was returned, but in the federal courts — where the question is no longer what Trump did, but whether a president can be judged for it at all.

A conviction that never went away

The hush-money conviction has followed Trump through his return to the White House like a shadow that will not lift. He was convicted in May 2024, in the middle of his campaign to return to the presidency. He won the election anyway — the first person ever elected president after being convicted of a felony — and took office in January 2025 with the conviction still on his record and the sentencing still to come. The unconditional discharge that followed was, in one sense, a formality: no punishment, no probation, no consequence that could be measured in days or dollars. But the conviction itself was never a formality. It was the one criminal judgment against him that had survived every challenge, and it was the one fact his opponents could always point to: whatever else he had done, a jury had found him guilty.

That is why Monday's ruling matters politically as well as legally. For Trump's supporters, the conviction has always been the symbol of a justice system turned against their candidate — a prosecution brought by a Democratic district attorney, tried in a Democratic city, before a jury drawn from one of the most Democratic counties in the country. Erasing it would vindicate that argument in the most complete way possible: not merely a pardon, not merely a commuted sentence, but a judicial finding that the conviction should never have stood at all. For his critics, the reverse is true. The conviction is the one place where the legal system held — the one case, among four criminal indictments brought against Trump before his return to office, that actually reached a jury and produced a verdict. Losing it would feel like losing the last piece of accountability.

The other three cases — the federal election interference case, the classified documents case and the Georgia election case — all collapsed or stalled after Trump's election victory, undone by the Justice Department's policy against prosecuting a sitting president and by the sheer difficulty of trying a man who had just won the White House. The hush-money case was the exception, because it was a state case, brought by a state prosecutor, in a state court, beyond the reach of the federal pardon power and the Justice Department's internal rules. That is what made it durable. And that is what makes Monday's ruling so consequential: the federal courts are now, for the first time, in a position to undo the one conviction that the federal government could never touch.

Whether they will do so remains an open question. The 2nd Circuit has ruled only on jurisdiction — on where the case should be heard, not on whether the conviction should stand. The immunity argument that Trump will make in federal court is powerful, but it is not a certainty. The Supreme Court's 2024 immunity ruling was broad, but it was also careful: it distinguished between official acts, which are immune, and unofficial acts, which are not. A payment made before Trump took office, to conceal an alleged affair from 2006, recorded in the books of a private company — that is, on its face, a long way from the core constitutional duties of the presidency. The Manhattan prosecutors will argue exactly that. And the federal judge who must decide the question will have to weigh a Supreme Court precedent that has never been applied to a case quite like this one.

What is certain is that the case is no longer where it was a week ago. A two-page order from three judges has revived a legal fight that many assumed was over, frozen an appeal that was nearing a decision, and put the only criminal conviction in American presidential history back in play. The verdict was returned in May 2024. Two and a half years later, it is still not final. And it may not be final for years to come.
