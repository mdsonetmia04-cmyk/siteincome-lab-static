Here is what happened. ---BODY---

The New York Stock Exchange on Wall Street. Photo: Arild Vågen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).

Wall Street roared back on Monday, November 23, in one of the most violent reversals the market has seen in years. The S&P 500 surged 4.73 percent, the Nasdaq Composite jumped 6.51 percent and the Dow Jones Industrial Average climbed 1,448 points — the best day for the two main benchmarks since April 2025, and a rally powered almost entirely by one extraordinary event: the return of Jensen Huang to Nvidia. In a single session, Nvidia's shares rose 31.48 percent and added roughly $952 billion in market value, the largest one-day gain ever recorded by any company. The Nasdaq-100 added more than $2 trillion in value. The S&P 500 climbed out of correction territory. And after seven straight days of losses that had left investors wondering whether the artificial intelligence boom was finally over, the market delivered its answer in the loudest possible terms.

The numbers, by any measure, were historic. The Dow rose 1,447.98 points, or 3.01 percent, to close at 49,595.31. The S&P 500 gained 326.78 points, or 4.73 percent, to finish at 7,237.17. The Nasdaq Composite surged 1,471.83 points, or 6.51 percent, to 24,089.56. For the S&P 500, the 327-point gain was the second-biggest points gain in the index's history. For the Nasdaq, the 6.51 percent jump was its 23rd-biggest daily gain since the index began in 1971, according to an analysis by Investopedia. Both indexes recorded their best single day since April 9, 2025, when stocks soared after President Donald Trump paused much of the tariff regime that had sent markets tumbling. The rally also ended a brutal stretch: the S&P 500 had fallen for seven consecutive sessions, and on Friday both the S&P 500 and the Dow had confirmed they were in correction territory, down 10 percent or more from their recent highs.

What changed on Monday was Nvidia. Eleven days earlier, on November 12, Jensen Huang had resigned as the company's chief executive after admitting to extramarital affairs, including one with a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy. The resignation had detonated the market's confidence in the entire AI trade. Nvidia's shares, which had peaked at around $240 on October 6 with a market capitalisation of about $5.8 trillion, slid relentlessly, closing at $126 on Friday, November 20 — down 48 percent from the peak, wiping out roughly $2.8 trillion in market value in just 45 days. On Monday, the board voted unanimously to bring him back as president and chief executive, effective immediately, and the market's reaction was instantaneous. Nvidia's shares shot up as much as 36.6 percent during the session before closing up 31.48 percent at $165.05, up from $125.53 on Friday. It was Nvidia's best day since May 9, 2003 — and the $952 billion added in a single day was more than double the previous one-day record for a US company, about $450 billion set by Microsoft on July 30, according to the Wall Street Journal.

The day in numbers

It is worth pausing on the scale of what happened on Monday, because the figures are the kind that normally appear once in a decade. The S&P 500's 4.73 percent gain took it to 7,237.17, its biggest daily percentage rise in 19 months. The Nasdaq's 6.51 percent surge to 24,089.56 was its largest since the same April 2025 session. The Dow's 1,447.98-point rise to 49,595.31 was its biggest points gain of the year. The S&P 500's information technology sector jumped 9 percent on the day, leading all sectors, and an index of semiconductor stocks gained 14 percent, its biggest daily percentage gain since April 2025. The Cboe volatility index, Wall Street's fear gauge, plunged. Gold fell 4.6 percent as investors rushed back into risk. And the Nasdaq-100, the index of the hundred largest non-financial companies on the Nasdaq, added more than $2 trillion in market value in a single session, according to Reuters.

The rally was broad, but it was not even. Nvidia was the engine, and everything connected to the AI trade followed it. Micron Technology soared 19.04 percent after securing new multi-year customer agreements for memory supply. Meta rose 6.6 percent. Alphabet gained 6.3 percent. Amazon was up 3.5 percent. The so-called neocloud and data-centre companies, whose shares had sold off sharply after Huang's abrupt exit, came roaring back: Nebius jumped 20.28 percent, CoreWeave rose 22.32 percent, Dell climbed 20.46 percent, Bloom Energy gained 18.09 percent and Lumentum rose 18.20 percent, according to the Motley Fool. The message from the market was unmistakable: for all the doubts about the AI trade that had built up over the previous two weeks, investors still believe that Jensen Huang is, in the words of one strategist quoted by Reuters, uniquely important to Nvidia and to the AI trade itself.

Yet the scale of the rebound also tells a story about how far the market had fallen. Even after Monday's surge, Nvidia remains about 31 percent below its October peak, according to the Financial Express. The S&P 500, for all its 327-point gain, had only just climbed out of correction territory. The Nasdaq had come close to entering a bear market — a fall of 20 percent from its high — before Monday's rally. The rally was historic, but it was a recovery, not a new high. It repaired some of the damage. It did not erase it.

Eleven days that shook the AI trade

To understand why Monday mattered so much, it helps to remember what the previous eleven days had done. When Jensen Huang resigned on November 12, the market lost more than a chief executive. It lost the man who had, more than any other single person, come to personify the artificial intelligence revolution. Huang founded Nvidia in 1993 and built it from a graphics-chip maker into the most valuable company on earth, the supplier of the chips that power almost every serious AI system in the world. His resignation, following revelations about his personal life, raised a question the market had never seriously asked: what is Nvidia without Jensen Huang?

The answer, over the following eleven days, was brutal. Nvidia's shares fell almost every day, losing nearly half their value. The damage spread far beyond one company. Investors began to question the heavy borrowing underpinning AI investment, and whether the sector could deliver sufficient returns to justify the trillions of dollars being poured into data centres, chips and power infrastructure. Fears of political scrutiny also mounted after Democrats swept the US midterm elections earlier in November, winning a large House majority. And the sell-off came even as the underlying business news remained strong: Anthropic, one of the leading AI companies, had a hugely successful market debut the previous week, and Nvidia itself had posted strong quarterly earnings. The market was not reacting to Nvidia's numbers. It was reacting to the loss of its leader — and to a growing fear that the AI boom, the trade that had carried the entire market higher for three years, might be built on shakier foundations than anyone had admitted.

President Donald Trump then intervened. According to multiple reports, Trump said he had secured a deal with Nvidia's board to bring Huang back, telling the board that Huang's return posed no risk to national security. On Monday, the board voted unanimously to reinstate Huang as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately, while the investigation into the allegations behind his dismissal continues. Stock futures were already sharply higher before the open, and when Nvidia's statement hit the wires, they jumped again. By the closing bell, the market had delivered its verdict: Huang's return was worth nearly a trillion dollars.

Why the market believes in one man

There is something remarkable — and slightly unnerving — about a stock market that can add $952 billion of value to a single company in a day because one man got his job back. It is a reminder of how concentrated the modern market has become, and how much of its value now rests on a small number of companies and, in Nvidia's case, on a single person. Nvidia is not just another large company. It is the cornerstone of the AI trade, the supplier of the chips without which the entire artificial intelligence industry would grind to a halt. Its graphics processing units power the data centres of Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta. Its software platform, CUDA, is the standard on which almost all AI development is built. And Huang, who has run the company for 33 years, is credited with seeing the AI opportunity a decade before anyone else, pivoting Nvidia from gaming graphics to the chips that would power the AI revolution.

That is why his resignation was so damaging, and why his return was so powerful. Investors were not just buying a chief executive. They were buying the reassurance that the person who built the AI trade is still in charge of it. As one market strategist put it, the thought had been, and Monday proved it, that Huang is uniquely important to Nvidia and to the AI trade. No other chief executive in America could move the market by a trillion dollars simply by returning to his desk. But Huang is not like other chief executives, and Nvidia is not like other companies.

There is also a political dimension to his return that investors cannot ignore. Lawmakers from both parties criticised Nvidia's decision to reinstate Huang, saying Congress should tighten restrictions on China's access to the company's advanced AI chips. The board's independent review of the allegations continues, and the Senate Banking Committee has scheduled a hearing for December 3. The questions about Huang's conduct, and about the alleged Chinese spy, have not gone away. They have simply been overtaken, for now, by the market's relief. That relief may prove short-lived if the investigation produces new revelations, or if Congress moves to restrict Nvidia's business in China. For Monday, though, the market chose to celebrate, and the celebration was worth nearly a trillion dollars.

The week that broke the market

Monday's rally did not happen in a vacuum. It was the mirror image of the previous week's rout, and the rout had its own causes. The S&P 500 had fallen for seven straight sessions before Monday, its worst run of the year. On Friday, both the S&P 500 and the Dow confirmed corrections, having fallen 10 percent or more from their highs. The Nasdaq had come close to bear-market territory. The proximate cause was Huang's resignation, but the selling had deeper roots. Investors had grown worried about the enormous sums being borrowed to fund AI infrastructure, about whether the returns on that investment would ever justify the spending, and about the political environment after the Democrats' sweeping midterm victory.

There was also a sense that the market had simply gone too far, too fast. Nvidia's shares had risen to around $240 in early October, valuing the company at $5.8 trillion — more than the entire stock market of most countries. The AI trade had become the market: a handful of technology giants accounted for a huge share of the S&P 500's value, and when they fell, everything fell with them. The correction, in that sense, was a healthy reminder that trees do not grow to the sky. But the speed of the fall — $2.8 trillion wiped off Nvidia alone in 45 days — was a reminder of how fragile the market's confidence had become, and how dependent it was on a single company's fortunes.

That fragility is the real story of Monday's rally. The market did not rise because the underlying doubts about AI spending, debt and returns had been resolved. It rose because the man at the centre of the trade came back. The doubts remain, and they will return. But for one day, at least, the market decided that the AI trade was alive, and that its leader was back where he belonged.

The winners beyond Nvidia

While Nvidia took the headlines, Monday's rally lifted almost everything connected to artificial intelligence, and the breadth of the gains was itself a signal. The S&P 500's information technology sector jumped 9 percent, its best day in years. The semiconductor index gained 14 percent, its biggest daily rise since April 2025. Micron Technology, which makes the memory chips that AI systems need, soared 19.04 percent after announcing new multi-year customer agreements. The neocloud companies — the smaller cloud providers that rent out Nvidia-powered computing — surged: CoreWeave rose 22.32 percent, Nebius 20.28 percent, Dell 20.46 percent, Bloom Energy 18.09 percent and Lumentum 18.20 percent. These are companies that had been among the hardest hit by the sell-off, and their rebound was the sharpest of all.

The mega-cap technology stocks also joined in. Meta rose 6.6 percent, Alphabet 6.3 percent and Amazon 3.5 percent. These are the companies that buy Nvidia's chips by the tens of thousands, and their gains reflected a simple logic: if Nvidia is back, the AI build-out is back, and the companies building it will benefit. The rally even spread beyond technology. The Dow, which is weighted towards older industrial and financial companies, rose 3.01 percent, its biggest points gain of the year. The market, in short, decided that the AI trade was not a narrow bet on one company, but the engine of the entire economy — and that its engine had just been switched back on.

There is a lesson in the breadth of the rally, and it is about how the market works in 2026. The S&P 500 is no longer a broad cross-section of the American economy. It is, to a remarkable degree, a bet on artificial intelligence, and the fortunes of the index now rise and fall with the fortunes of a handful of companies. That concentration is what made the sell-off so violent, and what made the rebound so spectacular. It is also what makes the market so vulnerable to the next shock — whether that shock comes from a boardroom, a courtroom or a hearing room in Washington.

What the rally does not fix

For all its drama, Monday's rally left the market's deeper problems untouched. The first is valuation. Nvidia, even after its 48 percent fall, was still one of the most valuable companies in history, and its shares, at $165.05, still trade at a multiple of earnings that would have been unthinkable for any company a decade ago. The second is debt. The AI build-out is being financed by enormous borrowing, and investors remain worried about whether the returns will justify the spending. The third is politics. The Senate Banking Committee's December 3 hearing will put Huang's conduct and Nvidia's China business under the spotlight, and lawmakers from both parties have already called for tighter restrictions on China's access to advanced AI chips. The fourth is the economy itself. October's PCE inflation data, due on Wednesday, November 25, at 8:30 a.m. ET, is expected to show headline inflation of 3.8 percent year on year, up from 3.6 percent in September, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has warned the Federal Reserve against overtightening. The odds of a December rate hike, meanwhile, have eased to about 89 to 92 percent.

None of those problems was solved on Monday. What Monday did was simpler and more emotional: it restored the market's faith in the person who built the AI trade. That faith may be justified. Huang has been right about artificial intelligence more often, and earlier, than almost anyone else in the world. But faith is not the same as fundamentals, and the market will eventually have to reckon with the questions that the sell-off raised: how much debt is too much, how fast can AI revenues grow, and how much of the economy's future should rest on one company's chips and one man's judgement.

There is also the question of what happens next to Huang himself. The board reinstated him while its independent review continues, and the investigation into the allegations behind his dismissal has not concluded. If that review produces new findings, the board could face difficult questions about why it acted before the investigation was complete. And if Congress follows through on its threats to restrict Nvidia's access to the Chinese market, the company's growth could slow sharply. For now, the market has chosen to look past those risks. But they have not gone away, and they will return — perhaps as early as the December 3 hearing.

What to watch next

The week ahead will test whether Monday's rally was the start of a recovery or a one-day wonder. The first test comes on Wednesday, November 25, when the October PCE inflation report is released at 8:30 a.m. ET. Economists expect headline inflation of 3.8 percent year on year, up from 3.6 percent in September, and a hotter-than-expected reading could revive fears that the Federal Reserve will keep rates higher for longer. The same day, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent faces a deadline related to Nvidia, and his comments about the company and about the Fed will be watched closely. Then comes the Thanksgiving holiday, traditionally a quiet week for markets, followed by the December 3 Senate Banking Committee hearing on Nvidia.

Beyond the calendar, the deeper test is whether the AI trade can sustain its gains. Monday's rally was driven by relief, not by new information about earnings, demand or technology. For the rally to last, investors will need to see evidence that the AI build-out is generating real returns, that the borrowing behind it is sustainable, and that the political risks around Nvidia and China are manageable. If that evidence comes, the market could push on to new highs. If it does not, Monday will be remembered as a spectacular dead-cat bounce — the biggest one-day gain in history, followed by the resumption of a decline.

There is one more thing to watch, and it is the simplest: Nvidia's share price. The company closed Monday at $165.05, still about 31 percent below its October peak of around $240. If the shares continue to climb back towards that peak, the market will take it as confirmation that the AI trade is intact. If they stall or fall back, the doubts will return. For now, though, the market has spoken, and it has spoken in the loudest terms available: $952 billion in a single day, the biggest one-day gain in the history of the stock market, for a company whose chief executive had been gone for eleven days and is now back.

What it means for ordinary investors

For the millions of Americans whose retirement savings are tied to the stock market through 401(k) plans, index funds and pensions, Monday was a good day — and a reminder of how much their financial security now depends on a small number of technology companies. The S&P 500 is the benchmark for most retirement savings, and its 4.73 percent jump added trillions of dollars to the value of those savings in a single session. But the previous two weeks had taken trillions away, and the volatility of the past fortnight is a warning: the market's dependence on the AI trade means that ordinary savers are now exposed to the fortunes of a handful of companies, and to the personal conduct of a handful of chief executives, in a way that would have been unimaginable a generation ago.

That exposure is not necessarily a bad thing. The AI trade has made many savers richer, and the long-term trend of the market remains upward. But it is a reminder that diversification matters, that single-day moves of 5 or 6 percent are not normal, and that the market's calm can vanish in days when confidence breaks. The sensible response is not to panic, and not to chase the rally, but to remember that the market's job is to price risk, and that risk, in 2026, is concentrated in a small number of very large bets on a single technology. Monday's rally was a reminder of how powerful those bets can be when they pay off. The previous two weeks were a reminder of what happens when they do not.

The story of Monday, November 23, will be told for years as one of the great days in market history. The S&P 500 rose 4.73 percent. The Nasdaq rose 6.51 percent. The Dow rose 1,448 points. Nvidia added $952 billion in a single day, more than double any previous record. And it all happened because a board of directors voted unanimously to bring back the man who built the company, eleven days after he had resigned. Whether that decision proves wise will be judged in the months ahead, in earnings reports, in congressional hearings and in the share price. But on Monday, at least, the market's verdict was clear, and it was worth nearly a trillion dollars.
