TITLE: UK Net Migration Just Crashed to 77,000 — the Lowest in 29 Years as 661,000 People Leave LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: UK net migration fell to 77,000 in the year to June 2026 — down 90% from its 2023 peak — as arrivals plunged and 661,000 people left Britain ---BODY---

Prime Minister Keir Starmer. Britain's net migration has fallen to 77,000 — the lowest level in almost three decades — after a series of tighter immigration rules cut arrivals and pushed departures to 661,000. Photo: UK Government via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0.

Britain has just recorded the lowest level of annual net migration in almost three decades. The Office for National Statistics (ONS) said on Thursday, November 26, 2026, that net migration — the number of people arriving minus the number leaving — fell to 77,000 in the year ending June 2026. That is down from 204,000 in the previous 12 months, a drop of more than 60 percent in a single year, and the lowest figure since 1997 outside the Covid-19 pandemic, when lockdowns and travel bans brought movement across the globe almost to a halt.

The scale of the fall is extraordinary by any measure. Just three years ago, in the year to March 2023, net migration to Britain peaked at 945,000 people — the highest figure ever recorded. Thursday's number represents a collapse of roughly 90 percent from that peak. About 738,000 people moved to the UK in the year to June 2026, the ONS said, while 661,000 people left. The difference between those two numbers — 77,000 — is the entire net addition to Britain's population from international migration over a full year.

The figures, which the ONS stressed are provisional, carry a wide margin of uncertainty: the statistics agency put the 90 percent confidence interval at between 31,000 and 128,000. Even at the top of that range, however, the number would still be the lowest in a generation. The political consequences are immediate and large. For years, immigration has been one of the most toxic issues in British politics, blamed by critics for pressure on housing, public services and wages, and wielded by successive governments as a promise they kept failing to keep. Now, for the first time in the modern era, a British government can point to a number that is not merely lower but historically low.

But the same figures carry a warning that economists and business leaders have been sounding for months. The fall was driven mainly by a drop in arrivals from outside the European Union, the ONS said — and those arrivals were not tourists. They were workers, students and asylum seekers: the nurses, care workers, engineers, researchers and fee-paying students on whom large parts of the British economy depend. The question now facing Britain is not whether immigration can be cut — that has now been demonstrated — but whether it has been cut too far, too fast, and in the wrong places.

The numbers behind the 77,000

To understand what happened, it helps to look at the two halves of the equation separately. Immigration — the number of people arriving to live in Britain for at least a year — fell to about 738,000 in the year to June 2026, down from 893,000 the year before. Emigration — the number of people leaving — rose to 661,000. Both movements pushed the net figure down at the same time: fewer people coming in, more people going out.

The fall in arrivals was broad-based. Study arrivals fell to 257,000, down from 290,000 the previous year. Work arrivals fell to 135,000, down from 180,000. And the pattern extended beyond the headline categories. Asylum migration fell, humanitarian arrivals fell, and family migration fell. Dr Peter Walsh, a senior researcher at the Migration Observatory at the University of Oxford, said in an analysis published on Thursday that the decline was driven by falling immigration across all major categories — work, study, asylum and humanitarian migration — and reflected a pattern of restrictionism that began under the previous Conservative government and has continued under the current Labour government.

The emigration side of the ledger tells its own story. Of the 661,000 people who left Britain in the year to June 2026, a striking share were British citizens themselves. Some 245,000 British nationals emigrated, while only 109,000 moved to the UK — a net outflow of British citizens of about 136,000 people. In other words, Britain is now losing its own citizens faster than it is gaining people from abroad in net terms, and the overall figure of 77,000 is positive only because arrivals of non-British nationals still slightly exceed departures.

The ONS was careful to stress that the figures are provisional estimates, subject to revision as more data becomes available. The agency's 90 percent confidence interval — 31,000 to 128,000 — reflects the difficulty of measuring migration in real time. But the direction of travel is not in doubt. The agency's own commentary described the fall as mainly driven by lower migration by people born outside the EU, continuing a trend that has been visible since 2023, when the numbers first began to fall from their record peak.

How Britain got from 945,000 to 77,000 in three years

The collapse did not happen by accident. It is the result of a deliberate, multi-year tightening of Britain's immigration rules, carried out by two governments of different political colours, and accelerated by a series of specific policy changes that each took a slice out of the numbers.

The story begins with the peak itself. Net migration to Britain surged after the Covid pandemic, reaching 745,000 in 2022 and then 945,000 in the year to March 2023. The surge was driven by a combination of factors: the reopening of borders after the pandemic, the arrival of large numbers of students and their dependants, a post-Brexit immigration system that opened work routes to non-EU nationals, and humanitarian schemes for people from Ukraine and Hong Kong. The numbers shocked the political system. The Conservative government, which had promised for years to bring net migration down to the tens of thousands, found itself presiding over the highest figures in British history.

What followed was a rapid reversal. From late 2023 onwards, the Conservative government introduced a package of restrictions: the social care visa route, which had allowed large numbers of care workers to come to Britain, was effectively closed to new dependants; salary thresholds for work visas were raised; the minimum income requirement for family visas was increased; and the refugee family reunion route was suspended. The Migration Observatory's Dr Walsh, in his analysis on Thursday, identified these measures as central to the fall, noting that the pattern of restrictionism began under the Conservatives and has been maintained since.

The current Labour government, led by Keir Starmer, has kept the restrictions in place and added its own. The government has tightened the rules further for workers, students and asylum seekers, and has made reducing net migration a central part of its political offer. The asylum system has also been reshaped: some 73,000 people sought asylum during the year covered by the new figures, and the government has pushed through measures designed to deter small-boat crossings of the English Channel. On that front, the numbers have moved too. Some 23,500 people arrived by small boat between the start of the year and November 13, a drop of nearly 40 percent compared with the same period in 2025, according to figures reported alongside the ONS release.

The result is a transformation that would have seemed implausible three years ago. Net migration has fallen by about 90 percent from its peak. The level of 77,000 is one that previous governments pursued for years and never reached — a target that was mocked as undeliverable when it was first set. It has now been delivered, not by a single policy but by the cumulative weight of dozens of restrictions, each narrowing a different route into the country.

A political victory — and a political problem

For the government, the figures are an unambiguous political win in the short term. Immigration has been the issue that has done more than any other to destabilise British politics over the past decade. It helped drive the vote to leave the European Union in 2016. It has fuelled the rise of Reform UK, the party led by Nigel Farage, which has made immigration its central cause and has surged in the polls on the back of it. And it has haunted every prime minister since David Cameron, who first promised to bring net migration down to the tens of thousands in 2010 and never came close.

Now a Labour prime minister can stand up and point to a number that is, by the standards of the last thirty years, extraordinarily low. The pressure that has built up around the issue for years has, at least on the numbers, eased. The government will argue that it has demonstrated control — that the system it inherited, which produced record numbers, has been brought under control by the measures it has taken and kept.

But the political relief comes with a catch. The same voters who want lower immigration also want a functioning National Health Service, shorter waiting lists, care for elderly relatives, and universities that can afford to keep their doors open. All of those things depend, to a significant degree, on the very people whose arrival has now been cut. The NHS employs hundreds of thousands of staff born overseas. The care sector, which looks after Britain's ageing population, has relied heavily on migrant workers for years. Universities depend on the fees paid by international students to subsidise the education of British students. If the fall in arrivals continues, all of those systems will feel the squeeze.

The Institute for Public Policy Research (IPPR), a think tank close to Labour, warned on Thursday that the risk to the British economy now is one of over-correcting. In a statement responding to the figures, the IPPR said the migration data showed the government had taken control — but that the danger had shifted from too much migration to too little. The argument, in essence, is that the headline number matters less than its composition: who is coming, who is leaving, and what they contribute. A fall in work migration, the IPPR and other analysts argue, tends to reduce the net fiscal contribution that migrants make to the public finances, because working migrants pay more in tax than they receive in public services. A fall in asylum migration, by contrast, reduces costs. The two are not the same, and a policy that cuts both indiscriminately may do more economic harm than good.

Dr Walsh made a similar point in his Oxford analysis, arguing that composition matters more than the headline number. Lower work migration tends to reduce the net fiscal contribution, he said, while lower asylum migration reflects reduced costs. The distinction matters because the political debate in Britain has almost always been conducted in terms of a single number — the net migration figure — as if all migrants were interchangeable. The new figures show why that is a mistake: the 77,000 is made up of very different groups, with very different economic effects, and the policy choices that produced it will have very different consequences depending on which groups were cut most.

What the fall means for the economy

The economic stakes are considerable. Britain's population is ageing. The number of people over 65 is rising steadily, while the birth rate has fallen to historic lows. In that context, migration has been one of the main sources of growth in the working-age population — the people who pay the taxes that fund pensions, healthcare and public services. When net migration falls to 77,000, the growth of the workforce slows sharply, and the ratio of workers to pensioners — the dependency ratio — worsens faster than it otherwise would.

The Office for Budget Responsibility, Britain's fiscal watchdog, has repeatedly warned that lower migration would damage the public finances. Its forecasts have assumed that net migration would settle at far higher levels than 77,000. If the new, lower level persists, the watchdog's projections for tax revenue, economic growth and the sustainability of public debt would all need to be revised downwards. The government would face a choice it has so far avoided: raise taxes, cut spending, or find another way to grow the economy without the workers it has just turned away.

The effects are already visible in specific sectors. The care sector, which provides home care and residential care for elderly and disabled people, has been among the hardest hit. Care providers have reported severe staff shortages since the social care visa route was tightened, and many have warned that they cannot recruit enough British workers to fill the gaps, even at higher wages. The result, in some areas, has been reduced care provision — fewer home visits, longer waiting lists for residential places, and greater pressure on families to provide care themselves.

The National Health Service faces a similar challenge. The NHS has long depended on overseas recruitment to fill nursing and medical posts, and the fall in work arrivals will make that recruitment harder. The government has said it wants to train more British nurses and doctors, and it has announced plans to expand domestic training. But training a nurse takes three years and training a doctor takes far longer, and the gap between the workers leaving and the workers being trained will not close quickly. In the meantime, hospitals and GP surgeries will compete for a shrinking pool of staff.

Universities, too, are exposed. International students pay fees that are typically two or three times what British students pay, and those fees have become a critical source of income for universities across the country. Study arrivals fell to 257,000 from 290,000 in the latest figures, and universities have warned that further falls could push some institutions into financial difficulty. The government has argued that the quality of the student route matters as much as its size, and that it wants genuine students rather than people using study as a back door to work. But the financial model of British higher education depends on the fees that international students pay, and a sharp fall in their numbers would force universities to cut courses, close departments or seek more funding from the taxpayer.

There is also a subtler economic effect. Migrants are, on average, younger than the British-born population, and they are more likely to be of working age. They are also more likely to start businesses, more likely to work in sectors with labour shortages, and — according to the ONS and the Migration Observatory — more likely to make a positive net fiscal contribution than the average British-born resident. When the number of working migrants falls, the economy loses not just their labour but their tax payments, their spending and their entrepreneurship. The IPPR's warning about over-correcting is, at its core, a warning about this: that a policy designed to cut a headline number may end up cutting the very people the economy needs most.

The small boats and the asylum system

While the headline figure dominates the coverage, the asylum numbers tell a separate and politically charged story. Some 73,000 people sought asylum in Britain during the year covered by the new figures. That is a large number by historical standards, though it is lower than the peaks of recent years, and it reflects the fact that the asylum system is driven by conflicts and crises abroad rather than by British policy alone.

Small-boat crossings of the English Channel remain the most visible and contentious part of the asylum picture. Some 23,500 people arrived by small boat between January 1 and November 13, 2026 — a fall of nearly 40 percent on the same period in 2025. The government will claim that fall as evidence that its approach — including tougher enforcement, faster processing and new agreements with France and other countries — is working. Critics will note that the numbers remain high in absolute terms, and that the crossings are driven by smuggling networks and by the desperation of people fleeing war and persecution, which British policy can influence only at the margins.

The backlog of asylum claims — the thousands of people waiting years for a decision on whether they can stay — has also been a persistent problem. The government has said it is working through the backlog, and it has introduced measures to speed up decisions and to remove people whose claims fail. But the system remains slow, expensive and, by common consent, in need of deeper reform. The new figures do not resolve that debate. They show only that the flow of new claims has eased, not that the system that processes them has been fixed.

Britain's place in a world of falling migration

Britain is not the only country experiencing a sharp fall in migration. Across the Western world, governments that once competed to attract workers are now competing to deter them. The United States, under President Donald Trump, has pursued aggressive deportation policies and tightened legal routes. Canada, which for years set ambitious immigration targets, has cut its own numbers sharply. Australia and several European countries have followed a similar path. The global mood has shifted decisively against large-scale migration, and Britain's figures are part of a broader pattern rather than an isolated case.

That matters because migration is, by definition, a global phenomenon. When one country tightens its rules, the people who would have gone there do not disappear; they go elsewhere, or they stay where they are. The competition for skilled workers — nurses, engineers, software developers, researchers — is global, and countries that close their doors risk losing talent to countries that keep theirs open. Britain's universities, hospitals and technology companies compete for the same people as their counterparts in the United States, Canada, Germany and Australia. A Britain that is harder to enter is a Britain that is less attractive to the people it most wants to attract.

There is also a demographic dimension that transcends politics. Across the developed world, birth rates have fallen below the level needed to replace the population. Japan, South Korea, Italy, Spain and Germany all face shrinking populations. Britain's birth rate is also below replacement level. Without migration, Britain's population would eventually begin to fall, and the workforce would shrink even faster. The new figure of 77,000 does not mean the population is falling — it is still growing, just much more slowly — but it does mean that the demographic cushion migration has provided for decades is now far thinner than it was.

What happens next

The most important question now is whether 77,000 is a floor or a staging post. The ONS figures are provisional, and they cover a year in which several policy changes were still working their way through the system. Some analysts expect the number to fall further before it stabilises, as the full effect of the restrictions feeds through and as emigration continues to rise. Others expect a partial rebound, as the economy adjusts and as the government comes under pressure from employers to reopen specific routes for workers in shortage occupations.

The government faces a difficult balancing act. On one side, there is the political imperative to keep the numbers low. Reform UK, which has built its entire appeal around immigration, will not be satisfied with 77,000; it will argue that the number should be lower still, and that the government should go further. On the other side, there is the economic imperative to keep the workforce growing and to fill the gaps in the NHS, the care sector and the universities. The tension between those two imperatives has defined British immigration politics for a decade, and the new figures do not resolve it. If anything, they sharpen it.

One likely outcome is a more selective system: tighter overall, but with specific routes reopened or expanded for the workers the economy needs most. The government has already signalled that it wants to prioritise high-skilled migration — the scientists, engineers and entrepreneurs who contribute most to growth — while restricting lower-skilled routes. Whether that distinction can be made to work in practice, and whether it can be done without damaging the sectors that depend on lower-skilled workers, remains to be seen.

Another likely outcome is a renewed debate about the numbers themselves. The ONS's confidence interval — 31,000 to 128,000 — is wide, and the provisional nature of the figures means they will be revised. Past revisions have been large: the ONS has previously revised net migration figures by hundreds of thousands as better data became available. The 77,000 figure may change, and the political debate will change with it. What is unlikely to change is the direction of travel. Britain's era of very high net migration — the era of 745,000 and 945,000 — appears to be over. The question now is what replaces it, and whether the country that emerges is more prosperous, more cohesive and better governed than the one that came before.

Why this number will define British politics for years

For all the caveats about provisional data and confidence intervals, the significance of Thursday's announcement is hard to overstate. A number that was once the subject of broken promises and political mockery — net migration in the tens of thousands — has now been achieved. It is the lowest figure since 1997, the year Tony Blair came to power, and it represents a fall of roughly 90 percent from a peak recorded only three years ago. No other major Western country has cut net migration so far, so fast, in such a short period.

That achievement will be claimed by the government, contested by its opponents, and studied by policymakers around the world. It will be used to argue that immigration can be controlled, that the political will to do so was the missing ingredient all along, and that the numbers were never as inevitable as they were made to seem. It will also be used to argue the opposite: that the control has come at a cost, that the cost will grow, and that a country that turns away the workers, students and families who want to come will find itself poorer, older and less dynamic as a result.

Both arguments contain elements of truth, and the debate between them will shape British politics for years to come. The figures published on Thursday do not settle that debate. They do something more important: they change its terms. For the first time in a generation, the question is no longer whether Britain can reduce net migration. It is whether Britain should reduce it further, whether it can afford the consequences of having done so, and what kind of country it wants to be now that the era of mass net migration has come to an end.

The answers to those questions will not be found in a single set of statistics. They will be found in hospital wards and care homes, in university lecture halls and on building sites, in the small-boat crossings that continue despite the fall, and in the lives of the 738,000 people who arrived and the 661,000 who left. The number is 77,000. What it means is still to be decided.
