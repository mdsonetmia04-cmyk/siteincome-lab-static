TITLE: Trump Just Gave Rudy Giuliani, 82, the Medal of Freedom — Inside the Private Oval Office Ceremony LABELS: Politics SEARCH DESCRIPTION: President Trump awarded Rudy Giuliani the Presidential Medal of Freedom in a private Oval Office ceremony honouring his lifetime of service. ---BODY---

Rudy Giuliani, former mayor of New York City, in his official congressional portrait. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

President Donald Trump has awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom — the highest civilian honour in the United States — to Rudy Giuliani, the former mayor of New York City, in a private ceremony in the Oval Office. The award, presented on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, honours a man of 82 whose career has spanned the prosecution of the mafia, the leadership of America's largest city through the September 11 attacks, and, in its final chapter, a bitter and divisive role in the effort to overturn the 2020 presidential election. Trump personally placed the medal around Giuliani's neck in a closed-press ceremony attended by family, friends and supporters, in a moment that the White House described as a tribute to a "lifetime of service to the American people" — and that critics immediately denounced as a reward for loyalty rather than for service.

The ceremony was small and, by design, private. No reporters were allowed in. The White House did not explain why the event was closed to the press. Afterwards, Giuliani's son, Andrew Giuliani, who served as executive director of the president's task force on the 2026 FIFA World Cup, spoke to reporters outside the White House and described a gathering in which Trump and his father reminisced about their long friendship. Photographs released afterwards showed Trump adjusting the medal on Giuliani's chest near the Resolute Desk, and a group photograph of Trump seated at his desk, surrounded by Giuliani and about fifteen family members and supporters. Giuliani, in a statement, said he was honoured and humbled. "I am honored and humbled to receive the Presidential Medal of Freedom from my nation and from our Commander-in-Chief, President Donald J. Trump," he said. On social media, he added a line that has become the summary of his own story: "Doing the right thing for others has been my guiding light."

Trump had announced his intention to honour Giuliani more than a year earlier, on September 1, 2025, in a post on Truth Social, days after the former mayor was seriously injured in a car crash in New Hampshire. "As President of the United States of America, I am pleased to announce that Rudy Giuliani, the greatest Mayor in the history of New York City, and an equally great American Patriot, will receive THE PRESIDENTIAL MEDAL OF FREEDOM, our Country's highest civilian honor," Trump wrote at the time. The announcement, and now the award itself, have divided Americans along lines that have defined Giuliani's career for a quarter of a century: to his supporters, he is "America's Mayor," the man who saved New York and stood tall on its darkest day. To his critics, he is a disgraced lawyer who helped spread false claims about an election and paid for it with his law licence and his reputation. Both stories are true, and the medal now sits at the centre of both of them.

America's Mayor: the career the White House chose to honour

The official citation for the award, as described by White House assistant press secretary Olivia Wales, framed the honour as a tribute to Giuliani's earlier career — the years before the controversies that came to define his later life. It is a career that, on its own terms, is extraordinary, and it is worth remembering why Giuliani was once one of the most admired public figures in America.

Giuliani first became a national figure as the United States Attorney for the Southern District of New York, the most powerful federal prosecutor's office in the country, where he served from 1983 to 1989. In that role, he took on the five mafia families that had controlled organised crime in New York for generations, bringing the leaders of the Commission — the governing body of the American mafia — to trial in 1985 and 1986 in a case that broke the power of organised crime in the city. He prosecuted corrupt politicians, Wall Street insider traders and drug traffickers, and he did it with a flair for publicity that made him a household name. The mafia prosecutions, in particular, were a genuine achievement: they dismantled an organisation that had corrupted New York's politics, its unions and its construction industry for decades.

He was elected mayor of New York City in 1993, and re-elected in 1997, serving from 1994 to 2001. As mayor, he presided over a dramatic fall in crime — murders in the city dropped by more than half during his tenure — and over a transformation of the city's public spaces, from Times Square to the parks. His supporters credit him with saving a city that, in the early 1990s, felt ungovernable. His critics argue that the crime drop was part of a national trend, and that his policing policies, including the aggressive use of stop-and-frisk, fell hardest on Black and Hispanic New Yorkers. But even his critics concede that, by the end of his second term, New York was a different city from the one he had inherited.

And then came September 11, 2001. Giuliani was in the final months of his mayoralty when two hijacked aeroplanes struck the World Trade Center, and his leadership in the hours and days that followed became the defining moment of his public life. He was on the streets within minutes of the attacks, he spoke to the city — and to the country — with a calm and a clarity that steadied a terrified nation, and he led the recovery effort in the weeks that followed. For that, he was named Time magazine's Person of the Year in 2001, he was knighted by Queen Elizabeth II, and he became, in the phrase that has followed him ever since, "America's Mayor." It is that man — the prosecutor who broke the mafia, the mayor who cut crime, the leader who held New York together on 9/11 — whom the White House citation honours.

The crash that brought the medal forward

The decision to award the medal was made in the aftermath of a frightening accident. In late August 2025, Giuliani was seriously injured in a car crash in New Hampshire, and Trump announced the award days later, on September 1, 2025, while Giuliani was still recovering. The timing gave the announcement the character of a tribute to a man who might have died — a gesture of friendship and gratitude from a president to a man who had stood by him through his most difficult years. More than a year passed between the announcement and the ceremony, and in that time Giuliani recovered, returned to public life, and attended the ceremony commemorating the 25th anniversary of the September 11 attacks in New York on September 11, 2026, at the National 9/11 Memorial and Museum.

The delay also meant that the ceremony, when it finally happened, was not a spontaneous gesture but a considered decision — one that the White House had more than a year to think about, and that it chose to carry out in private. The decision to close the ceremony to reporters is, in itself, a statement. Most Medal of Freedom ceremonies are public events, held in the East Room with cameras present and the citation read aloud. This one was held in the Oval Office, behind closed doors, with only family and supporters present. The White House has not explained the choice, but it is not hard to guess at the reason: a public ceremony would have invited the questions that a private one avoids — about the 2020 election, about the disbarment, about the defamation judgment — and the White House preferred a photograph of a medal being placed around a neck to a press conference about the man wearing it.

The other half of the story

Any honest account of Giuliani's career must also tell the other half of the story, because it is the half that makes this award so divisive. After leaving the mayor's office, Giuliani built a lucrative career as a consultant and a lawyer, and he ran, unsuccessfully, for the Republican presidential nomination in 2008. But it was his role as President Trump's personal lawyer, beginning in 2018, that brought him back to the centre of American politics — and that, in the end, cost him nearly everything he had built.

After the 2020 presidential election, Giuliani became the most visible public face of the effort to overturn the result. He appeared at press conferences, in courtrooms and on television, arguing that the election had been stolen — claims that were rejected by courts across the country, including by judges appointed by Trump himself, and that were contradicted by recounts, audits and the findings of election officials in both parties. The consequences for Giuliani were severe. His law licence was suspended in New York in 2021, and he was later disbarred in New York and in Washington, DC — the professional disgrace of a man who had once been the most feared prosecutor in America.

The most serious consequence came in a courtroom in Washington, where two Georgia election workers, Ruby Freeman and her daughter Shaye Moss, sued Giuliani for defamation after he accused them — falsely, as a jury found — of manipulating ballots. In December 2023, a jury ordered him to pay them 148 million dollars in damages, a judgment that drove him into bankruptcy. Freeman and Moss testified that Giuliani's claims had brought them threats, harassment and fear for their lives. The case became, for many Americans, the symbol of what the post-election campaign had cost — not to politicians, but to ordinary people who had done nothing more than count votes.

These are the facts that Giuliani's critics cite when they call the Medal of Freedom a disgrace, and they are the facts that the White House citation does not mention. The award, they argue, rewrites Giuliani's career, honouring the mayor of 2001 while erasing the lawyer of 2020. It is, in their telling, not a recognition of service but a reward for loyalty — a medal for a man who stood by Trump when it cost him his reputation, and who is now being repaid with the highest honour the country can bestow.

What the Medal of Freedom is — and who has received it

The Presidential Medal of Freedom is the highest civilian honour in the United States, established by President John F. Kennedy in 1963 to recognise people who have made "an especially meritorious contribution to the security or national interests of the United States, world peace, cultural or other significant public or private endeavours." It is awarded at the discretion of the president, and its recipients have included presidents and prime ministers, scientists and artists, athletes and activists, soldiers and civil rights leaders. Among them are figures whose place in American history is beyond dispute — and figures whose awards were, at the time, controversial.

That controversy is not new. Presidents of both parties have used the medal to honour political allies, donors and friends, and each time, critics have asked whether the honour has been cheapened. What is different about Giuliani's award is the scale of the controversy that surrounds him, and the fact that the honour comes not at the end of a career of public service, but after a chapter that ended in disbarment, bankruptcy and a 148-million-dollar defamation judgment. No previous recipient of the medal has arrived at the White House with a record quite like that — and that, for his critics, is the point. For his supporters, the point is the opposite: that a man who gave his best years to public service, and who was broken by the political battles of the last decade, deserves to be remembered for what he did right.

Trump has now awarded the medal to a series of allies and supporters in his second term, and Giuliani's award fits that pattern. It is a reminder that the Medal of Freedom, for all its prestige, is ultimately a presidential choice — and that each president's choices say as much about the president as they do about the recipient. In honouring Giuliani, Trump is honouring a man who stood with him, and he is also making a statement about how he sees the events of 2020: not as a crime, but as a cause, and not as a disgrace, but as a service.

The friendship at the heart of it

Underneath the politics, there is a friendship. Trump and Giuliani have known each other for decades, since the days when Giuliani was mayor of New York and Trump was the city's most famous developer. They moved in the same circles, they appeared at the same events, and when Trump entered politics, Giuliani was one of the first prominent Republicans to stand with him. He campaigned for Trump in 2016, he advised him during the presidency, and he became his personal lawyer at the moment when Trump needed a lawyer most — during the investigation into Russian interference in the 2016 election, and then during the effort to overturn the 2020 result.

That loyalty cost Giuliani dearly. He lost his law licence, his fortune and his standing. He went from being a man who commanded hundreds of thousands of dollars for a single speech to a man facing bankruptcy. Trump, who is not known for sentimentality, has repaid that loyalty in the most public way available to him: with a medal, in the Oval Office, with the cameras — eventually — allowed to see the photographs. Andrew Giuliani, speaking to reporters after the ceremony, described a gathering in which the two men reminisced about their long friendship, and in which each spoke to the small room of relatives, friends and supporters. It was, by all accounts, a warm and personal occasion, and that warmth is part of why the award matters to the people who were there.

It is also why the award is so hard for Giuliani's critics to accept. A medal given in friendship is, to them, a medal given for the wrong reasons — and a medal given to a man who helped spread false claims about an election is a medal that says those claims did not matter. The argument will not be settled by the ceremony, or by this article. It will be settled by history, and history has not yet decided what to make of Rudy Giuliani.

The long road from City Hall to the Oval Office

Giuliani's path to the Oval Office on Tuesday was a long one, and it passed through chapters that, at the time, seemed to point in very different directions. After leaving the mayor's office at the end of 2001, he founded Giuliani Partners, a security and consulting firm, and became one of the most sought-after speakers in America, commanding fees that ran into six figures for a single appearance. His reputation as the man who had led New York through September 11 made him, for a time, the most popular Republican in the country, and in 2007 he led the field for the Republican presidential nomination by a wide margin. He ran for president in 2008, betting his campaign on a late surge in Florida — a gamble that failed. He dropped out in January 2008, after finishing third in the Florida primary, and the presidency he had seemed destined for never came.

He remained a prominent figure in Republican politics, and when Donald Trump ran for president in 2016, Giuliani was among the first senior Republicans to stand with him, campaigning across the country and advising the candidate. In April 2018, he joined Trump's legal team as the president's personal lawyer, taking on the role at the height of the investigation into Russian interference in the 2016 election. It was a decision that would define the rest of his career. The lawyer who had once prosecuted the mafia was now defending a president, and the skills that had made him a formidable prosecutor — his command of the media, his willingness to fight in public, his certainty in his own cause — were turned to a new purpose.

The years since have been, by any measure, the hardest of his life. The disbarment, the defamation judgment, the bankruptcy — each one, on its own, would have ended most careers. Giuliani, who is now 82, has endured them in public, and he has done so while continuing to insist that he did nothing wrong. His attendance at the ceremony marking the 25th anniversary of the September 11 attacks, at the National 9/11 Memorial and Museum on September 11, 2026, was a reminder of the chapter of his life that no controversy can erase: the morning he walked the streets of a burning city and spoke to a frightened nation. The medal, in the end, is a judgment about which chapter matters most — and different Americans will give different answers.

Why the cameras were kept out

The decision to hold the ceremony behind closed doors deserves attention, because it says something about how the White House itself views the award. Most Medal of Freedom ceremonies are public occasions. The president reads the citation, the recipient speaks, the cameras record it, and the country watches. This one was different. It was held in the Oval Office, not the East Room. Reporters were not admitted. The only images the public has seen came afterwards, in photographs released by the White House and by Giuliani himself, showing Trump placing the medal around his neck and the group gathered around the Resolute Desk.

There are innocent explanations. The ceremony was described as a small gathering for relatives, friends and supporters, and a private occasion may simply have been what Giuliani and his family wanted. But there is also a political explanation, and it is hard to ignore: a public ceremony would have turned the award into a news conference, and the questions would have been about the 2020 election, the disbarment and the 148-million-dollar judgment. By keeping the event private, the White House ensured that the story of the day was the photograph, not the questions. It is a measure of how divisive the award is that the White House chose, in effect, to present it quietly — and it is a measure, too, of the care with which the administration handled a decision it knew would be attacked.

What happens now

Giuliani is 82 years old. He has received the highest honour his country can give a civilian, in a ceremony attended by his family, from a president he has served loyally for a decade. For him, the medal is a vindication — a sign that, whatever the courts and the bar associations have said, the president of the United States believes his life of service deserves to be honoured. For his critics, it is the opposite: a sign that the institutions that held Giuliani accountable — the courts, the bar, the jury that awarded 148 million dollars to two election workers — have been overruled, in effect, by a presidential gesture.

The award does not change any of the legal facts. Giuliani remains disbarred. The defamation judgment remains in force. The history of the 2020 election remains what the courts found it to be. A medal cannot rewrite a record, and it cannot restore a law licence. What it can do is shape how a life is remembered — and that is, in the end, what the Medal of Freedom is for. It is a statement, by the president, about who deserves to be remembered, and for what.

Rudy Giuliani will now be remembered, by the official record, as a recipient of the Presidential Medal of Freedom. He will also be remembered as the mayor who led New York through September 11, as the prosecutor who broke the mafia, and as the lawyer who helped try to overturn an election and lost everything in the attempt. All of those things are true. The medal does not resolve the contradiction. It embodies it — and that is why, on a Tuesday afternoon in the Oval Office, behind closed doors, the awarding of America's highest civilian honour to its most divisive recipient became a story about the country itself.
