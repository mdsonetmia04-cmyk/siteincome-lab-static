TITLE: Apple Sold the iPhone 16 on AI That Didn't Exist — South Korea Says 4 of 9 Features Never Arrived, $50 Million Fine Looming LABELS: Technology SEARCH DESCRIPTION: South Korea says Apple advertised 9 AI features on the iPhone 16 — but 4 never arrived as promised. A fine of up to $50M now looms. ---BODY---

Apple chief executive Tim Cook. South Korea's Fair Trade Commission says Apple advertised nine Apple Intelligence features when it launched the iPhone 16 in September 2024 — and four of them were not delivered as promised. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0, Austin Community College.

South Korea has accused Apple of selling the iPhone 16 on a promise it did not keep. The country's antitrust regulator, the Korea Fair Trade Commission, announced on Thursday, November 26, 2026, that it has begun formal sanctions proceedings against Apple Inc. and Apple Korea, alleging that the company misled consumers by advertising nine Apple Intelligence features with the iPhone 16 — four of which were never delivered as advertised. The regulator says Apple concealed, minimised or omitted information about whether the features would actually be available, and when, at the very moment millions of people were deciding whether to buy the phone.

The numbers behind the case are large. The commission calculated the sales tied to the alleged deception at approximately 3.3527 trillion Korean won — about 2.5 billion dollars — the value of iPhone 16 series sales in South Korea during the period the advertising ran. Under Korean law, a violation of this kind can draw a penalty surcharge of up to 2 percent of relevant sales, which would put the ceiling at 67 billion won, roughly 50 million dollars. The commission's examiner has recommended a fine in the range of 0.8 to 1.6 percent of those sales — between 26.8 billion and 53.6 billion won — along with corrective orders that would force Apple to stop the conduct and publicly disclose what happened. Apple now has six weeks to submit its written defence before the commission decides whether the law was broken and how much the company should pay.

The case is the most serious regulatory challenge Apple has faced in South Korea over its advertising, and it lands at a moment when the company's artificial intelligence strategy is already under intense scrutiny around the world. Apple Intelligence was the centrepiece of the iPhone 16 launch in September 2024 — the reason, Apple said, to buy a new phone. Two years later, South Korea's regulator is saying that for Korean buyers, a large part of that promise was, in its words, contrary to the actual facts.

The nine features Apple advertised — and the four that never came

When Apple launched the iPhone 16 series in September 2024, its Korean website carried a clear message: the new phone came with Apple Intelligence, the company's own artificial intelligence system, and its features would be available with the phone. According to the Fair Trade Commission, Apple advertised nine Apple Intelligence features on the iPhone 16 homepage between September 2024 and March 2025. They were: writing tools that proofread and summarise text; summaries of notes and calls; priority messages that display important messages first; Genmoji, which lets users create their own emojis from text descriptions; Image Playground, which generates images from text descriptions; Clean Up, for editing photos; Siri with the ability to understand personal context; Siri with enhanced language comprehension; and Siri with step-by-step guidance.

The investigation found that four of those nine features were not provided as advertised: Genmoji, Image Playground, the version of Siri that understands personal context, and the version of Siri capable of providing step-by-step guidance. The other five features — the writing tools, the summaries, priority messages, Clean Up and the enhanced-comprehension Siri — were delivered, the regulator said. But the four that were missing were not minor extras. They were among the most eye-catching capabilities Apple had used to sell the phone: the ability to create any emoji you can describe, to generate an image from a sentence, and to have a voice assistant that actually knows who you are, what you are doing and what you need next.

The commission's examiner concluded that Apple's advertising amounted to a serious violation of Article 3 of the Act on Fair Labeling and Advertising, the Korean law that prohibits deceptive display and advertising, including the omission of information important to consumers' purchasing decisions. "The examiner determined that the respondents' conduct constituted a serious violation of the act and recommended corrective measures, including an order prohibiting the conduct in the future and a public disclosure order, as well as the imposition of a fine," an official at the commission said, according to the Korea Times. The recommended corrective measures matter as much as the money: a prohibition order would bar Apple from repeating the conduct, and a public disclosure order would require the company to tell the public what it did.

What makes the case unusual is not just that features were late. It is that, according to the regulator, Apple advertised them as available — or as arriving on a specific schedule — at a time when their availability was uncertain, and then failed to tell buyers that the schedule had slipped. The commission said Apple "concealed, minimized, or omitted important information for consumers' purchasing decisions," in the words of a commission source quoted by the Chosun Ilbo. The distinction matters in Korean law. A company that says a feature is coming soon and then delivers it late has a problem. A company that advertises a feature as available when it is not, and hides the uncertainty, has a much bigger one.

The Siri timeline that gave the case away

The clearest example in the regulator's case is the version of Siri that understands personal context — the assistant that was supposed to know your calendar, your messages and your habits, and use that knowledge to help you. Apple advertised it as launching first in US English during 2024, and then, in October 2024, claimed on its website that it was already being serviced in US English. In February 2025, Apple advertised that Korean-language support for the feature would follow in early April 2025.

None of that happened on schedule. According to the Fair Trade Commission, the personal-context Siri did not actually launch in US English until September 2026 — almost two years after Apple said it would arrive within 2024 — and Korean-language support did not follow until October 2026, six months after the date Apple had advertised. The gap between the promise and the delivery was so large that the regulator made it the centrepiece of its case. A feature sold as a reason to buy a phone in the autumn of 2024 did not exist in Korean until the autumn of 2026, by which time the iPhone 16 was no longer Apple's newest phone at all.

The other three missing features followed a similar pattern. Genmoji and Image Playground — the tools for creating custom emojis and generating images from text — were advertised as part of the iPhone 16's Apple Intelligence suite, but were not provided as advertised. The step-by-step Siri, which was supposed to guide users through tasks one instruction at a time, also failed to arrive as promised. The commission's investigation, which began after a complaint in March 2025, established that all four features fell short of what Apple had told buyers to expect.

The timeline is important because it shows the regulator is not punishing Apple for a delay of a few weeks. The features were advertised from September 2024 to March 2025, a period of seven months during which the iPhone 16 was Apple's flagship phone and the subject of its biggest marketing campaign. Buyers who purchased the phone in that window — and in South Korea, where Apple is one of the two dominant smartphone brands, that means a very large number of people — were buying, in part, on the strength of capabilities that did not yet exist. The 3.35 trillion won in sales the commission identified is the measure of how many of those purchases were made while the advertising was running.

How the case started — and what happens next

The investigation began with a complaint. According to the Fair Trade Commission, it received a report in March 2025 that Apple had violated the Display and Advertising Act by advertising that all features of Apple Intelligence would be available upon the release of the iPhone 16 series in September 2024, despite uncertainty over whether some of the features would actually be provided. The Korea Herald reported that the probe began after a complaint from the Seoul YMCA, the consumer group. The commission has been investigating ever since — more than a year and a half of examining Apple's advertising, its internal timelines and its sales records.

On Tuesday, November 24, the commission's secretariat submitted its examination report to the full commission, and on the same day sent the report to Apple. The examination report is the formal document that sets out the facts uncovered during an investigation, the examiner's assessment of any legal violations, and the recommended remedies. Its submission opened the deliberation process — the formal proceedings in which the commission will decide whether Apple broke the law and what penalty to impose.

Apple now has six weeks from receiving the report to submit written opinions and to request access to, or copies of, the evidence as part of its right to defend itself. A commission official told the Korea Times that the company is "fully guaranteed the right to defend itself," and the commission has said it will convene its committee to make a final decision as soon as the procedures guaranteeing the company's right of defence are completed. The final decision will determine whether the law was violated and what penalty surcharge, if any, to impose — and the commission has made clear that the examiner's recommendation is only a recommendation. The commission itself will make the final call.

The fine calculation follows a set formula. For major violations of the Fair Labeling and Advertising Act, the current surcharge notice imposes a penalty of 1.5 to 1.8 percent of relevant sales. But because the violations ended before a June 2026 revision of the notice, the previous standard of 0.8 to 1.6 percent applies — which is how the examiner arrived at the range of 26.8 billion to 53.6 billion won. The absolute ceiling under the law, 2 percent of relevant sales, would be 67 billion won. The Yonhap News Agency, citing the commission, put the maximum at 64 billion won, or 47.7 million dollars — a slightly different figure that reflects the rounding of the exchange rate. Whichever figure the commission ultimately adopts, the fine would be one of the largest ever imposed on Apple in South Korea for advertising, and a signal to every technology company that sells in the country.

Why this case matters far beyond South Korea

The Fair Trade Commission's action against Apple is a Korean case, but its implications reach far beyond Seoul. It is the latest and most concrete example of a question regulators around the world are now asking about artificial intelligence: when a company sells a product on the strength of AI features, what happens when those features do not exist yet?

For the past two years, the technology industry has raced to put artificial intelligence into every product it sells. Phones, laptops, cars, televisions and kitchen appliances have all been marketed as "AI-powered," often on the strength of features that were demonstrated on stage long before they were ready for customers. The gap between the demonstration and the delivery has become a familiar pattern — a feature announced with fanfare, then quietly delayed, then delivered in a limited form, then delayed again. What South Korea's regulator is saying, in effect, is that this pattern has a legal limit: a company may not advertise a feature as available, or as arriving on a firm date, when it knows — or should know — that the feature may not be ready.

The case also lands in the middle of a tense relationship between South Korea's regulators and American technology companies. Korean media noted that the action comes while the United States government has been raising criticism of Korean regulators' sanctions on American technology companies, with one report describing the commission's continued action against US firms as "threatening the broader development of the Korea-US alliance." That political backdrop does not change the facts of the case — the dates, the missing features and the sales figures are all matters of record — but it does mean the commission's final decision will be watched closely in Washington as well as in Seoul. Apple is one of the most valuable companies in the world, and a finding that it misled consumers in one of Asia's largest smartphone markets would be a precedent that regulators in other countries could follow.

There is also a broader lesson for the industry. The iPhone 16 was not a minor product. It was Apple's flagship phone, the device the company used to introduce Apple Intelligence to the world, and the foundation of its claim that the future of the smartphone is an AI-powered assistant that knows its owner. If a regulator can show that the marketing of that phone overstated what it could do, then the marketing of every AI product deserves the same scrutiny. The commission's case is, at its heart, a consumer protection case: people paid real money for a phone, in part because of features they were told it had, and those features did not arrive. The size of the fine matters less than the principle — that an AI feature advertised on a product page must actually exist, or the company must say clearly that it does not.

Apple Intelligence and the two-year wait

To understand why the case matters, it helps to understand what Apple Intelligence was supposed to be. When Apple unveiled the system in June 2024, it described a new kind of personal intelligence: software woven into the iPhone that could write and summarise text, sort messages by importance, clean up photographs, generate images and emojis from a sentence, and — most ambitiously — power a version of Siri that understood the person using it. The personal-context Siri was the centrepiece. It would know your calendar, your messages, your photos and your habits, and it would use that knowledge to answer questions, complete tasks and anticipate what you needed. Apple presented it as the biggest change to Siri since the assistant was introduced in 2011.

The iPhone 16, launched three months later, was built around that promise. Apple marketed the phone as "built for Apple Intelligence," and the AI features were the main reason the company gave customers to upgrade. The advertising in South Korea reflected that: nine features, presented as the capabilities of the new phone, with dates attached to their arrival. The problem, as the Fair Trade Commission has now documented, is that the most ambitious of those features were not ready — not in September 2024, not in early 2025, and not, in the case of the personal-context Siri, until September 2026.

That two-year gap is the heart of the case. A buyer who purchased an iPhone 16 in the autumn of 2024 on the strength of Apple's advertising was buying a phone whose headline feature would not exist in English for another two years, and in Korean for longer still. By the time the feature finally arrived, the buyer would have owned the phone for most of its useful life — and Apple would have launched two more generations of iPhone. The commission's argument is that this is not a delay. It is a sale made on false premises.

Apple has not, in the reporting so far, publicly responded to the commission's examination report, and the company will have its opportunity to do so in the six weeks it has been given to submit its defence. It is possible that Apple will argue that its advertising carried qualifications, that the features were described as coming in the future rather than as available now, or that the delays were caused by technical difficulties that could not have been foreseen. Those arguments will be tested in the commission's deliberations. But the regulator's report is detailed and specific — it names the nine features, the dates they were advertised, the dates they were actually delivered, and the sales that were made in between — and that specificity will be difficult to answer with generalities.

What Korean buyers were told — and what they actually got

The Korean-language dimension of the case deserves particular attention, because it is where the gap between promise and delivery was widest. Apple advertised in February 2025 that Korean-language support for some Apple Intelligence services was scheduled for early April of the same year. For Korean buyers, that was a concrete date, attached to a concrete feature, in their own language. But according to the commission, certain features could not be made available in Korean as advertised, because of their actual release schedules. The personal-context Siri, advertised for Korean in early April 2025, became available in Korean only in October 2026 — eighteen months late.

This matters because language support is not a minor detail. A voice assistant that does not understand Korean is of limited use to a Korean speaker, and a set of AI features advertised in Korean, on a Korean website, to Korean buyers, carries an implicit promise that they will work in Korean. The commission's finding that Apple concealed or minimised information about the availability of features in Korean is, in that sense, the most direct part of its case: the buyers most affected were the ones who were told, in their own language, that the features were coming soon.

The scale of the affected sales underlines the point. The 3.3527 trillion won in relevant sales — about 2.5 billion dollars — represents iPhone 16 series sales in South Korea during the seven months the advertising ran. That is a very large number of phones, and a very large number of buyers who made their purchase while the advertising was in place. The fine, even at its maximum, would be a small fraction of those sales — 2 percent at most. But the corrective orders the examiner has recommended — a prohibition on repeating the conduct and a public disclosure of what happened — would have a longer-lasting effect than the money, because they would require Apple to tell the public, in plain terms, what it did.

What happens now

The next step is Apple's defence. Within six weeks of receiving the examination report, the company must submit its written opinions and may request access to the evidence the commission relied on. After that, the commission will convene its committee and make a final decision on whether the law was violated and what penalty to impose. The commission has said it will move as soon as the defence procedures are complete, but it has not set a date — and these proceedings, given the sums and the stakes involved, are unlikely to be quick.

If the commission upholds the examiner's findings, Apple would face a fine of up to 53.6 billion won under the recommended range, or up to 67 billion won under the statutory ceiling, plus the corrective orders. The company could also challenge the decision in court, as companies sanctioned by the Fair Trade Commission frequently do. Either way, the case is likely to run for months, and its outcome will be watched by every technology company that advertises AI features — and by every regulator that has wondered whether to act.

For consumers, the lesson is simpler. The iPhone 16 was sold as the first iPhone built for Apple Intelligence, and for Korean buyers, a significant part of that intelligence did not arrive for two years. The regulator's case does not say that the iPhone 16 was a bad phone, or that Apple Intelligence is a bad idea. It says something narrower and more important: that when a company tells you a feature is coming, and charges you for a phone on that basis, the feature has to come — on time, in your language, as advertised. South Korea's Fair Trade Commission is now asking Apple to answer for the gap between what it promised and what it delivered. The answer, when it comes, will matter to every company that sells the future before it is ready.

The key numbers

Nine Apple Intelligence features were advertised with the iPhone 16 in South Korea between September 2024 and March 2025. Four of them — Genmoji, Image Playground, the personal-context Siri and the step-by-step Siri — were not delivered as advertised. The personal-context Siri, promised in US English within 2024 and in Korean in early April 2025, arrived in US English in September 2026 and in Korean in October 2026. The sales tied to the advertising totalled 3.3527 trillion won, about 2.5 billion dollars. The recommended fine is 26.8 billion to 53.6 billion won; the statutory ceiling is 67 billion won, roughly 50 million dollars. Apple has six weeks to submit its defence. The complaint that started the investigation was filed in March 2025.
