TITLE: GTA 6 Made $3.79 Billion in One Day — More Than Avengers: Endgame Made in Its Entire Run LABELS: Entertainment SEARCH DESCRIPTION: GTA 6 made $3.79 billion in day-one sales with 32.5 million pre-orders, the biggest entertainment launch in history, Rockstar confirms. ---BODY---

Downtown Miami — the real-world inspiration for Vice City, the sun-soaked setting of Grand Theft Auto VI. Photo: Dori / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0).

Grand Theft Auto VI made $3.79 billion in its first day on sale, Rockstar Games and its parent company Take-Two Interactive announced on Friday, November 20 — the biggest entertainment launch in history, across any medium, ever. The game sold 32.5 million copies before it was even released, a record across all gaming platforms, and its day-one haul is nearly five times what its predecessor, GTA V, made on its first day in 2013. It is more than the entire lifetime box office of Avengers: Endgame, the highest-grossing film of all time, which took in about $2.94 billion in cinemas around the world. It is more than Avatar, more than any film, any album, any book, any single entertainment product ever released. After 13 years of waiting, after delays, trailers, leaks and rumours, after a marketing campaign reported to have cost $1 billion or more, the most anticipated game in history has arrived — and it has rewritten the record books in 24 hours. Here is what happened, how big it really is, and what comes next.

The numbers were confirmed by Rockstar Games in a press release published on November 20, a day after the game launched worldwide for PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S on November 19, 2026. According to the company, GTA VI generated $3.79 billion in worldwide day-one retail sales, based on company estimates through the end of November 19, and sold 32.5 million units pre-release. The figures were described by Rockstar and Take-Two as the biggest entertainment launch in history, and the claim is not marketing exaggeration. No film has ever opened this big. No game has ever opened this big. No single product, in the history of entertainment, has ever taken this much money in a single day. The game also achieved a 99 score on Metacritic, the review-aggregation site, one of the highest scores ever awarded to a video game — a reminder that the launch was not only a commercial triumph but a critical one.

How big is $3.79 billion, really?

To understand the scale of $3.79 billion in a single day, it helps to compare it with the records it broke. GTA V, the previous game in the series, made an estimated $815.7 million on its first day in September 2013, according to Guinness World Records — a figure that was, at the time, itself a record, and that stood for 13 years. GTA VI made about 4.65 times that amount. It sold 32.5 million copies before release, compared with the 11.21 million units that GTA V sold in its first 24 hours. And it did it in a market that is, in many ways, harder than the one GTA V faced: the game launched on only two platforms, PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S, with no PC version yet, and at a higher price than its predecessor.

The comparison with film is even more striking. Avengers: Endgame, the highest-grossing movie of all time, earned about $2.94 billion over its entire run in cinemas, according to media analytics firm Rentrak — a run that lasted months, across thousands of screens, in every country on earth. GTA VI made more than that in a single day. Its day-one haul also beats the lifetime earnings of Avatar and of every other film ever released, and it is more than three times the global opening weekend of Endgame. The game, in other words, did in 24 hours what the biggest film in history could not do in its entire theatrical life. That is not a gaming record. It is an entertainment record, full stop.

Even the most optimistic forecasts did not see it coming. Before Take-Two released its official data, the market intelligence firm Sensor Tower projected that the game had earned $2.36 billion from roughly 24.6 million digital copies across PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S by the end of launch day. Rockstar's confirmed retail total — which accounts for both digital storefronts and physical global retailers — exceeded that early projection by more than $1.4 billion. The gap is a measure of how much demand there was beyond the digital storefronts: the physical copies, the midnight queues, the fans who walked into a shop and bought a disc. It is also a measure of how hard this launch was to predict, even for the professionals who do nothing but predict it.

There is one important qualification, and it is worth stating plainly. The $3.79 billion figure is a company estimate of worldwide day-one retail sales through the end of November 19, and it includes preorder-period activity — it is not $3.79 billion of purchases placed exclusively on November 19, and it is estimated retail spending, not confirmed net revenue or profit. The money that players spent at the till is not the money that Take-Two keeps: platform fees, retailer margins, taxes and accounting deferrals all take their share before the profit is counted. But even with that qualification, the scale is extraordinary. The bottom of Newzoo's older forecast for the launch week was $3.3 billion to $5.2 billion, and Rockstar's day-one figure already exceeds the bottom of that range. Wells Fargo projects the game will sell 65 million copies by March 2027. If that projection holds, the $3.79 billion will look, in retrospect, like the beginning of something even bigger.

Thirteen years of waiting

GTA VI is the first new instalment in the blockbuster series in 13 years, and the wait has been a story in itself. GTA V was released in September 2013, and it became one of the best-selling entertainment products of all time, generating billions of dollars over more than a decade through its initial release, its re-releases, and its online mode, GTA Online. For years, fans asked when the next game would come. Rockstar said nothing, or almost nothing. The company is famously secretive, and it spent more than a decade building a game that it would not discuss, would not show, and would not date — until it did.

The game was announced, the trailers arrived, and the anticipation built to a pitch that no other entertainment product has matched. Preorders opened in June 2026, and the demand was immediate. By the time the game launched on November 19, 32.5 million people had already bought it — a record across all platforms, and a figure that meant the game was a historic success before a single copy had been played. The launch itself was a global event: midnight openings in every time zone, queues outside shops, millions of players logging on at the same moment, and a wave of social media posts, streams and videos that made the game impossible to ignore even for people who have never played a video game in their lives.

The first unlock came in New Zealand at 11:00 UTC on November 18, as the game went live at midnight local time in every region, and the American PlayStation unlock followed at 05:00 UTC on November 19. It was, in the literal sense, a worldwide launch: a single moment, repeated in every time zone, as the clock struck midnight and the game became playable. That, as much as the money, is what makes this launch historic. It was not a release. It was an event, shared by tens of millions of people at the same time, in every country, in every language, on the same day.

Lucia and Jason, and the return to Vice City

The game itself is set in Leonida, a fictional American state inspired by Florida, and its centrepiece is Vice City — the neon-soaked, sun-drenched metropolis modelled on Miami, and the setting of the beloved 2002 game GTA: Vice City. The story follows a criminal duo, Lucia and Jason, as they navigate the streets, swamps and highways of Leonida, in a tale that Rockstar has described as a modern Bonnie and Clyde story. Lucia is the series' first female protagonist in a mainline game, and her presence — alongside Jason, her partner in crime and in life — has been one of the most talked-about aspects of the game since its first trailer.

What players found when they finally got their hands on it, according to the reviews, was a game of extraordinary scale and detail. The 99 score on Metacritic reflects a critical consensus that is rare for any game, let alone one carrying this much expectation. Reviewers praised the world, the story, the characters and the sheer density of things to do, and the score places GTA VI among the highest-rated games ever made. For a game that had to live up to 13 years of anticipation, that is not a small achievement. It is, in fact, the other half of the story: the game did not merely sell. It delivered.

The setting matters, and not only for the game's atmosphere. Vice City is Miami, and Miami is a city that Rockstar has been returning to, in fiction, for more than two decades. The neon, the palm trees, the Art Deco hotels, the ocean, the swamps, the highways — all of it is recognisable to anyone who has been to South Florida, and all of it has been rebuilt, in the game, with a level of detail that makes the fictional city feel like a real place. That is part of the game's appeal: it is not a fantasy world. It is a version of a real world, exaggerated and heightened, but recognisable — and for the millions of players who know Miami, or who have visited it, or who have seen it in films and television, that recognition is part of the pleasure.

A billion dollars to make it

GTA VI is reported to be one of the most expensive entertainment projects ever made, with Take-Two said to have spent $1 billion or more on production costs. That figure, if accurate, makes it more expensive than most Hollywood blockbusters, more expensive than any game ever made, and a bet of extraordinary scale on a single product. It is the kind of spending that only makes sense if the return is equally extraordinary — and on the evidence of the first day, it is.

The scale of the spending is a measure of how the games industry has changed. A decade ago, a game that cost $100 million to make was considered expensive. Today, the biggest games cost ten times that, employ thousands of people, and take the better part of a decade to build. GTA VI was in development for years, with teams across Rockstar's studios working on a world that is larger, denser and more detailed than anything the company has made before. The $1 billion figure is not just a production budget. It is a statement about what a video game is now: not a niche product, not a toy, but the largest, most expensive and most ambitious form of entertainment on earth.

That ambition carries risk. A game that costs $1 billion to make must sell tens of millions of copies to break even, and if it fails, the loss is catastrophic. That is why the industry watched this launch so closely, and why the $3.79 billion figure matters beyond the headlines. It is proof that the bet paid off — that a game can cost $1 billion to make and return nearly four times that in a single day. It is also a signal to every other publisher in the industry: the ceiling has moved. The biggest games are now bigger than the biggest films, and the economics of entertainment have shifted, permanently, towards the interactive.

The people who made it — and their fight for a union

Behind the numbers and the records, there is a quieter story, and it is one that Rockstar would probably prefer not to be told. The workers who made GTA VI are voting on whether to recognise their branch of the Independent Workers Union of Great Britain, in a ballot that runs between November 12 and November 26. The vote is a landmark for the games industry, in which unionisation has been rare and in which workers have long complained of long hours, job insecurity and "crunch" — the practice of working extreme overtime in the final months before a game's release.

The timing of the vote, in the middle of the biggest launch in entertainment history, is not a coincidence. The workers who built a game that made $3.79 billion in a single day are asking for the job security and the working conditions that, they say, the success of their work should guarantee. It is a story that runs counter to the celebration, and it is one that will continue after the headlines fade. The game is a triumph. The question of who benefits from that triumph — and on what terms — is still being decided, and the ballot that closes on November 26 will be part of the answer.

It is a tension that runs through the entire games industry, and it is not going away. The games that make the most money are made by the largest teams, over the longest periods, under the most pressure, and the people who make them have begun to organise. GTA VI is the biggest game ever made, and it may also be the game that changes how games are made — not only in what they contain, but in how the people who make them are treated. That would be a legacy to match the sales figures.

What comes next: PC, online, and 65 million copies

For all its success, GTA VI has not yet finished its launch. The game is currently available only on PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S. Its PC release and its online mode, GTA Online's successor, are expected in 2027, and when they arrive they will open the game to tens of millions more players — PC gamers, who have waited for every Grand Theft Auto game, and online players, who made GTA V's online mode into a phenomenon that generated billions of dollars over a decade. The $3.79 billion, in other words, is not the total. It is the beginning.

Wells Fargo projects that the game will sell 65 million copies by March 2027, and if that projection is right, the total revenue will dwarf even the day-one figure. The online mode, when it arrives, is expected to generate revenue for years, through in-game purchases, expansions and events, in the same way that GTA Online did. Take-Two's guidance, reported before the launch, suggested Rockstar-label revenue of approximately $2.96 billion to $3.03 billion for the relevant period — a figure that the day-one sales have already exceeded. The company, in short, has underestimated its own success, and the market will be watching its next financial results with intense interest.

For Take-Two Interactive, the parent company, the launch is transformative. The company is also the publisher behind other hits, and its share price will reflect, in the months ahead, whether investors believe the $3.79 billion is a one-off or the start of a new era. For Rockstar Games, the studio, it is a vindication of 13 years of silence, secrecy and work. And for the games industry, it is a moment of reckoning: the biggest entertainment launch in history was a video game, and the biggest entertainment product in the world is now interactive.

The road to launch: five months of preorders

The $3.79 billion did not appear out of nowhere on launch day. It was five months in the making. Rockstar opened preorders for GTA VI in June 2026, and from that moment the sales began to accumulate — 32.5 million copies sold before the game was even released, a record across all platforms. The preorder figure is, in its own right, one of the most remarkable numbers in the history of entertainment: tens of millions of people paid for a game they had not played, had not reviewed, and in many cases had only seen in trailers, on the strength of a name and a reputation. It is a measure of trust — trust in Rockstar, trust in the Grand Theft Auto series, and trust that 13 years of waiting would be rewarded.

The official figures were disclosed by Take-Two Interactive in a Form 8-K filing, Exhibit 99.1, dated November 20, 2026 — the formal, legal document in which a public company tells its investors and the market what has happened. The filing states the day-one estimate and the preorder figure, and it is the source on which the reporting of the launch has been based. There is no cut-off time given in the release; Take-Two says the figure covers sales "through end of day November 19 worldwide." That precision matters, because it is what separates the official figure from the estimates that circulated before it. Sensor Tower's early estimate of $2.36 billion, based on digital sales alone, was a serious piece of market intelligence — and it was wrong by more than $1.4 billion, because it could not see the physical copies, the retail sales, and the full scale of a launch that spanned every shop and every storefront on earth.

A record that will stand — until it doesn't

Records are made to be broken, and this one will be broken eventually — by another game, or by a film, or by something that has not been invented yet. But the scale of GTA VI's achievement makes it a record that will stand for a long time. To break it, a product would have to take more than $3.79 billion in a single day, sell more than 32.5 million copies before release, and beat the lifetime earnings of the biggest film ever made in 24 hours. That is a high bar, and it is one that only a product with the same combination of anticipation, scale and global reach could clear. For now, the record belongs to Lucia and Jason, to Vice City, to Rockstar Games, and to the 32.5 million people who bought a game before they had played it — and who, on the evidence of the reviews, were not disappointed.

The story of GTA VI is, in the end, a story about what entertainment has become. A video game, made over 13 years at a cost of $1 billion or more, has outsold the biggest film in history in a single day. It has done so not by accident but by design: by building a world that tens of millions of people wanted to enter, and by making them wait long enough to want it desperately. The wait is over. The game is here. And the numbers — $3.79 billion, 32.5 million copies, a 99 on Metacritic — will be quoted for years, as the measure of what a launch can be, and as the moment when the games industry became, unambiguously, the biggest entertainment industry on earth.
