TITLE: Europe Just Picked Its Next Central Bank Chief — Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos Takes Over the ECB in February LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: EU leaders picked Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos, 55, to succeed Christine Lagarde as ECB president — the first Spaniard to hold the job. ---BODY---

Pablo Hernández de Cos in his official portrait, 2025. European leaders chose the 55-year-old Spaniard on November 26, 2026, to succeed Christine Lagarde as president of the European Central Bank. Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC0.

Europe has a new central bank chief. On Thursday, November 26, 2026, European Union leaders meeting in Brussels chose Spain's Pablo Hernández de Cos to be the next president of the European Central Bank, handing the most powerful economic job on the continent to a 55-year-old career central banker who has spent his working life inside the institutions he will now lead. He will be the first Spaniard ever to run the ECB, and he will take over in February 2027, when Christine Lagarde steps down nine months before her eight-year term was due to end.

The choice ends a race that began the moment Lagarde announced, on October 30, that she would resign on February 7. It also settles, at least for now, a contest that pitted the Spanish technocrat against Klaas Knot, the former head of the Dutch central bank, and it sets up a changing of the guard at the Frankfurt institution at one of the most difficult moments in its history: France is wrestling with a debt crisis, growth across the eurozone is flagging, stock markets are jittery, and inflation is being driven higher by the war in Iran. The man who will steer the euro through all of that is, by every account, a quiet, methodical economist who prefers spreadsheets to speeches.

French President Emmanuel Macron confirmed the result on X, welcoming what he called a "balanced trio of individuals serving the stability and prosperity of the euro area, with Pablo Hernández de Cos as President and Laurence Boone and Markus Brunnermeier on the Executive Board." The announcement was also made by António Costa, the president of the European Council, who said the euro area member states had endorsed the appointment. The formal process now runs through the European Parliament and the ECB's own governing council, with a final seal of approval by EU leaders expected at their December summit. Those steps are widely regarded as formalities.

Who is Pablo Hernández de Cos?

Pablo Hernández de Cos is 55 years old, and his career has been, in the words of the Wall Street Journal, that of a career central banker. He got his start as an economist at Spain's central bank, the Banco de España, and rose through its ranks until he became its governor, a post he held from 2018 to 2024. In that role he also sat on the ECB's rate-setting governing council for six years, so the room he will now chair is one he already knows from the inside.

Since leaving the Bank of Spain, he has been the general manager of the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, Switzerland — the forum for the world's major central banks, often described as the central bank for central banks. It is a job that put him at the centre of the global conversation among monetary policymakers, and it is the post he will leave to take the ECB presidency. The Associated Press, reporting from Brussels, described him as the former head of Spain's central bank, and noted that his nomination still must pass review by the European Parliament and the ECB's governing council, but that those steps are formalities.

He won out over one other main contender, Klaas Knot, the former head of the Netherlands' central bank, who was regarded as a hawk — someone who favours higher interest rates to ward off inflation. According to diplomats who spoke to Le Temps on condition of anonymity, the Netherlands, which had formally put forward Knot's candidacy, eventually fell in behind the Spaniard, who had received the decisive backing of Paris and Berlin. The contest, in the end, was settled the way so many European appointments are settled: by a consensus built in the capitals, with the biggest countries' support proving decisive.

The other two posts decided on Thursday complete the new leadership team. Laurence Boone of France and Markus Brunnermeier of Germany were recommended as members of the ECB's executive board, succeeding Germany's Isabel Schnabel and Ireland's Philip Lane. According to Reuters, Boone is in line to head the ECB's market operations, while Brunnermeier is set to become the bank's chief economist. Together with de Cos, they form the trio Macron praised — a Spaniard at the top, a Frenchwoman and a German on the board — a balance of nationalities that matters enormously in an institution where the big member states watch each other's influence with a jeweller's eye.

Why Lagarde is leaving early

Christine Lagarde is 70, and she announced on October 30 that she would resign on February 7, nine months before the end of her eight-year term, which was due to run until October 2027. Her early departure set off a race against time among the 21 countries that share the euro, because the appointment of her successor had to be completed by the end of the year, following a process that includes consulting the European Parliament and the ECB's governing council.

Lagarde's tenure at the ECB began in November 2019, when she became the first woman to lead the institution. Before Frankfurt, she had run the International Monetary Fund, and before that she was France's finance minister. Her presidency spanned the pandemic, the sharpest inflation surge in a generation, and the fastest interest-rate increases in the ECB's history, followed by the careful path back down. Her decision to leave early was her own, announced in her own words at the end of October, and it left Europe with barely three months to choose the person who would take the euro's helm.

That compressed timetable is one reason the decision came as it did: a quick, consensus-driven choice of a known quantity, rather than a long, divisive contest. De Cos was the candidate who could command support in Paris and Berlin, who knew the ECB's machinery, and who carried no political baggage that would alarm the markets. The Wall Street Journal's verdict was that European leaders were "betting a technocrat can navigate the continent through debt problems in France, flagging growth and stock-market jitters." It is a bet on steadiness, not on drama.

The in-tray: France, inflation and a French election

What de Cos inherits is, by any measure, a daunting in-tray. The Associated Press reported that the ECB confronts "inflation driven by the war in Iran and worries about high government debt and borrowing costs in Europe." France, the eurozone's second-largest economy, is at the centre of those worries: its public finances are under strain, its borrowing costs have risen, and its politics are unsettled. The Wall Street Journal noted that de Cos "will step into the role two months before France begins voting to choose a successor to departing President Emmanuel Macron." A presidential election in France, in the spring of 2027, will be one of the first great tests of his presidency — and the markets will be watching every word he says about it.

Then there is the interest-rate question. The ECB's deposit facility rate — the rate the bank pays on overnight deposits, and the one the headlines quote — stands at 2.50%, in force since September 16, 2026, when the governing council raised it by a quarter of a percentage point. The main refinancing rate is 2.65% and the marginal lending rate is 2.90%. The governing council last met on October 29 and left all three rates unchanged. Its next decision comes on Thursday, December 17, 2026 — before de Cos takes office, but with the new president's shadow already falling across the room.

And the markets are already looking past December. All 71 economists in a Reuters poll taken from November 10 to 12 expected the ECB to raise its deposit rate to 2.75% at the December meeting, and market pricing now almost fully reflects a quarter-point hike. Whether de Cos, once in office, continues that tightening path or pauses to assess the damage from higher borrowing costs will be the single most-watched question of his early months. The rate path matters to everyone in the eurozone: to governments borrowing to fund their budgets, to companies deciding whether to invest, and to the roughly 350 million people whose mortgages and savings rates follow, sooner or later, from what the ECB does in Frankfurt.

Growth is the other half of the problem. The eurozone economy has been flagging, and the combination of high debt, war-driven energy costs and trade tensions has left policymakers with little room for error. Raise rates too far and growth stalls; cut too soon and inflation, stoked by the war in Iran and by energy prices, comes roaring back. It is the classic central banker's dilemma, and de Cos will face it from his first day.

What the job actually is

To understand why this appointment matters so much, it helps to understand what the ECB president actually does. The European Central Bank, based in Frankfurt, is the central bank for the 21 countries that use the euro. Its governing council — the six members of its executive board plus the governors of the 21 national central banks — sets interest rates for the whole euro area. Its primary mandate, written into the European treaties, is price stability: keeping inflation close to 2% over the medium term.

The president chairs the governing council, sets its agenda, speaks for the institution to the world, and represents the eurozone at the great gatherings of global finance — the International Monetary Fund, the Group of Seven, the Bank for International Settlements. When the president speaks, markets move. A single phrase in a press conference can shift the value of the euro, the price of government bonds across the continent, and the cost of borrowing for hundreds of millions of people. It is, for that reason, one of the most consequential jobs in the world economy, and one of the most closely watched.

The list of de Cos's predecessors shows the weight of the office. Wim Duisenberg, the first president, built the institution from scratch when the euro was born in 1999. Jean-Claude Trichet steered it through the global financial crisis. Mario Draghi, who led the bank from 2011 to 2019, is remembered above all for three words spoken in London in July 2012 — "whatever it takes" — a promise to do whatever was needed to preserve the euro, which calmed a sovereign debt crisis that had threatened to tear the currency apart. Lagarde, the fourth president, led it through the pandemic and the inflation surge that followed. De Cos will be the fifth. Each of them inherited a crisis, and each of them is remembered for how they handled it.

A technocrat, not a politician

What stands out about de Cos, in the portraits drawn by those who know him, is how un-political he is. He is not a former finance minister, as Lagarde was, nor a figure who has campaigned for the job in public. He is an economist who worked his way up through a national central bank, who spent six years on the ECB's governing council, and who now runs the Bank for International Settlements — the institution where the world's central bankers meet, in Basel, to talk shop away from the cameras. His appointment is, in that sense, a return to type for the ECB: a technocrat chosen for competence rather than for political symbolism.

That profile has advantages. Markets tend to trust central bankers who have done the job before, and de Cos has done it at every level: as a staff economist, as a national governor, as a member of the ECB's council, and as the head of the BIS. He knows the people, the procedures and the pressures. The risk, if there is one, is that the ECB presidency is also a political job — it requires managing 21 national governors with different national interests, facing down governments that want lower rates, and communicating with a public that is increasingly sceptical of distant institutions. The technocrat's skill set and the politician's skill set are not the same, and the coming years will test whether de Cos has both.

There is also the question of style. Lagarde was a communicator: a former lawyer and politician who spoke in clear, memorable language and who made a point of explaining the ECB's decisions to ordinary Europeans. Draghi was a different kind of communicator: terse, deliberate, and capable of moving markets with a single sentence. De Cos's own style, at the Bank of Spain and at the BIS, has been quieter — careful, analytical, and low-key. The markets will learn his voice quickly enough; the first press conference will be studied word by word, as every ECB president's first press conference is.

Spain's moment

For Spain, the appointment is a landmark. De Cos will be the first Spaniard to lead the ECB in its 27-year history, and the first Spaniard to head any of the world's most influential central banks. It is a recognition, too, of how Spain's economy has performed: after the devastation of the euro crisis a decade ago, when Spanish unemployment topped 26%, the country has become one of the eurozone's fastest-growing economies, and its central bank governor is now the choice of Europe's leaders to run the institution at the top.

The symbolism matters in Madrid, and it matters in the politics of the appointment. The ECB's top jobs have traditionally been shared among the big member states — a German here, a Frenchwoman there, an Italian before that — and the fact that the presidency has now gone to Spain reflects a slow shift in the balance of the eurozone. Spain is the fourth-largest economy in the currency union, and its turn at the top of the ECB has been a long time coming. The appointment of Boone, a Frenchwoman, and Brunnermeier, a German, to the executive board keeps the traditional balance intact: Paris and Berlin remain represented at the highest level, even as the presidency itself moves south.

There is a neat symmetry in the timing, too. De Cos takes over in February, and two months later France begins voting for a successor to Emmanuel Macron — the president who, on Thursday, was among the first to confirm the appointment on social media. Macron's own legacy, in part, is bound up with the European institutions he has championed, and the choice of a steady, consensus candidate to run the ECB is very much in his mould. Whether the next French president shares that vision is a question for the spring.

The road to December — and beyond

The process from here is straightforward, if not quite finished. The European Parliament and the ECB's governing council must be consulted, and the appointment must be formally approved by EU leaders at their December summit. The Parliament's opinion is not binding, and the political agreement reached in Brussels on Thursday means the appointment is, as the Straits Times put it, "essentially assured." The formalities are expected to be completed before the end of the year, clearing the way for de Cos to take office when Lagarde steps down on February 7.

What happens after that is the real story. De Cos will chair his first governing council meeting in the spring of 2027, with France in the middle of a presidential election, with the war in Iran still shaping energy prices, and with the eurozone's governments under pressure to bring their debts under control. He will have to decide, meeting by meeting, whether interest rates should go up, down, or stay where they are — and each decision will be second-guessed by markets, by governments, and by the millions of Europeans whose livelihoods depend on the answer.

He will also have to manage the institution itself. The ECB employs thousands of people in Frankfurt, supervises the eurozone's biggest banks, and is at the centre of debates about the future of the euro — including whether to launch a digital euro, how to handle the vast bond holdings accumulated during years of crisis-fighting, and how to respond to the rise of new financial technologies. The presidency is not only about interest rates; it is about the architecture of European money itself.

For now, though, the story is simpler: Europe's leaders have made their choice, and they have chosen a man who knows the institution from the inside, who has the backing of Paris and Berlin, and who has spent his career preparing, whether he knew it or not, for exactly this job. On February 7, 2027, Pablo Hernández de Cos will walk into the ECB's Frankfurt headquarters as its fifth president — the first Spaniard to hold the post, and the man charged with steering the euro through whatever comes next. The continent will be watching.

The December decision — his first test before he starts

The first formal test of the new era comes before de Cos even takes office. On Thursday, December 17, 2026, the ECB's governing council will announce its last interest-rate decision of the year, at 14:15 Frankfurt time, with the press conference about half an hour later. Lagarde will still be in the chair. But the decision will be read, by every trader and economist in Europe, as the opening move of the de Cos era — because the man who inherits the decision will have to live with it.

The expectations are unusually clear. All 71 economists in a Reuters poll taken from November 10 to 12 expected the ECB to raise its deposit rate to 2.75% at the December meeting, and market pricing now almost fully reflects a quarter-point hike, according to Nordea. The money markets agree: on November 25, one-month Euribor — the rate that runs past the decision — stood at 2.432%, while one-week Euribor, which ends before any new rate would apply, stood at 2.421%. The gap, tiny as it is, points in one direction. If the council delivers the hike, the deposit rate will stand at 2.75% when de Cos walks into the building in February, and his first decisions will be about whether to keep going, pause, or stop.

That matters because the rate path is the ECB's main tool, and it is a blunt one. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive for governments, for companies and for households, and they cool spending — which is how they bring inflation down. But they also slow growth, and growth in the eurozone is already flagging. The December hike, if it comes, will be the third increase in the space of a few months, following the rises that took the deposit rate to 2.25% in June and 2.50% in September. De Cos will inherit a tightening cycle that is already under way, and a debate — inside the council and outside it — about how much further it should go.

The hawks on the council, of whom Klaas Knot was the most prominent voice, have argued that inflation must be crushed whatever the cost to growth, and that the war in Iran gives the ECB no choice but to keep rates high. The doves argue that the eurozone economy is too fragile for aggressive tightening, and that the inflation driven by war and energy prices will fade on its own if the bank is patient. De Cos's own record suggests he sits somewhere in the middle: a central banker who takes inflation seriously, but who has also spent his career studying how economies actually behave, rather than how textbooks say they should. His first press conference will be watched for any hint of where he stands — and the markets, as they always do, will over-read every word.

What the markets will watch in his first 100 days

The first hundred days of a central bank presidency are, by tradition, a period of intense scrutiny. Every speech is parsed, every interview is dissected, and every vote of the governing council is examined for signs of the new president's influence. For de Cos, the calendar of those hundred days is already crowded. He takes office on February 7, 2027. Within weeks, he will chair his first governing council meeting. Within two months, France will begin voting for a successor to Emmanuel Macron. And through it all, the war in Iran will continue to shape energy prices, the French government's debt will continue to weigh on bond markets, and the eurozone's governments will continue to argue about how much they can afford to spend.

The French election is the event that will dominate his first spring. France is the eurozone's second-largest economy, and its public finances are the single biggest worry in the currency union. A new French president could change the country's fiscal policy, its attitude to European rules, and its relationship with the ECB itself. De Cos will have to navigate all of that without appearing to take sides — because a central banker who is seen to favour one candidate, or one party, loses the independence that is the whole point of the job. It is a test of diplomacy as much as of economics, and it comes almost immediately.

Beyond the headlines, there is the quieter work of running the institution. The ECB supervises the eurozone's biggest banks, and its decisions on supervision shape the financial system in ways that rarely make the news but matter enormously when things go wrong. It is also at the centre of the debate about a digital euro — a central-bank digital currency that would, if launched, change how Europeans pay for things. And it holds vast portfolios of government bonds, bought during years of crisis-fighting, which it is slowly reducing. Each of these is a decision that will fall, in part, to the new president, and each of them will be watched by people who understand exactly how much is at stake.

For now, the markets have given their verdict on the appointment, and it is a calm one. There was no sharp move in the euro, no jump in bond yields, no sign of alarm. That, in itself, is a compliment: Europe's leaders chose a man the markets already know, already trust, and already understand. The drama, if it comes, will come later — in the decisions he makes, the words he chooses, and the crises he cannot yet see. On February 7, Pablo Hernández de Cos will take the chair in Frankfurt. The quiet technocrat will have the loudest job in European economics, and the whole continent will be listening to what he says next.
