North Korea has temporarily suspended its military support for Russia, halting deliveries of ballistic missiles and artillery shells and barring Russian military transport aircraft from its airspace, according to Ukraine's military intelligence agency. The claim, published on November 26, 2026, by the Main Directorate of Intelligence of Ukraine's Defence Ministry, known as the GUR or HUR, is one of the most surprising developments of the war in Ukraine — and one of the least certain. If it is true, it means that the closest military partnership Russia has built during its war has cracked, and that the man who ordered the crack is Kim Jong Un himself. If it is not true, or if it is exaggerated, it is still a significant piece of information warfare, published at a moment when the diplomacy around the war is moving faster than it has in months. Either way, the claim deserves to be reported carefully, with its source stated plainly and its limits made clear: Reuters, which reported the story, said it was unable to independently verify the content of the report, and the Ukrainian agency did not disclose how it obtained the information.

According to the GUR, the suspension was announced by North Korea's Foreign Minister, Choe Son Hui, in late October, and the decision was made personally by Kim Jong Un. The measures, as described by the Ukrainian agency, are specific and sweeping. North Korea has halted further deliveries of KN-23 ballistic missiles, the short-range weapons that Russia has fired at Ukrainian cities and energy infrastructure. It has suspended the next batch of artillery ammunition, the shells that have fed Russia's artillery-heavy way of war. It has barred Russian military transport aircraft from using North Korean airspace. And it is holding back on a Russian request to increase the number of North Korean troops stationed in Russia from 8,500 to 50,000 — a sixfold expansion that Moscow had been seeking and that Pyongyang has now, according to the Ukrainians, refused to implement. The GUR's statement, published on its website on Thursday, November 26, and reported by Reuters, Interfax-Ukraine, Ukrinform and the Kyiv Independent, is the first public claim that the military partnership between Moscow and Pyongyang has not just cooled but been formally suspended.

The reported cause of the rupture is as strange as the rupture itself. According to the GUR, the trouble began in October, when Russia's Deputy Foreign Minister, Andrei Rudenko, said that Moscow would help to de-escalate tensions between North Korea and South Korea. To most ears, that sounds like a harmless diplomatic pleasantry. To Pyongyang, according to the Ukrainian account, it was an offence. Choe Son Hui, the North Korean foreign minister, is said to have responded at the end of October that such statements were inconsistent with the nature of the alliance agreements between the two countries, and to have demanded that Moscow clarify its policy towards South Korea. The suspension of military support, the GUR says, followed from that demand. The remarks by Rudenko that are said to have triggered the backlash have not been independently confirmed, and the Ukrainian agency has not explained how it learned of the internal deliberations in Pyongyang. The story, in other words, rests entirely on the word of one of the parties to the war — and that party has every reason to want the world to believe that Russia's allies are deserting it.

What North Korea has given Russia — and why it matters

To understand why a suspension would matter, it is necessary to understand the scale of what North Korea has supplied. According to a Reuters investigation, North Korea has been supplying Russia with weapons since 2023, and with troops since 2024. The weapons include KN-23 ballistic missiles, a North Korean short-range missile closely modelled on Russia's own Iskander system, and millions of rounds of artillery ammunition. The troops include soldiers who fought Ukrainian forces after Ukraine's incursion into Russia's Kursk region, where North Korean units were deployed alongside Russian forces and suffered heavy casualties. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said on November 7 that Russia had fired KN-23 missiles at a Ukrainian energy facility, and on November 16 and 17 he said that dozens of North Korean-made ballistic missiles are supplied to Russia every month. The scale of the supply is what makes the reported suspension significant: if dozens of missiles a month have suddenly stopped arriving, Russia's ability to sustain its bombardment of Ukrainian infrastructure will be measurably reduced.

The artillery is, if anything, more important than the missiles. Russia's war has been, from the start, an artillery war, and the country has burned through its Soviet-era stockpiles at a rate that its own factories have struggled to match. North Korean shells — millions of them, by most estimates — have filled the gap. They are old, they are of variable quality, and they have reportedly caused accidents in Russian guns, but they exist in enormous quantities, and quantity is what matters in an artillery war. A suspension of the next batch of artillery ammunition, if it is real and if it lasts, would bite into Russia's firepower within weeks. The same logic applies to the KN-23 missiles, which have become a regular feature of Russia's strikes on Ukrainian cities. Ukraine's air defences have learned to intercept many of them, but each missile that is not fired is one fewer that has to be intercepted, and one fewer that can hit a power station, a hospital or a residential block.

The troop question is the most dramatic of all. According to the GUR, Russia had asked North Korea to increase its military contingent in Russia from about 8,500 troops to 50,000 — a request that, if granted, would have made North Korea a major participant in the war on the ground, not just a supplier of weapons. Pyongyang is now, the Ukrainians say, holding off on that request. The figure of 50,000 is itself remarkable: it suggests that Moscow's appetite for foreign manpower is far from satisfied, and that the Russian military is looking for tens of thousands of additional soldiers from wherever it can find them. The refusal, if it is real, is a significant rebuff, and it suggests that the relationship between the two countries is under strain in ways that go beyond a single diplomatic remark.

The Tumen River bridge: a partnership built in concrete

The reported suspension is all the more surprising because the partnership between Moscow and Pyongyang has, until now, appeared to be deepening. On September 7, 2026, Russia and North Korea held an opening ceremony for a road bridge across the Tumen River — the Tumangang, on the border between the two states — that is expected to become an important logistics hub, accelerating the growth of bilateral transport between the two countries. The bridge is a physical investment in the relationship, the kind of infrastructure that is built when two governments expect to be doing business together for a long time. The GUR's own statement noted the bridge opening, and the contrast between a new bridge built to carry more goods across the border and a suspension of military supplies across the same border is striking. It suggests either that the suspension is a short-term diplomatic signal rather than a permanent break, or that the relationship is more complicated — and more transactional — than the public displays of friendship have suggested.

The strategic partnership between the two countries was formalised in a treaty signed in 2024, which includes a mutual defence clause, and it has been the subject of repeated public celebration. Kim Jong Un visited Russia in September 2023, meeting Vladimir Putin at the Vostochny Cosmodrome and touring Russian military facilities. Putin visited Pyongyang in June 2024, the first visit by a Russian leader in 24 years, and the two men were photographed driving together, toasting each other and signing the treaty that now binds their countries. The imagery was designed to convey permanence: two isolated leaders, united against a common enemy, building a partnership that would outlast the war. The GUR's claim, if true, suggests that the permanence was an illusion, and that the partnership is as fragile as any alliance built on convenience rather than trust.

Why would Kim do this? The view from Pyongyang

If the suspension is real, what would motivate Kim Jong Un to order it? The Ukrainian account offers one answer: anger at Rudenko's remarks about de-escalation with South Korea. That answer deserves scrutiny, because it is, on its face, a strange reason to cut off a military ally. North Korea's entire foreign policy is built on the premise that South Korea is an enemy state, and the idea that Russia might help to ease tensions between the two Koreas is, from Pyongyang's perspective, not a kindness but a threat. It implies that Moscow sees a future in which the two Koreas talk to each other, and in which North Korea's isolation — the very isolation that makes it dependent on Russia and China — might ease. For a regime whose survival depends on maintaining a state of permanent confrontation, even the suggestion of de-escalation can be read as a betrayal. The GUR's account has Kim and his foreign minister reacting to the suggestion with fury, and using the suspension of military support as a way of forcing Moscow to clarify its position. It is, in this telling, a punishment and a warning: do not presume to speak for us about our enemy.

There are other possible explanations, and the Ukrainian agency itself hinted at one of them. The GUR's analysis, as reported by South Korea's SBS, said that massive losses among the North Korean troops deployed in Russia, and the contemptuous attitude of the Russian military leadership towards North Korean soldiers, are factors hindering the expansion of military cooperation. The North Korean soldiers who fought in Kursk are reported to have suffered heavy casualties, and to have been treated by their Russian counterparts as expendable. If those reports are accurate, they would give Kim a powerful reason to refuse a request to send 50,000 more of his soldiers into a war that is already killing them in large numbers. A leader who sends his soldiers to die in a foreign war needs to be able to tell his people, and his generals, that the sacrifice is worth it. Heavy losses and contemptuous treatment make that story hard to tell, and a pause in the deployment is a way of signalling displeasure without breaking the alliance outright.

A third possibility is that the suspension is a negotiating tactic — a lever that Pyongyang is using to extract a better price from Moscow for its continued support. North Korea's economy is in desperate need of food, fuel and hard currency, and its military support for Russia has been paid for, in part, with Russian oil, food and technology. If Kim believes that Moscow needs his missiles and shells more than he needs Moscow's payments, a temporary suspension is a way of testing that belief and of raising the price. The history of North Korea's diplomacy is full of such tactics: the regime has repeatedly created crises in order to extract concessions, and a suspension that can be reversed is a more useful tool than a rupture that cannot. The word "temporary" in the GUR's account is doing a lot of work, and it may be the most important word in the whole story.

What we do not know — and why it matters

The most important thing to say about this story is how much of it cannot be verified. The claim comes from a single source: Ukraine's military intelligence agency, which is a party to the war and which has an obvious interest in portraying Russia's alliances as crumbling. The agency did not disclose how it obtained the information, and it did not provide documents or other evidence. Reuters, which reported the story, said explicitly that it was unable to independently verify the content of the report. The Kyiv Independent, which also reported it, noted that the agency had not disclosed how it obtained the information and that the contents could not be independently verified. The remarks by Deputy Foreign Minister Rudenko that are said to have triggered the backlash have not been confirmed by any independent source. And neither Moscow nor Pyongyang has commented publicly on the claim. In the absence of confirmation from either capital, the story must be treated as a claim, not as an established fact, and any reporting that presents it otherwise would be misleading.

That does not mean the claim is false. Intelligence agencies sometimes publish information that is accurate, and the GUR has a track record of publishing claims about Russian military affairs that have later been confirmed. The specificity of the claim — the KN-23 missiles, the artillery batch, the airspace ban, the figure of 50,000 troops — is the kind of detail that is harder to invent than to report, and the fact that multiple reputable news organisations have carried the story suggests that it is being taken seriously in Kyiv and beyond. But specificity is not proof, and the history of this war is full of claims that were specific, widely reported and wrong. The responsible course is to report the claim, attribute it clearly, note the absence of independent verification, and watch for what happens next. If KN-23 missiles stop appearing in Russian strikes, if North Korean artillery shells stop turning up on the battlefield, if Russian transport aircraft stop flying through North Korean airspace, the claim will gain credibility. If the missiles keep coming, it will lose it. The evidence, in the end, will be on the battlefield.

The wider war: a week of fast-moving diplomacy

The claim arrives at a moment when the diplomacy around the war is moving unusually fast. The Trump administration has ended its strategy of using energy and aviation deals to persuade Moscow to end the war, and has moved instead to a traditional land-for-peace framework, according to reports published on November 27. President Trump has invited Vladimir Putin to the G20 summit in Florida on December 14 and 15, saying "if it were up to me, I'd like to have him" there, and has urged Putin to pull back aggression against Europe. The president's special envoy, Jared Kushner, has said that the largest prisoner swap of the war — 1,200 to 1,300 prisoners from each side, possibly up to 1,500 — could take place before Christmas, following talks in Abu Dhabi on November 10 and 11. And Trump has updated NATO's secretary general, Mark Rutte, on his talks with Putin, telling reporters that a Ukraine peace deal could come quickly. Against that background, a crack in the Moscow-Pyongyang partnership would be a significant piece of news for the negotiators, because it would suggest that Russia's position is weaker than it appears and that its allies are less reliable than it claims.

It also arrives in the same week as a series of other developments that point in the same direction. Ukraine struck the Savasleyka air base in Russia, damaging five aircraft including three MiG-31 interceptors, in a long-range attack that demonstrated Kyiv's growing ability to hit targets deep inside Russia. The United States has imposed sanctions on Iranian shadow banking networks and on Chinese firms accused of helping Russia evade export controls. And the American Congress is moving towards a ban on Chinese cars, part of a broader hardening of Washington's stance towards Beijing. The picture, taken together, is of a Western alliance that is tightening the screws on Russia's partners and suppliers, and of a Russian war effort that is increasingly dependent on a small number of allies whose loyalty cannot be taken for granted. North Korea is the most important of those allies, and a suspension of its support — even a temporary one — would be a blow to Moscow's confidence at a delicate moment.

There is also a South Korean dimension that should not be overlooked. The GUR's account has the rupture triggered by a Russian offer to help ease tensions between Seoul and Pyongyang, and that offer, if it was made, is itself significant. South Korea has a new government, and the politics of the Korean peninsula are in flux. If Moscow is genuinely interested in a de-escalation between the two Koreas, it would be a departure from decades of Russian policy, which has generally treated the peninsula as a useful source of tension rather than a problem to be solved. If, on the other hand, Rudenko's remarks were a casual aside, blown out of proportion by Pyongyang or by Kyiv, the whole story may be less than it appears. Either way, the episode is a reminder that the war in Ukraine is not a bilateral conflict but a knot of relationships — between Moscow and Pyongyang, between Pyongyang and Seoul, between Washington and all three — and that a tug on one thread can move the whole knot.

What to watch in the coming days

The test of this story will come quickly. If North Korea has genuinely suspended its military support, the effects should be visible within days and weeks. The most immediate indicator will be the pattern of Russian missile strikes on Ukraine: if KN-23 missiles stop appearing in the wreckage, or appear less frequently, the suspension is likely to be real. The second indicator will be the flow of artillery ammunition: if Russian artillery fire slackens, or if Russian units begin to report shortages, the halt in shell deliveries will be showing its effects. The third will be the movement of Russian military transport aircraft: if they stop using North Korean airspace, flight-tracking data and satellite imagery may show the change. And the fourth will be the diplomatic record: if Moscow and Pyongyang issue statements clarifying their positions on South Korea, or if Choe Son Hui makes a public appearance to confirm or deny the suspension, the picture will become clearer.

There is also the question of what happens to the 8,500 North Korean troops already in Russia. The GUR's account does not say that they are being withdrawn, only that the request to increase their number to 50,000 is being held back. The troops who are already there will presumably stay, and will presumably keep fighting, unless and until Pyongyang decides otherwise. Their fate is a reminder that the suspension, even if it is real, is a pause rather than a reversal. North Korea has not joined the Western alliance. It has not condemned the invasion. It has not withdrawn its soldiers. It has, according to one intelligence agency, stopped sending more missiles and shells for the time being, and it has done so because it is angry about a diplomatic remark. That is a long way from a change of sides, and it would be a mistake to read it as one.

The broader lesson is about the fragility of alliances built on transactions. Russia and North Korea came together because each had something the other needed: Moscow needed shells and soldiers, Pyongyang needed food, fuel and technology. That kind of partnership can be very effective, and it has been, for both sides. But it is also vulnerable to the kind of misunderstanding that the GUR describes — a remark by a deputy foreign minister, interpreted in Pyongyang as a betrayal, answered with a suspension of the very supplies that make the partnership valuable. Great powers have learned, over centuries, that alliances survive only if both sides understand each other's red lines. The claim from Kyiv suggests that Moscow and Pyongyang may have discovered, too late, that they do not understand each other's red lines at all. Whether the discovery leads to a repair or to a rupture is the question that will shape the next phase of the war.
