The Texas State Capitol in Austin. A bill filed for the 90th Texas Legislature would make it a third-degree felony to conduct a marriage ceremony for any marriage Texas law calls void — and Texas law still lists same-sex marriage as void. Photo: Farragutful, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

A bill filed in the Texas Senate would make it a felony — punishable by two to ten years in prison and a fine of up to $10,000 — to officiate a wedding ceremony for a marriage that Texas law considers void. The bill does not mention same-sex marriage by name. It does not have to. Texas law still contains a provision declaring marriages between people of the same sex to be void — a provision that has been unenforceable since the United States Supreme Court legalised same-sex marriage nationwide in 2015, but which the legislature has never removed from the books. Senate Bill 178, filed on November 9, 2026, by Republican State Senator Brent Hagenbuch, would attach a third-degree felony penalty to ceremonies for any marriage in that "void" category.

The bill has ignited a national row. Critics, including constitutional law scholars, call it "patently unconstitutional" and warn that it would criminalise the clergy, judges and civil officiants who perform same-sex weddings. Supporters of the bill's author say it was never meant to target same-sex couples at all — and Hagenbuch himself has told the Houston Chronicle that any language appearing to have that effect would be removed as the legislation moves through committee. But the bill, as filed, does what it does: it takes a dead law that calls same-sex marriage void, and it builds a prison sentence on top of it.

The timing matters. The bill was pre-filed ahead of the 90th Texas Legislature, which convenes on January 12, 2027. It arrives weeks after decisive Democratic victories in the midterm elections, in a session where 1,694 bills and joint resolutions have already been filed. And it arrives in a state where the battle over marriage equality was supposedly settled eleven years ago — but where the statute book still says otherwise.

The Bill: Senate Bill 178 in Plain English

Senate Bill 178 is short — its text as introduced runs to just 280 words — but its effect is sweeping. The bill amends Section 2.202 of the Texas Family Code, which governs who may conduct a marriage ceremony and what happens when someone conducts one without authorisation. Under current law, a person commits an offence if they knowingly conduct a marriage ceremony without authorisation, punishable as a Class A misdemeanour. A person also commits an offence — already a third-degree felony — if they knowingly conduct a ceremony for a marriage involving a minor barred by law, or for a bigamous marriage.

SB 178 adds a third category to that felony list: a ceremony "for a marriage that is void under Subchapter C, Chapter 6" of the Family Code. Subchapter C is where Texas law defines void marriages — and Section 6.204 of that subchapter states that a marriage between persons of the same sex, or a civil union between persons of the same sex, is void in Texas. That section has been unenforceable since Obergefell v. Hodges, the Supreme Court's 2015 decision, but it has never been repealed. By linking the felony penalty to the whole of Subchapter C, SB 178 would extend the third-degree felony — two to ten years in prison and a fine of up to $10,000 — to anyone who knowingly conducts a ceremony for a same-sex couple.

The bill's official caption is dry and bureaucratic: "Relating to the criminal offense of conducting a marriage ceremony for a void marriage." The introduced version was filed on November 9, 2026, and received by the Secretary of the Senate the same day. If passed, it would take effect on September 1, 2027. Between now and then, it must pass through committee, survive floor votes in both chambers, and be signed by the governor — a long road on which, its author insists, the offending language will be fixed.

How a Dead Law Came Back to Life

To understand SB 178, you have to understand the strange afterlife of laws the Supreme Court has struck down. When the Supreme Court ruled in Obergefell v. Hodges in 2015 — by a 5-4 majority — that the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license marriages between two people of the same sex and to recognise same-sex marriages lawfully performed in other states, it did not erase the contrary laws from the statute books. It made them unenforceable. The words stayed on the page; they simply lost their power. Texas's Section 6.204 is one of those zombie provisions: still printed in the Family Code, still declaring same-sex marriages void, still legally dead.

Zombie laws are common in American statute books. Texas also still has a section of its Penal Code criminalising "homosexual conduct" — a provision the Supreme Court struck down in Lawrence v. Texas in 2003, more than two decades ago. It, too, remains on the books. In the current session, two Democratic legislators have filed bills to overturn the ban on homosexual conduct, and a separate bill, House Bill 653, would repeal the Penal Code section entirely. The existence of these dead provisions is usually harmless: prosecutors cannot enforce them, courts will not uphold them, and they sit in the code as historical artefacts. SB 178 is dangerous precisely because it does not leave the dead law alone — it wires a live penalty to it.

That is the point the legal experts are making. Emily Berman, a constitutional law professor at the University of Houston Law Center, called the bill "patently unconstitutional" in a statement to the Houston Chronicle, noting that it would create a different set of laws for same-sex couples, which the Supreme Court made illegal in Obergefell. Roger Diefendorf, an attorney and former administrative law judge in California, told Newsweek that criminalising ceremonies on the basis that the couple are of the same sex would likely conflict with the Fourteenth Amendment and the Supremacy Clause — the constitutional principle that federal law, and the Constitution itself, override conflicting state law. Diefendorf cautioned against describing SB 178 simply as a proposed ban on performing same-sex marriages, saying its wording could raise significant constitutional questions if enacted in its current form. But the direction of those questions is clear: a state cannot do indirectly, through its criminal code, what the Constitution forbids it to do directly.

The Author's Defence: "Not Meant to Target Same-Sex Weddings"

Hagenbuch has pushed back against the interpretation that has made his bill national news. He told the Houston Chronicle that the legislation was never intended to target same-sex weddings and that he expected any language appearing to have that effect to be removed during the committee process. The bill's defenders point out that its text does not mention same-sex marriage at all, and that its purpose — as they describe it — is to strengthen penalties for ceremonies involving minors and bigamous marriages, categories already covered by the felony provision.

That defence raises an obvious question: if the bill was not meant to touch same-sex marriage, why does it extend the felony to every marriage declared void under Subchapter C, rather than to the specific categories — minors and bigamy — that its supporters say it is about? The answer, according to the bill's critics, is that the drafting does exactly what the text says it does, whatever the author's intentions. Laws are enforced as written, not as intended, and a prosecutor or a court reading SB 178 as filed would find a felony offence that reaches same-sex ceremonies. The promise to fix it in committee is a promise about the future; the bill as filed is a fact about the present.

The episode also illustrates a recurring feature of the Texas legislative process: the pre-filing period. Bills for the 90th Legislature could be pre-filed from November 9, 2026, and by November 26, a total of 1,694 bills and joint resolutions had been filed — 1,179 House bills, 445 Senate bills, 59 House joint resolutions and 11 Senate joint resolutions, with resolutions counted bringing the total to 1,737. Pre-filed bills are sent to committees only after the session opens on January 12, 2027, which means SB 178 will sit, as filed, for two months before any committee can amend it. In that time, it will be read, analysed, debated and campaigned on — exactly as it is being read now.

Who Would Be at Risk: Clergy, Judges and Officiants

If SB 178 became law in its current form, the people at risk would not be the couples getting married. The bill targets the person who conducts the ceremony — the officiant. That is a broad category in Texas: it includes clergy of every faith, rabbis, judges, justices of the peace, and any other person authorised to conduct a marriage ceremony under Texas law. A minister who marries a same-sex couple in her church, a judge who performs a civil ceremony for two men at the courthouse, a rabbi who blesses a lesbian couple's union — each would, on the bill's plain text, be committing a third-degree felony, facing two to ten years in prison and a fine of up to $10,000.

The breadth of that exposure is what has alarmed religious leaders and civil liberties advocates alike. The right of clergy to perform marriages according to their faith is a matter of religious freedom, and the idea that a minister could be imprisoned for conducting a wedding her church recognises strikes at the heart of the First Amendment as well as the Fourteenth. For judges and justices of the peace, the conflict is even sharper: they are officers of the state, required by the Constitution to treat same-sex and different-sex couples equally, and SB 178 would put them in the position of choosing between their constitutional duty and a felony charge.

The practical effect, even before any prosecution, would be a chilling one. Wedding officiants who are unsure of the law tend to err on the side of caution — and caution, in this case, would mean refusing to marry same-sex couples. That is the mechanism by which a bill that never mentions same-sex marriage could nevertheless make same-sex weddings harder to obtain in Texas: not by banning the marriages, but by threatening the people who perform them.

The National Backdrop: A Country Still Arguing About Marriage

SB 178 did not emerge from nowhere. It comes at a moment when marriage equality, settled law for more than a decade, is being re-litigated in state legislatures across the United States. The Supreme Court's 2015 decision in Obergefell settled the constitutional question, but it did not settle the political one. In state after state, legislators have filed bills testing the edges of the ruling — on religious exemptions, on the recognition of out-of-state marriages, on the duties of county clerks and officiants. Most of these bills go nowhere. A few become law and are struck down. All of them signal to voters where their legislators stand.

The Texas bill also arrives in a changed political landscape. The midterm elections of November 3, 2026, delivered decisive Democratic victories, including a Democratic majority in the United States House of Representatives — 258 seats, the subject of an earlier World News Desk report — and the political map of 2027 will look different from the map of 2025. In Texas itself, the response to SB 178 has been swift: alongside the bills to repeal the homosexual conduct ban, legislators have filed measures moving in the opposite direction, and advocacy groups are tracking 13 bills in the 2027 session that would affect LGBTQ Texans — two described as anti-LGBTQ and eleven as pro-LGBTQ.

That context matters because SB 178 must survive a legislature, not just a headline. The Texas Senate is Republican-controlled, and bills filed by Republican senators routinely advance. But a bill that constitutional scholars call "patently unconstitutional" carries political risk as well as legal risk: it invites litigation, national attention and a court defeat that would embarrass its supporters. The author's promise to amend the bill in committee may reflect that calculation as much as any change of heart.

What the Constitution Says — and What Happens in Court

The constitutional analysis of SB 178 is, according to the experts quoted in coverage, straightforward. The Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause requires states to treat same-sex and different-sex couples equally in marriage. Obergefell held that the right to marry is a fundamental right, and that states may not deny it to same-sex couples. A law that criminalises the performance of same-sex wedding ceremonies, while permitting identical ceremonies for different-sex couples, draws exactly the distinction Obergefell forbids.

The Supremacy Clause adds a second barrier: the Constitution is the supreme law of the land, and a state statute that conflicts with it is void. Section 6.204 of the Texas Family Code is already void in this sense — unenforceable, a dead letter. SB 178 would attempt to give that dead letter new life by attaching a criminal penalty to it, but the penalty cannot revive what the Constitution has killed. As Professor Berman put it, the bill would create a different set of laws for same-sex couples, which the Supreme Court made illegal.

If SB 178 were enacted, the likely sequence is familiar from other states' experience: an officiant or a couple would file suit, a federal court would issue a preliminary injunction blocking enforcement, and the law would be struck down — probably quickly, and probably at considerable cost to the state in legal fees. Texas has been through this cycle before, notably in its long-running litigation over abortion and immigration laws. The question is whether the legislature wants to spend its 2027 session, and the state's money, on a law its own author's allies concede needs to be fixed before it can pass.

The Federal Backstop: The Respect for Marriage Act

There is one more layer of protection that did not exist when Obergefell was decided: federal statute. In December 2022, Congress passed the Respect for Marriage Act, which requires the federal government to recognise any marriage that was valid in the state where it was performed, and requires every state to recognise valid same-sex marriages performed in other states. President Joe Biden signed it into law. The Act was Congress's answer to the fear that the Supreme Court might one day revisit Obergefell: even if the constitutional ruling were overturned, the statute would still require interstate recognition of same-sex marriages.

The Act does not, however, require a state to issue marriage licences to same-sex couples within its own borders — that obligation still rests on Obergefell. And it does not speak directly to the question SB 178 raises: the criminal liability of officiants. A state law that threatened clergy and judges for performing same-sex ceremonies would have to survive the Fourteenth Amendment and the First Amendment's protection of religious exercise, not merely the Respect for Marriage Act. But the Act's existence shows how seriously Congress took the risk of backsliding — and how determined the federal government is that a marriage valid in one state should be valid in all of them. For a same-sex couple married in New York who move to Texas, the Act guarantees recognition. For a couple seeking to marry in Texas itself, the guarantee still depends on the Constitution — and on bills like SB 178 never becoming law.

What Happens Next

The bill's immediate future is procedural. SB 178 was filed on November 9 and received by the Secretary of the Senate the same day. It will be referred to a committee after the 90th Legislature convenes on January 12, 2027. In committee, the author has said the language raising concerns about same-sex weddings will be removed — a commitment that supporters of marriage equality will be watching closely, and that opponents of the bill will test by reading the amended text, not by taking the promise on trust.

If the bill is amended to cover only ceremonies for minors and bigamous marriages — the categories its supporters say it is about — the controversy largely disappears, and SB 178 becomes an unremarkable toughening of an existing penalty. If it is not amended, it will become one of the most closely watched bills of the Texas session, drawing national attention, legal challenges and a debate that will reach far beyond Austin. Either way, the episode has already served a purpose for its critics: it has put the zombie provision in Section 6.204 back in the spotlight, and it has reminded Texans that the words in the statute book still matter — because a legislature can, at any moment, decide to enforce them.

For the couples planning weddings in Texas in 2027, the practical advice is simple: nothing has changed yet. Same-sex marriage remains legal in Texas and in every state in the union, and no officiant has been charged under a law that has not been passed. But the filing of SB 178 is a reminder that rights established by courts can be challenged by legislatures, and that the defence of those rights depends on watching what gets filed in November — not just what gets voted on in May.

Why This Story Matters

Senate Bill 178 is 280 words long. It mentions no couple, no church, no wedding. It is, on its face, a technical amendment to a section of the Family Code that most Texans have never read. And yet it has become a national story, because it illustrates a truth about how rights are lost: not always in a single dramatic vote, but in a quiet cross-reference — a bill that points to a section of law that points to a category of people, and attaches a prison sentence to the connection.

The story also illustrates how rights are defended: by reading the bill, by tracing the cross-reference, by asking the constitutional question, and by refusing to accept that a law's intentions matter more than its text. Hagenbuch may well amend SB 178 in committee, and the controversy may fade. But the provision it pointed to — Section 6.204, declaring same-sex marriage void — will still be sitting in the Texas Family Code, waiting for the next bill to point at it. Until the legislature repeals the dead laws it no longer means, the risk they will be revived remains. That is the real lesson of Senate Bill 178: in a statute book, nothing is ever truly dead until it is deleted.
