TITLE: Putin Just Unveiled Russia's First Hypersonic-Missile Submarine — and Promised 5 More by 2030 LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Putin has unveiled the nuclear submarine Perm, the first boat armed with Zircon hypersonic missiles, and promised five more by 2030. ---BODY---

Vladimir Putin in February 2026. The Russian president unveiled the nuclear-powered submarine Perm at the Sevmash shipyard on 27 November 2026. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Vladimir Putin has unveiled a new weapon that Russia says changes the balance of power under the sea. Standing aboard a submarine at the Sevmash shipyard in the far north of Russia on Friday, 27 November 2026, the Russian president watched the naval flag rise over the Perm — a nuclear-powered attack submarine that Moscow says is the first in the world to carry Zircon hypersonic cruise missiles as its standard armament. And he used the moment to make a promise: five more nuclear submarines will join the Russian navy by 2030.

The ceremony, held at Severodvinsk on the White Sea coast, was short, formal and heavy with symbolism. Putin attended the flag-raising aboard the Perm, marking the boat's formal transfer to the Russian navy, according to video released by the Kremlin. The submarine will shortly join the Pacific Fleet, the Kremlin said, putting a hypersonic-armed hunter-killer thousands of miles from the shipyard where it was built — and within range, in theory, of the sea lanes of the Pacific.

"Russia is one of the few countries in the world capable of building powerful nuclear submarines of this kind, weapons with global reach and significance," Putin said at the ceremony. It was a sentence designed to be heard far beyond Severodvinsk — in Washington, in London, in Beijing, and in Kyiv, where the war Russia started in February 2022 is now approaching its fifth year.

The Perm is the fifth Yasen-M-class submarine to join the Russian navy in the past six years, according to Putin. It is the first of the class to carry the Zircon missile, a weapon Moscow says can fly at hypersonic speed — many times the speed of sound — and strike targets at sea and on land before existing defences can react. Whether the missile lives up to those claims is a question Western analysts have debated for years. What is not in doubt is that Russia has now put it on a submarine, and that the submarine is now in service.

What happened at Severodvinsk

The Sevmash shipyard, in the north-western city of Severodvinsk in the Arkhangelsk region, is the only yard in Russia that builds nuclear-powered submarines. It is a closed, secretive place on the shore of the White Sea, and it has built every Russian nuclear submarine for decades. On Friday it was the stage for a ceremony the Kremlin wanted the world to see.

Putin attended the raising of the St Andrew's flag — the white ensign of the Russian navy — aboard the Perm, the Project 885M Yasen-M boat that had completed its sea trials and state trials. The flag-raising is the formal act by which a warship enters service: the moment a hull under construction becomes a commissioned ship of the navy, with a crew, a commander and a place in the order of battle. The crew of the Perm took part in the ceremony as Putin spoke, and the Kremlin released video of the event the same day.

The Perm is named after the city of Perm, a major industrial centre in the Ural mountains. It was floated out of the construction hall in 2025 and has spent the months since on trials in the White Sea and the Barents Sea. Those trials, the Kremlin said, are now complete, and the boat is ready for service.

Putin said the navy would receive five more nuclear submarines by 2030, including three vessels from the Yasen-M class, the same class as the Perm. The boats, he said, can operate in "any part of the world's oceans" while carrying a range of cruise missiles. He also said Russia intends to continue modernising its navy, and expressed confidence that all plans to build a powerful naval force will be implemented in full and on time.

The timing of the announcement was not accidental. Russia's parliament has just approved a record defence budget — $609 billion for 2027 to 2029, with spending on defence taking 35.2 per cent of all government expenditure, the largest share in the country's modern history. The 2027 figure alone, $208.8 billion, is a third higher than the year before. The Perm is the sharp end of that money: a single boat that costs billions, built to carry weapons that cost millions each, in a navy that is being rebuilt while a war is being fought on land.

The Perm and the Yasen-M class

The Perm is a Project 885M Yasen-M-class submarine, the most modern attack submarine Russia has ever built. The Yasen class — known to NATO as the Severodvinsk class — is Russia's answer to the American Virginia class and the British Astute class: a multi-purpose nuclear-powered boat designed to hunt other submarines, sink surface ships, and strike targets on land with cruise missiles, all while staying hidden for months at a time.

The Yasen-M is the improved version of the original Yasen design. The first boat of the class, the Severodvinsk, entered service in 2014 after nearly two decades of construction, a victim of the economic collapse that followed the end of the Soviet Union. The M-model boats — the Kazan, the Novosibirsk, the Krasnoyarsk, the Arkhangelsk and now the Perm — have been built faster, with improved sensors, quieter propulsion and a larger missile load. The Perm is the fifth of these improved boats to enter service in six years, a pace of production that Russia's shipbuilding industry has not sustained since the Soviet era.

What makes the Perm different from its sister boats is its armament. According to the Kremlin, it is the first submarine to carry the Zircon hypersonic cruise missile as standard armament. Earlier Yasen-M boats carry the Kalibr cruise missile and the Oniks anti-ship missile, both well-established weapons. The Zircon is something else: a missile that Moscow says flies at around nine times the speed of sound, at altitudes and speeds that make it extremely difficult for existing air defences and missile defences to intercept.

Western analysts have long been sceptical about some of Moscow's claims for the Zircon, and about how many of the missiles Russia can actually produce. The missile has been tested from surface ships — a frigate in the Barents Sea, and reportedly from a submarine in a submerged launch — but the Perm is the first boat designed from the start to carry it as a routine weapon. If the Zircon works as Moscow claims, a submarine armed with it could strike a carrier group or a coastal target with very little warning. If it does not, the Perm is still a formidable boat, armed with the same Kalibr and Oniks missiles as its sisters.

The Perm will join the Pacific Fleet, according to the Kremlin. That matters. The Pacific Fleet, based at Vladivostok and at Petropavlovsk-Kamchatsky on the Kamchatka peninsula, is Russia's naval presence in the Pacific — the ocean where the United States, China, Japan, South Korea and Australia are all building up their own submarine forces. A Yasen-M boat in the Pacific, armed with hypersonic missiles, is a statement of intent in the region where the great-power competition of the next decades is expected to play out.

The Zircon: what we know, and what we don't

The 3M22 Zircon — Tsirkon in Russian — is a hypersonic cruise missile developed by the NPO Mashinostroyeniya design bureau. Russia first announced it publicly in 2019, when Putin said in his annual address to parliament that the missile could fly at nine times the speed of sound and strike targets more than 1,000 kilometres away. Since then it has been tested from the frigate Admiral Gorshkov and, according to Russian reports, from a submarine, and it was declared to have entered service with the navy in 2023.

Hypersonic, in military terms, means a speed above Mach 5 — five times the speed of sound, or roughly 6,000 kilometres per hour. At those speeds a missile crosses hundreds of kilometres in minutes, and the time available for a defender to detect it, track it and fire an interceptor shrinks to almost nothing. That is the promise of the Zircon, and the reason Moscow has talked about it so insistently: a weapon that, if it works, makes the missile defences of an aircraft carrier group — the most powerful surface force in the world — look suddenly inadequate.

But the gap between a weapon's claimed performance and its real performance is where wars are won and lost, and the Zircon's real performance is not publicly known. Western intelligence agencies have studied the missile's test launches and have been cautious in their public assessments. Some analysts note that the Zircon's range and speed claims have varied over the years, and that the number of test launches has been small. Others point out that Russia has reportedly used Zircon missiles in the war in Ukraine — against targets in Kyiv in early 2024 — and that Ukrainian air defences have claimed to intercept at least some of them.

None of that diminishes the significance of the Perm. A submarine is a far more survivable launch platform than a surface ship: it can approach a target unseen, fire, and disappear. Putting a hypersonic missile on a submarine multiplies the threat, because the defender does not know where the launch will come from. That is the logic of the Perm, and it is why the boat's commissioning was treated by the Kremlin as a major event rather than a routine handover.

It is also worth noting what the Perm does not change. A single submarine, however capable, does not alter the balance of naval power on its own. The United States Navy operates around fifty nuclear-powered attack submarines, and Britain and France operate their own fleets. Russia's submarine force, for all the money now being poured into it, remains smaller and older than the force the Soviet Union maintained. The Perm is a step towards closing that gap, not the closing of it.

Five more by 2030 — and the money behind them

Putin's promise of five more nuclear submarines by 2030, including three more Yasen-M boats, is the most concrete part of the announcement. It is also the part that will be hardest to deliver. Building a nuclear submarine is one of the most complex industrial undertakings in the world: a single boat takes years to build, requires thousands of specialist workers, and depends on a supply chain of hundreds of firms. Russia's shipbuilding industry has struggled with delays for decades — the first Yasen boat took 21 years from keel-laying to commissioning — and the war in Ukraine has put new strains on it, from sanctions on specialist components to the diversion of skilled labour.

The money, at least on paper, is there. Russia's parliament has approved a defence budget of $609 billion for the years 2027 to 2029, with military spending consuming more than a third of all government expenditure. That is a wartime budget, and it is being paid for by cutting civilian spending — health, education, infrastructure — and by drawing on the reserves Russia built up before the war. The question is whether the money can be turned into submarines, given the constraints of the shipyards, the sanctions and the war.

The Yasen-M programme itself shows both sides of the story. Five boats in six years is, by Russian standards, a remarkable pace — the fastest submarine production since the Soviet Union. But it is still a long way from the production rates of the 1970s and 1980s, when Soviet yards launched multiple nuclear boats a year. And the three further Yasen-M boats Putin promised by 2030 are already under construction at Sevmash; the promise is less a new commitment than a statement that the existing programme will be completed on time.

There is also the question of what the submarines are for. Russia's navy has two jobs: to protect the ballistic-missile submarines that carry the country's nuclear deterrent, and to project power far from Russia's shores. The Yasen-M boats are designed for both. They can escort the missile submarines of the Northern and Pacific Fleets, and they can operate independently, far from home, carrying cruise missiles that can strike land targets hundreds of kilometres inland. The Perm, joining the Pacific Fleet, will do both.

Why a submarine matters more than a missile

Nuclear-powered submarines are the most survivable part of any country's nuclear deterrent, and that is the real reason the Perm matters. A land-based missile can be found and destroyed. A bomber can be shot down. A submarine, running silent and deep, is almost impossible to find — and as long as it survives, it can retaliate. That is why every nuclear-armed country that can afford it builds submarines, and why the United States, Britain, France, China and Russia all maintain them.

Putin said it himself at the ceremony: "Russia is one of the few countries in the world capable of building powerful nuclear submarines of this kind, weapons with global reach and significance." The phrase "global reach and significance" is doing a lot of work. It is a reminder that Russia, whatever its difficulties on land in Ukraine, remains a nuclear superpower with the means to strike anywhere on earth — and that its submarine force is the guarantee of that capability.

The Perm's commissioning also comes at a moment of unusual tension in the world's oceans. The United States has been blockading Iranian ports since April, and on 27 November its aircraft disabled a merchant ship, the Pacific Archer, in the Gulf of Oman after it tried to run the blockade — the fifteenth vessel disabled since the blockade began. Ukraine has been attacking Russian ships in the Black Sea and, according to Kyiv, as far away as Libya. The world's navies are busier, and more confrontational, than they have been in decades.

In that context, the Perm is both a military asset and a political message. To the United States and its allies, it says that Russia is still building the weapons of a great power, despite sanctions and despite a war that has consumed enormous resources. To the Russian public, it says that the state is still capable of great industrial feats. And to the rest of the world, it says that the competition under the sea — the quietest and least visible of all military competitions — is intensifying.

The race under the sea

Russia is not the only country building submarines at a furious pace. China is building nuclear-powered submarines faster than any country in history, and its fleet is expected to grow substantially over the next decade. The United States is building the Virginia-class attack submarines and the Columbia-class ballistic-missile submarines, the largest and most expensive submarines it has ever built. Britain is building the Dreadnought class to replace its Vanguard boats, and Australia, under the AUKUS agreement with the United States and Britain, will acquire nuclear-powered submarines for the first time.

The Perm enters service in the middle of this race. It is a capable boat, and the Zircon gives it a weapon that no Western submarine currently carries. But it is also a single boat, in a navy that has lost ships in the Black Sea, that is spending a third of its national budget on defence, and that is fighting a war that shows no sign of ending. The question for the next five years is not whether Russia can build five more submarines — it probably can, if the money keeps flowing — but whether it can do so while also fighting a war, maintaining its existing fleet, and keeping its economy from breaking under the strain.

The Perm's first deployment will be watched closely. When it leaves Severodvinsk for the long voyage around the north of Russia to the Pacific — through the Arctic, past Norway, and down the coast of Siberia — Western navies will track it, as they track every Russian submarine. Its arrival in the Pacific, expected in the coming months, will put a hypersonic-armed boat into the waters where the United States and China are already manoeuvring. For the crew of the Perm, it will be the start of a new life: months at sea, in silence, under the ice and under the waves. For the rest of us, it is a reminder that the most consequential military competition of our time is happening where almost no one can see it.

Sevmash: the yard that built the Soviet submarine fleet

The shipyard where the Perm was built is itself a piece of history. Sevmash — the Northern Machine-Building Enterprise — was founded in 1936 on the shore of the White Sea, and it built the Soviet Union's first nuclear-powered submarine, the Leninsky Komsomol, which entered service in 1958. Over the following three decades it built more nuclear submarines than any other yard in the world: ballistic-missile boats, attack boats, and the giant Typhoon class, the largest submarines ever constructed, each one longer than two football fields and heavier than a battleship.

After the Soviet Union collapsed, Sevmash nearly died. Orders dried up, workers went unpaid, and the yard survived on a handful of export orders and the slow, painful construction of the first Yasen boat, which took 21 years to complete. Its revival is one of the quiet stories of Putin's Russia: a deliberate, state-funded rebuilding of the country's submarine industry, paid for with oil money in the 2000s and with war money in the 2020s. The Perm is the product of that revival — a boat built by a yard that was, thirty years ago, on the verge of closing.

Sevmash now employs tens of thousands of workers in a city, Severodvinsk, that exists largely because of the yard. The city is closed to foreigners without special permission, and the yard itself is one of the most secretive industrial sites in Russia. The ceremony on Friday was one of the rare moments when its doors opened, even briefly, to the cameras.

What happens next

The Perm is now a commissioned ship of the Russian navy, and its trials are complete. In the coming weeks it is expected to make the long transit to its home port in the Pacific Fleet, where it will begin operational patrols. Three more Yasen-M boats are under construction at Sevmash, and Putin has promised that five more nuclear submarines in total will join the fleet by 2030.

Whether that promise is kept will depend on things far from Severodvinsk: on the course of the war in Ukraine, on the state of the Russian economy, on the sanctions that restrict Russia's access to technology, and on the political will to keep spending a third of the national budget on the military. The Perm is proof that Russia can still build a world-class submarine. The next five years will show whether it can build five more — and what it will have to give up to do it.

For now, the flag has been raised, the speeches have been made, and the Perm is at sea. The quietest weapon in Russia's arsenal has just got louder.
