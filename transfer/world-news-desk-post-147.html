TITLE: Small Business Saturday 2026: 88% of Americans Will Shop Small Today — and $18 Billion Is Riding on Main Street LABELS: Business SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Small Business Saturday is today, 28 November 2026. 88% of Americans plan to shop small — but last year's $18 billion was 19% down on 2024. ---BODY---

SBA Administrator Kelly Loeffler. The Small Business Administration has co-sponsored Small Business Saturday since 2011. Photo: U.S. Small Business Administration, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

Today is the biggest day of the year for America's smallest shops. Small Business Saturday falls on Saturday, 28 November 2026 — the day after Black Friday, two days before Cyber Monday — and the numbers behind it tell a story of a country that loves its local shops, even as its wallets get thinner. According to the 2026 American Express Shop Small Impact Study, 88% of American consumers say they are likely to shop at small businesses this holiday season, the highest reading since 2012. The intention is there. The question, as every year, is whether the money follows.

Last year it did not follow as far as it used to. American Express estimated that consumers spent about $18 billion at small businesses on Small Business Saturday in 2025 — roughly 19% below the $22 billion reported for 2024, according to WBEZ. One Chicago gift-shop owner told the station that October had been the top-selling month at one of her locations, and November had been slow. The demand is real, the surveys say. The spending is selective, the surveys also say. Today is the test of which one wins.

The National Retail Federation expects 182.7 million people to shop over the Thanksgiving weekend, slightly below last year's 186.9 million, according to its survey with Prosper Insights, published on 19 November. In the same survey, 82% of holiday shoppers said they are taking steps to manage costs: 25% are buying less expensive gifts, 24% are cutting back elsewhere, and 20% are buying for fewer people. The NRF's holiday forecast calls for sales of $1.013 trillion, up 4.2% on last year. The money is there. It is being spent carefully, and small businesses have one day to prove they deserve a share of it.

What Small Business Saturday is

Small Business Saturday was founded by American Express in 2010, and the U.S. Small Business Administration has co-sponsored it since 2011, according to the U.S. Census Bureau. The idea was simple: Black Friday belonged to the big-box stores and the online giants, and Cyber Monday belonged to the e-commerce platforms, so the Saturday in between should belong to the independent shops, the neighbourhood restaurants, and the local entrepreneurs. Sixteen years later, it is a fixture of the American holiday calendar, marked by the SBA on its official observances page, promoted by downtown business associations in every state, and tracked as a spending event in its own right.

American Express estimates that consumers have spent approximately $241 billion at small businesses during Small Business Saturday events since spending began being tracked in 2012. That is a large number, and it is also a reminder of how much of the American economy runs through small firms. According to the SBA Office of Advocacy's 2026 report, there are 36,207,130 small businesses in the United States — 99.9% of all businesses in the country. They employ 62.3 million people, or 45.9% of private-sector workers. They account for 43.5% of GDP and pay 38.7% of private-sector payroll, about $3.5 trillion. Since 1995, they have created 20.7 million net new jobs — 61% of all net new jobs in that period. A small business, in the government's definition, is an independent business with fewer than 500 employees. Most are far smaller than that: 29,811,495 of them — 82.3% — have no employees besides the owner.

SBA Administrator Kelly Loeffler, in a statement on 19 November, put the case for the day in the language the agency has used for years. "Participating in Small Business Saturday means part of every dollar spent at a local business stays local and has a multiplier effect in your community," she said. The multiplier is the argument that has carried the day for sixteen years: money spent at a local shop is more likely to be spent again locally — at the supplier down the road, at the café across the street, on the wages of a neighbour — than money spent at a national chain or an online marketplace.

The shoppers are coming — but they are counting every dollar

The surveys agree on the demand. The American Express Shop Small Impact Study, fielded in September and released on 12 November, found 88% of consumers likely to shop at small businesses this holiday season, the highest reading since 2012. The third annual AT&T Business Holiday Shopping Survey found that more than half of Americans plan to shop on Small Business Saturday itself in 2026: 52% in total, with 34% planning to shop in person and 24% online. One in five holiday shoppers plan to buy from small or local businesses, according to an NRF survey summary — and among them, 71% cite supporting community businesses, 56% cite unique gifts, 37% cite handmade work, and 33% cite quality.

But the same surveys agree on the caution. KPMG's Consumer Pulse Holiday 2026 survey found that average holiday shopping spend will increase 5% to $783, with 45% of consumers expecting to spend more and 26% expecting to spend less. The NRF's figures show the same split: shoppers are spending, but selectively, buying less expensive gifts, cutting back elsewhere, and buying for fewer people. The Conference Board's index of consumer confidence rose 11.4 points in November to 103.3, its highest level since January 2025 — a number this newspaper reported earlier this week — but the Conference Board also noted that consumers' written comments still express frustration with high prices. Nikki Baird, vice president of strategy and product at Aptos, told Retail TouchPoints that early forecasts for the season were bullish, but consumer confidence has slipped since then. The picture is not a collapse. It is a squeeze, and the squeeze is what today is about.

That is why the advice from the trade press this week has been consistent: treat the day as customer acquisition, not as a discounting contest. A sales playbook published on 26 November put it plainly — shoppers intend to support small businesses, but they are spending selectively, so the weekend rewards sellers who protect margin and collect customers for January. Promote early, build offers around bundles and bonuses instead of blanket markdowns, capture contact details at checkout, and measure repeat purchases through December. The day is worth more as the start of a relationship than as a single transaction.

The deals on the street

Unlike Black Friday, Small Business Saturday has no national doorbuster list. The discounts are set by individual merchants, and the offers announced so far show how local the day really is. In Boise, Idaho, 44 East Boutique is offering 15% off purchases, Bandanna Running and Walking is offering 20% off shoes and 25% off apparel, and Mixed Greens is offering 30% off one eligible item, excluding gift cards. Downtown Boise is also running a shopping passport programme with a chance to win a $100 downtown gift card.

In Traverse City, Michigan, American Spoon is offering 10% off purchases of $50 or more through 30 November, Evil Queen is offering 25% off candles and room sprays, excluding candle-making activities, and Benjamin Twiggs is offering 10% off selected ready-made cherry gift boxes. In Tucson, Arizona, El Regalo is offering 25% off eligible Rialto merchandise, Reilly Craft Pizza and Drink is offering a complimentary appetiser with an entrée purchase, and About Me Hair Studio is offering 20% off retail products when customers mention the promotion. In Salinas, California, the city centre has announced a 25% discount on its Lettuce Cash digital gift cards, alongside downtown holiday festivities and a shopping passport programme.

On Cape Ann, Massachusetts, eighteen local businesses told the Cape Ann Current their plans for Saturday, 28 November, and fourteen of them sent deals — from 25% off Christmas décor to a buy-two-get-one-free deal on cruise tickets. Cape Ann has 598 shops, galleries and places to eat and drink in the Current's free business directory, and the eighteen businesses that answered the survey are a small slice of what is there. The offers are the stores' own, the Current stresses, and it makes no guarantee that a store will accept them. That is the nature of the day: it is organised from the bottom up, shop by shop, street by street.

The online shift

One of the biggest changes in the day's history is where the shopping happens. The AT&T Business survey found that preference for shopping online with small businesses rose five percentage points from the year before, to 37%, while preference for large retailers fell by the same amount, to 63%. More than half of Americans plan to shop on Small Business Saturday, and nearly a quarter of them plan to do it online. The small shop is no longer only the shop on the corner. It is also the website, the Instagram storefront, and the marketplace listing — and the customers expect the same speed and convenience they get from the giants.

Seven in ten shoppers expect a same-day response to a message or enquiry, according to the AT&T survey, including 20% who expect a reply within an hour. That is a big-retailer expectation applied to a small business, and it is one of the hardest for a one-person shop to meet. The retailers that are succeeding, according to the survey data, are the ones that pair the personal service and community ties that small businesses already offer with the responsiveness customers now take for granted. The technology is part of the story too. Artificial intelligence is becoming a bigger part of the holiday shopping journey, influencing gift discovery, price comparison and retailer selection, even as most shoppers stop short of letting AI complete purchases for them. Salesforce reports that U.S. online sales from 1 October through 15 November rose 5% year over year, and 5.5% globally. The company predicts that Cyber Week, which runs from 24 to 30 November, will produce a record $357 billion in global online sales and $84 billion in the United States, each up 6%.

November discount rates are up 8% in the United States and 5% globally, according to Salesforce, and as a result average selling prices for physical goods are down 1% in the United States and 2% globally. Basket sizes are up 3%. The discounts are deeper, the prices are lower, and the baskets are bigger — and the small businesses that can hold their prices while offering something the chains cannot are the ones that come out ahead.

Why today matters more than the number

The $18 billion figure from last year is the headline, but it is not the whole story. Small Business Saturday has always been measured in two currencies: the money spent on the day, and the customers found for the year. A shopper who walks into a local store today and leaves an email address is worth more than the purchase they make today, because January is the hardest month for a small retailer, and the customers collected in November and December are the ones who come back in January. That is why the playbook advice this week has been to capture contact details at checkout and to measure repeat purchases through December, not just the takings on the day.

It is also why the day matters to the towns, not just the shops. The SBA's argument — that part of every dollar spent locally stays local and multiplies — is a claim about the high street as much as the till. A downtown with open shops is a downtown with foot traffic, and foot traffic is what keeps the café, the bookshop, and the hardware store alive through the winter. The passport programmes in Boise and Salinas, the directory in Cape Ann, the gift-card bonuses and the buy-two-get-one-free cruise tickets — all of them are attempts to get people out of the house and onto the street, because the street is the product as much as anything in the shops.

The wider weekend

Small Business Saturday sits in the middle of the biggest shopping weekend of the year, and the numbers around it frame the stakes. Thanksgiving online sales set a record of $7.1 billion, up 10.4%, according to figures reported this week. Black Friday was forecast at $12.9 billion online, Cyber Monday at $15.1 billion, Cyber Week at $47.5 billion, and the full holiday season at $275.1 billion. Buy-now-pay-later spending was forecast at $21.3 billion for the season. AI-driven traffic was up 130%. The weekend is enormous, and the small shops' share of it is small — but it is the share that stays in the community, and it is the share that the day was created to protect.

The context is a consumer who is more confident than in January, more careful than in 2024, and more online than ever. The Conference Board's 103.3 reading is the highest since January 2025. The NRF's 182.7 million Thanksgiving-weekend shoppers is only slightly below last year's 186.9 million. The AT&T survey's 52% who plan to shop today is a majority. The pieces are in place for a strong day. Whether it is a strong day will be known on Monday, when the spending estimates come in — and the comparison will be against last year's $18 billion, a number that fell 19% and that every small business in America would like to beat.

How to shop small today

For readers who want to take part, the advice is straightforward. Shop in person where you can, because the in-person visit is what the day was built for — 34% of Americans plan to shop in person today, according to the AT&T survey. Check the downtown association or the local paper for the passport programmes and the deals, because the offers are local and they are not advertised nationally. Ask about gift cards and bundles, because the shops are offering them instead of blanket discounts, and a gift card bought today is a customer kept for January. And if you shop online with a small business, expect a reply — seven in ten shoppers do — but give the shop the time a one-person business needs to answer.

The day ends at midnight, and the weekend moves on to Cyber Monday. The big platforms will dominate Monday, as they always do. But today belongs to the 36.2 million small businesses that make up 99.9% of American firms, employ 62.3 million people, and create six in ten of the country's new jobs. They are, as the SBA's figures show, not a niche. They are the economy. And today, for one day a year, they are the headline.

The cost squeeze behind the caution

The caution in this year's surveys has a cause, and the surveys name it. The NRF's chief economist said that inflation and rising gas prices will affect income groups differently — a polite way of saying that the households with the least to spend feel the squeeze first, and that the squeeze shows up in the shopping basket before it shows up in the headline sales figure. The Conference Board's researchers, reading the written comments that accompany their confidence survey, found that consumers still express frustration with high prices, even as the headline index rose 11.4 points in November to 103.3. The confidence number is the highest since January 2025. The comments underneath it are angrier than the number suggests.

That tension — more confident, still frustrated — is exactly the environment in which a small business either wins or loses a customer. A chain can absorb a deeper discount: Salesforce found November discount rates up 8% in the United States, and average selling prices for physical goods down 1% as a result. A small shop cannot match that depth for long, and the trade press advice this week has been blunt about it: do not try. Build the offer around a bundle, a bonus, or a gift card, where the value is real but the margin survives. The $10 bonus arcade card for every $50 gift card at Cape Ann Lanes, the 25% discount on Lettuce Cash digital gift cards in Salinas, the $100 downtown gift card in the Boise passport draw — these are not discounts on the product. They are reasons to come back. A gift card sold today is a visit in January, and January is when a small retailer most needs a customer through the door.

The gift card is, in fact, one of the quiet engines of the day. American Express's cumulative $241 billion figure counts spending on the day, but the gift cards bought today are spent later, and the later spending is the point. A shop that sells a $50 gift card today has a customer who will walk back in during the slowest weeks of the year, and who will very often spend more than the card is worth. The passport programmes, the bonus cards, the bundle offers — all of them are designed around the same insight: the day is not a sale. It is a first date.

Sixteen years on

Small Business Saturday began in 2010 as an American Express campaign, and the Small Business Administration signed on as a co-sponsor in 2011. The Census Bureau, which records the day's history, notes both dates, and the SBA has marked the day on its observances page ever since. In the sixteen years since, the day has grown from a marketing idea into a civic ritual: the downtown associations print the passports, the local papers publish the deal lists, and the shops open early. The spending tracked since 2012 — about $241 billion in total, according to American Express — is the measure of how far a single Saturday can reach when a whole country decides, for one day, to shop where it lives.

The day has also changed with the country. The first Small Business Saturdays were almost entirely about the physical high street. This year's is split: 34% of Americans plan to shop in person, 24% online, according to the AT&T Business survey, and the preference for shopping online with small businesses has risen five points in a year, to 37%. The shop on the corner now has a website, and the website now has a customer who expects a reply within the hour. The day that was founded to defend the high street against the internet is now, in part, an internet day — and the small businesses that are thriving are the ones that learned to be both.

What has not changed is the arithmetic that made the day worth founding. There are 36.2 million small businesses in America, and they are 99.9% of all firms. They employ 62.3 million people. They create six in ten new jobs. When they do well, the towns they sit in do well, because the money they earn is spent close to home. That is the argument Kelly Loeffler made on 19 November, and it is the argument American Express made in 2010. Sixteen years later, on a Saturday in late November, it is still the argument that brings people out of the house and into the shop.

What to watch next

The first read on today will come on Monday, when American Express and the retail analysts publish their spending estimates. The number to watch is whether the total beats last year's $18 billion — and whether the 88% who said they were likely to shop small turned into spending, or stayed as intention. The second read will come in January, when the retailers report whether the customers they collected this weekend came back. The third is the longer trend: the online preference for small businesses rose five points this year, to 37%, and if it rises again next year, the day will look different again — less about the street, more about the screen, and no less important for the shops that depend on it.

Small Business Saturday was founded in 2010 to give the smallest shops a day of their own. Sixteen years later, the day is bigger, the shoppers are more careful, and the stakes are higher. The 88% is the highest since 2012. The $18 billion is the number to beat. Today, across America, the shops are open, the passports are stamped, and the tills are waiting.
