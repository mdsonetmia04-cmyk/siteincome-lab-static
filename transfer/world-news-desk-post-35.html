TITLE: Cristiano Ronaldo Just Scored Goal No. 987 — Only 13 Away From 1,000 LABELS: Sports SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Cristiano Ronaldo scored his 987th career goal in Al-Nassr's 2-1 win over Al-Sadd — just 13 goals from becoming football's first 1,000-goal man. ---BODY---

Cristiano Ronaldo playing for Al-Nassr. The 41-year-old scored his 987th career goal on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, in a 2-1 win over Al-Sadd — leaving him just 13 goals short of 1,000. Photo: Wikimedia Commons.

Cristiano Ronaldo is now thirteen goals away from immortality. The Portuguese superstar scored his 987th career goal on Tuesday, November 24, 2026, converting a penalty in the thirteenth minute of Al-Nassr's 2-1 victory over Al-Sadd in the AFC Champions League Elite at Al-Awwal Park in Riyadh, and moved to within thirteen goals of becoming the first footballer in history to score 1,000 official goals. He is forty-one years old. He has played 1,345 matches. He has scored in six of his last five matches, and eleven times in fourteen matches this season. And he has now announced, earlier this month, that he has retired from international football with Portugal — which means that the chase for 1,000, the last great milestone of the greatest goalscoring career the sport has ever seen, will be completed entirely in the yellow shirt of Al-Nassr, in the stadiums of Saudi Arabia, in front of crowds that have come to watch a man do something that no one has ever done before.

The goal itself was, in the way of so many of Ronaldo's goals, both ordinary and extraordinary. It was a penalty, won by Ronaldo himself, after he was fouled in the box by Al-Sadd midfielder Mohamed Camara. It was struck low and hard, into the bottom corner, past the Qatari goalkeeper Meshaal Barsham, who dived the right way and still could not reach it. It was, by the standards of Ronaldo's career, unremarkable — a penalty, scored by a man who has scored hundreds of them, in a match that Al-Nassr were expected to win. But it was also goal number 987, and goal number 987 is not an ordinary goal. It is the goal that leaves thirteen to go. It is the goal that makes 1,000 not a dream but a matter of arithmetic. And it is the goal that, when the history of this chase is written, will be remembered as the moment the finish line came into view.

The goal: thirteenth minute, 987th goal

The match had barely begun when Ronaldo made his mark. In the twelfth minute, he burst into the Al-Sadd penalty area and was brought down by Mohamed Camara, the Al-Sadd midfielder, who clipped his legs as he shaped to shoot. The referee pointed to the spot without hesitation. Ronaldo picked up the ball, placed it on the penalty spot, and waited. He has taken hundreds of penalties in his career, and he has missed some of the most important of them — but on this night, in this stadium, there was no doubt. He stepped up in the thirteenth minute and fired the ball low and hard into the bottom corner, beyond the reach of Meshaal Barsham, who had dived to his right. The stadium erupted. Ronaldo ran to the corner flag, arms spread wide, and the crowd — thousands of fans in yellow, waving Saudi Arabian flags and filming on their phones — roared his name. It was, as Al-Nassr's own social media account put it, "goal number 987."

The penalty carried a particular significance for Ronaldo, because it ended a run of frustration from twelve yards. According to Opta, he had missed both of the penalties he had taken in the Saudi Pro League this season before Tuesday's match — a rare drought for a player whose penalty record, over the course of his career, has been among the best in the game. The miss in the league had become a talking point, a small crack in the armour of a player who has built his career on ruthless efficiency. Tuesday's penalty, struck with conviction and precision, closed that conversation. It was not merely a goal. It was a restoration — a reminder that the old certainties still hold, and that the man who has scored more goals than anyone in the history of the sport has not forgotten how to score them.

The match: Felix's bicycle kick steals the show

Ronaldo's goal was not, as it turned out, the finest goal of the night. Al-Sadd responded eight minutes later, in the twenty-second minute, when Mohammed Waad — the Al-Sadd forward — struck an equaliser to make it 1-1, and then imitated Ronaldo's famous celebration, arms spread wide, in front of the Al-Nassr fans. The imitation was a provocation, and it was answered. Waad was shown a straight red card in first-half stoppage time for a reckless challenge, reducing Al-Sadd to ten men, and Al-Nassr made the extra man count early in the second half. In the fifty-second minute, after the Al-Sadd defence had cleared an initial cross, Angelo headed the ball back towards the edge of the penalty area, where Joao Felix — the Portuguese forward, formerly of Chelsea and Barcelona — was waiting with his back to goal. Felix launched himself into the air and unleashed a spectacular bicycle kick that flew into the top corner, leaving the goalkeeper Meshaal Barsham helpless. It was, by common consent, one of the great goals of the season, and it gave Al-Nassr a 2-1 lead that they would not surrender.

Ronaldo himself was denied a second goal before half-time, when he found the net only for VAR to rule that he had been offside in the build-up. The decision was correct, and it was a reminder of how close he came to scoring twice on the night — and to moving to 988, and to twelve to go. But the night belonged, in the end, to both men: to Ronaldo, for the milestone, and to Felix, for the moment of brilliance. The match, played in the fifth round of the league phase of the AFC Champions League Elite, left Al-Nassr with nine points from four matches, sitting fifth in the Western Zone standings. The victory mattered for the campaign. But the number — 987, and thirteen to go — mattered more, and it is the number that will be remembered long after the points have been forgotten.

987 in 1,345: the numbers behind the chase

The scale of Ronaldo's achievement is best understood through the numbers, and the numbers are staggering. He has now scored 987 official goals in 1,345 matches — a ratio of 0.73 goals per game, sustained over twenty-four years, from his professional debut as a teenager at Sporting Lisbon in 2002 to the present day. He has scored eleven goals in fourteen matches this season for Al-Nassr, in all competitions, and six goals in his last five matches. He scored a hat-trick — three goals in a fifteen-minute span in the second half — in Al-Nassr's 5-3 league victory over Al-Kholood, a performance that moved him from 984 to 986 in a single evening. He has scored in three consecutive Saudi Pro League matches. He is, at forty-one, scoring at a rate that would be remarkable for a player ten years younger.

The chase for 1,000 is not a new ambition. Ronaldo has spoken openly about it for years, describing it as one of his remaining goals in the sport, and he has structured the final years of his career around it. The decision to retire from international football with Portugal, announced earlier this month, was made with the milestone in mind: by stepping away from the national team, he has removed the fixture congestion and the travel that international duty demands, and he has concentrated his remaining energy on the club football where the goals will come. The consequence is that the thirteen goals he still needs will all be scored for Al-Nassr — in the Saudi Pro League, in the AFC Champions League, and in the domestic cup competitions. It is a narrower stage than the one he occupied for two decades, when he scored for Portugal in World Cups and European Championships. But it is a stage on which he is still, unmistakably, the main event.

The road to 1,000

Thirteen goals. That is the distance that now separates Cristiano Ronaldo from a milestone that no footballer has ever reached. No player in the history of the sport has scored 1,000 official goals in competitive matches. Pele's tally, so often cited as 1,000, includes friendly matches and unofficial games; the official count, recognised by football's statisticians, falls short of the mark. Romario, the Brazilian striker, claimed to have reached 1,000, but his count, too, included goals that the record books do not recognise. Ronaldo's 987 are official goals, scored in competitive matches for club and country, counted by Opta and by every major statistical authority in the sport. When he scores his 1,000th, there will be no argument about it. It will be the first time it has ever been done, and it will be done by a man who has spent his entire career proving that the impossible is merely difficult.

The arithmetic of the chase is, on its face, straightforward. At his current rate — six goals in his last five matches, eleven in fourteen this season — thirteen goals is a matter of weeks, not months. Al-Nassr play regularly in the Saudi Pro League and in the AFC Champions League, and Ronaldo starts almost every match. If he continues to score at anything like his current pace, he will reach 1,000 before the end of the year, or in the opening weeks of the new one. But the arithmetic conceals the difficulty. Thirteen goals is still thirteen goals, and thirteen goals require thirteen performances, thirteen moments of precision, thirteen occasions on which a forty-one-year-old body must do what it has done nearly a thousand times before. The margin for error is small. An injury, a suspension, a run of bad luck in front of goal — any of these could delay the milestone. And the pressure, which will grow with every goal, is itself a factor: the closer he gets, the more the world will watch, and the more the weight of the occasion will bear down on every shot.

The penalty hoodoo — and why it mattered

Tuesday's goal was a penalty, and penalties have been a small but telling part of the Ronaldo story this season. Before the Al-Sadd match, he had missed both of the penalties he had taken in the Saudi Pro League this season, according to Opta — a statistic that, for a player of his standing, was striking. Penalties are, in theory, the easiest goals in football: a free shot from twelve yards, with only the goalkeeper to beat. But they are also, in practice, among the most psychologically demanding, and the misses had become a talking point — evidence, some said, that the years were finally catching up with him, that the nerve that had defined his career was beginning to fray. The goal against Al-Sadd answered that argument in the most direct way possible: by scoring from the spot, with conviction, in a match that mattered, and by doing so at the moment when the milestone demanded it.

The penalty also illustrated something about the way Ronaldo has managed the final years of his career. He wins his own penalties — he was fouled by Camara as he drove into the box — and he takes them himself, accepting the responsibility and the risk. He does not hide from the moments that define a match. He seeks them out. That has always been his way, from the free kicks at Manchester United to the headers at Real Madrid to the penalties at Juventus and Al-Nassr. The hoodoo, such as it was, lasted two penalties. It ended on a Tuesday night in Riyadh, with a ball struck low into the bottom corner, and with a number — 987 — that will be remembered long after the misses have been forgotten.

What the legends say

The football world has begun to speak about the milestone as a matter of when, not if. Mohamed Al-Deayea, the former Al-Hilal and Saudi Arabia goalkeeper — a man who spent his career trying to stop the best strikers in Asia, and who now watches Ronaldo from the other side of the great Riyadh rivalry — has said that Ronaldo is now capable of reaching 1,000 goals, and that if Al-Nassr maintain their current level, he could reach the milestone before the next winter transfer window. "Ronaldo is now capable of reaching 1,000 goals in his career," Al-Deayea said, speaking after the Al-Sadd match. "I think Cristiano Ronaldo is capable of scoring 1,000 goals, and if Al-Nassr stay at this level, he could reach that milestone before the next winter transfer window." It is a striking prediction, not least because it comes from a goalkeeper — a man whose job was to prevent goals, and who knows better than most how hard they are to score.

Al-Deayea's prediction is also notable for what it reveals about the way Ronaldo is now perceived in Saudi Arabia. When he arrived at Al-Nassr in January 2023, he was seen by some as a fading star, a great player in the twilight of his career, coming to Saudi Arabia for a final payday. Three years later, he is seen as the standard-bearer of the league, the player around whom the competition is built, and the man whose pursuit of 1,000 goals has become the defining story of Saudi football. His teammates, too, have bought into the chase: in the Al-Sadd match, as Al-Deayea noted, even Angelo — the Brazilian winger — chose to pass to Ronaldo rather than shoot himself, when a goal was on offer. The team is playing for the milestone, and the milestone is playing for the team. It is a virtuous circle, and it is one of the reasons the thirteen goals may come faster than the arithmetic alone would suggest.

A career in numbers

To understand what 1,000 goals would mean, it is necessary to understand the career that has produced 987 of them. Ronaldo made his professional debut for Sporting Lisbon in 2002, as a skinny seventeen-year-old with pace to burn and a stepover that defenders could not read. He moved to Manchester United in 2003, where Sir Alex Ferguson turned him from a tricky winger into a goalscorer, and where he won his first Ballon d'Or in 2008. He moved to Real Madrid in 2009, for a then-world-record fee, and spent nine years there scoring 450 goals in 438 matches — a ratio of more than a goal a game, sustained over nearly a decade, in the most demanding league in the world. He moved to Juventus in 2018, and scored 101 goals in three seasons in Italy. He returned to Manchester United in 2021, and then moved to Al-Nassr in January 2023, where he has continued to score at a rate that defies his age.

Along the way, he won five Ballon d'Or awards, five Champions League titles, league titles in England, Spain and Italy, and the European Championship with Portugal in 2016 — the first major trophy in his country's history. He is the all-time leading goalscorer in men's international football, the all-time leading goalscorer in the Champions League, and the all-time leading goalscorer in the history of Real Madrid. He has scored more goals than any player in the history of the sport, and he has done so in four different leagues, in three different decades, and in every competition he has entered. The 1,000-goal milestone would be the last great number in a career defined by great numbers — the final entry in a ledger that has, for twenty-four years, recorded only addition.

When could he get there?

The question that every football fan is now asking is simple: when? When will Cristiano Ronaldo score his 1,000th goal? The answer, based on his current form, is: soon. He has scored six goals in his last five matches. He has scored eleven in fourteen this season. He is starting every match, he is taking the penalties, and his teammates are looking for him in the box. At that rate, thirteen goals is a matter of ten or twelve matches — a matter of weeks. Al-Nassr's schedule, across the Saudi Pro League and the AFC Champions League, provides ample opportunity. The winter transfer window, which Al-Deayea mentioned as a possible deadline, opens in January, and Ronaldo could, on his current pace, reach the milestone before it closes — or even before it opens.

But the chase is not only a matter of arithmetic. It is a matter of narrative, and the narrative is what makes it compelling. Every goal from now on will be counted. Every match will be watched. Every near-miss will be dissected. The thirteenth goal from now — the 1,000th — will be one of the most watched moments in the history of the sport, and the twelve that precede it will be watched almost as closely. The world will know, in real time, how many remain: twelve, eleven, ten, nine. The countdown has begun, and it began on a Tuesday night in Riyadh, with a penalty in the thirteenth minute, and a number — 987 — that will be remembered as the moment the finish line came into view. For Ronaldo, the remaining thirteen goals are not a burden. They are an invitation. He has spent his career accepting invitations like this one. He will accept this one, too.
