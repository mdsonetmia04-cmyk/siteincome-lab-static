TITLE: Solomon Islands Just Dumped China and Signed With Australia — $490 Million, and 'No Foreign Military Base' LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: Solomon Islands signed a $490m security treaty with Australia on Nov 25, naming Canberra its primary partner and barring any foreign military base. ---BODY---

Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, whose government signed a sweeping security treaty with Solomon Islands on November 25, 2026, cementing Australia as the island nation's primary security partner. Photo: Wikimedia Commons (official portrait).

Solomon Islands has dumped China and signed with Australia — and it has done so in the most public way possible, in the Australian Parliament in Canberra, in front of the cameras, with a treaty that says, in plain language, that there will never be a foreign military base on its soil. On Wednesday, November 25, 2026, the prime ministers of Australia and Solomon Islands signed a sweeping bilateral security treaty that names Australia as the Solomons' "primary security partner", commits Canberra to A$740 million — about $490 million — in funding over the next decade, and bars any foreign power from establishing a military or security base in the archipelago. The treaty also requires Solomon Islands to consult Australia before making deals with other countries on critical infrastructure or security cooperation outside the Pacific Islands Forum — a clause aimed squarely at Beijing, whose secretive 2022 security pact with Honiara raised fears across the Pacific of a Chinese naval foothold astride the sea route between the United States and Australia. The signing follows reports that Solomon Islands has told China its defence deal will not be renewed when it expires after five years in early 2027 — a claim the Solomons' prime minister, Matthew Wale, would neither confirm nor deny, citing a non-disclosure clause in the Chinese treaty. "Solomon Islands has been for a number of years a source of uncertainty and doubt in the region," Wale told journalists alongside Albanese. "Those days are gone."

The treaty is the biggest strategic shift in the South Pacific in years, and it is a victory for Australia — and, by extension, for the United States and its allies — in a contest for influence over a region that has become one of the sharpest front lines of the rivalry with China. Solomon Islands is a small country, with a population of fewer than 900,000 people, scattered across an archipelago 2,000 kilometres — 1,200 miles — northeast of Australia. But it sits on the sea route between the United States and Australia, the same waters from which the American general Douglas MacArthur commanded the Pacific campaign in the Second World War, and the same islands where US allies fought some of the most brutal battles of that war against Japanese forces. Whoever controls, or influences, Solomon Islands matters — not because of its size, but because of where it is. For four years, that influence appeared to be moving towards China. On Wednesday, in Canberra, it moved back.

The China deal: four years of fear in the Pacific

To understand why Wednesday's treaty matters, it is necessary to understand what came before it: a secretive security pact, signed in 2022 between Solomon Islands and China, that set off alarm bells from Canberra to Washington. The deal, struck under the Solomons' former prime minister, Manasseh Sogavare, was negotiated in secret, and its full text has never been made public. What was known — or feared — was enough to cause a crisis. The pact provided for Chinese police trainers to be stationed in the Solomons' capital, Honiara. It raised the possibility, which Australia and the United States took seriously, that it could also cover the Chinese navy — that Chinese warships might one day be able to dock, refuel or base themselves in a country that sits directly on the sea lanes between America and Australia. The fear was not abstract. In the Second World War, the Solomon Islands were the scene of the Guadalcanal campaign, one of the longest and bloodiest of the Pacific war, fought precisely because the islands command the approaches to Australia. A Chinese naval presence there would have been, for Australia, a strategic nightmare — a hostile navy operating 2,000 kilometres from its coast, in waters it has long regarded as its own backyard.

The 2022 pact was part of a broader pattern. Solomon Islands had switched its diplomatic recognition from Taiwan to China in 2019, and in the years that followed, China built infrastructure across the archipelago under million-dollar loans, through its Belt and Road Initiative — roads, bridges, ports, telecommunications, energy projects. The security pact was the logical extension of that economic relationship: a country that owed China money, and that depended on Chinese construction, was now inviting Chinese police into its capital. The United States and Australia protested, loudly and publicly. Wale himself, then in opposition, was highly critical of the deal, saying it would upset regional security and could jeopardise the Solomons' relationships with its existing partners. But Sogavare stayed in power, the pact stayed in force, and the fear stayed with it — that Solomon Islands, a country of fewer than 900,000 people, had become the place where China's ambitions in the Pacific would first take military form.

What changed was the election. Wale was elected leader in May, and he has spent the months since on what can only be described as a diplomatic sprint: upending the close ties with Beijing built by his predecessor, travelling to Canberra and Washington to rebuild relationships with traditional partners, and signalling, at every step, that the days of uncertainty were over. The treaty signed on Wednesday is the culmination of that sprint — and it is, in its terms, a direct repudiation of the 2022 pact. Where the Chinese deal was secret, the Australian treaty is public. Where the Chinese deal raised the prospect of a foreign military presence, the Australian treaty bars one. Where the Chinese deal was negotiated behind closed doors, the Australian treaty was signed in the Australian Parliament, in front of the press, with 15 Solomon Islands ministers in attendance. The symbolism was deliberate, and it was unmistakable: Solomon Islands has chosen its side, and it has chosen it in public.

What the treaty actually says

The treaty signed on Wednesday is broader than security, and its terms go well beyond the headline commitment on military bases. At its core is a simple bargain: Australia becomes Solomon Islands' "primary security partner", and in return Solomon Islands affirms its "sovereign decision" that there will be no foreign military or security base on its soil, and that its sensitive infrastructure will remain free from militarisation, foreign interference and unauthorised access. That commitment covers airports, ports, energy and telecommunications — the areas where China has built infrastructure under million-dollar loans since the country switched ties from Taiwan in 2019 — although the clause will not be applied retrospectively to projects already built. Under the deal, Australia will support the expansion and development of Solomon Islands' police and border force, while Honiara will deny any request from a foreign power to establish military or security bases on its territory. And Solomon Islands will be required to consult with Australia before making deals with other countries on critical infrastructure or security cooperation outside members of the Pacific Islands Forum, the regional bloc — a consultation requirement that gives Canberra, in effect, a say over any future security arrangement Honiara might contemplate with a country outside the Pacific family.

The money is substantial, and it is structured to last. Over a decade, Australia will provide A$670 million in grants and A$70 million in concessional loans — a total of A$740 million, or about $490 million — to boost education, health, policing, resources and climate resilience, and to assist with a sovereign wealth fund. The treaty also covers road and bridge construction — areas China has dominated in the islands through its Belt and Road Initiative — and includes improved visa arrangements for Solomon Islanders. The Australian funds will be in place by January, bolstering Wale's government as parliament returns. The scale of the commitment reflects how seriously Canberra takes the relationship: A$740 million over ten years is, for a country of fewer than 900,000 people, a very large sum, and it is designed not only to buy influence but to build the kind of long-term partnership — in schools, in hospitals, in policing, in roads — that is harder to displace than a single infrastructure loan or a single security pact. Albanese said the treaty elevated bilateral relations to "a level which has never been seen before and has never been stronger", and that Wale had shown "courageous leadership". "Security in the Pacific is the responsibility of the Pacific," Albanese said — a line that, in context, was a message to China as much as to the Solomons: the Pacific's security will be decided by Pacific countries, not by Beijing.

The treaty is also part of a pattern. Australia has recently signed similar agreements with Papua New Guinea, Vanuatu and Fiji — a series of pacts that, taken together, amount to a deliberate strategy of binding the Pacific island nations into a security architecture centred on Canberra, and of closing the doors through which China has been entering the region. Each pact is different in its details, but the logic is the same: offer money, training, infrastructure and partnership, and in return secure a commitment that the Pacific will not become a theatre for Chinese military power. The Solomon Islands treaty is the most significant of the series, because Solomon Islands was the country where China had made the most progress, and where the risk of a Chinese military foothold was greatest. Its signing marks, if not the end of China's ambitions in the Pacific, then at least a serious setback for them — and a demonstration that the island nations themselves, when offered a genuine alternative, will choose it.

Wale's gamble — and the question he would not answer

The man at the centre of Wednesday's signing is Matthew Wale, and his story is, in many ways, the story of the treaty itself. Wale was a long-time critic of China who had, in recent years, appeared to soften his stance — a shift that made his election in May, and his subsequent diplomatic sprint, all the more striking. Since taking office, he has moved quickly and decisively: travelling to Canberra and Washington, rebuilding relationships with traditional partners, and, according to reports in the Australian media, giving China six months' notice of his country's withdrawal from the 2022 security pact. On Wednesday, asked directly about those reports — about whether he had ended the secretive security pact with Beijing — Wale would not confirm or deny them. "Unfortunately there is a non-disclosure clause in the treaty with China," he told reporters at the press conference with Albanese. "So I am unable to talk about that. We have ongoing discussions with China." It was a careful, lawyerly answer, and it left the most important question about the treaty unanswered: is the China pact actually dead, or is it merely dying?

The ambiguity is deliberate, and it reflects the delicacy of Wale's position. Solomon Islands still owes China money. Chinese-built infrastructure still stands across the archipelago. Chinese police trainers are, as far as is publicly known, still in Honiara. Ending a security pact with a great power is not like cancelling a subscription — it involves negotiations, notice periods, and the management of a relationship that, whatever its strategic implications, still matters economically to a small, poor country. Wale's refusal to confirm the reports may be a matter of legal obligation — the non-disclosure clause he cited is, if it exists, a real constraint — or it may be a matter of diplomacy, a way of managing the transition without provoking a public confrontation with Beijing. Either way, the treaty signed on Wednesday speaks for itself: whatever the formal status of the 2022 pact, Solomon Islands has now committed, in a public treaty with Australia, to a security architecture that leaves no room for a Chinese military presence. The 2022 pact, if it survives in any form, survives as a dead letter.

China's response, so far, has been measured. Its foreign ministry said on Tuesday that there was "no element of geopolitical rivalry or bloc confrontation in cooperation between China and Pacific island countries, including the Solomon Islands" — a formulation that denies the rivalry even as it acknowledges the stakes. Beijing has not, publicly, condemned the Australian treaty, or threatened retaliation, or demanded that Honiara reconsider. That restraint may reflect a calculation that a public confrontation would only confirm the narrative the treaty embodies — that China is a rival to be contained — or it may reflect a belief that the relationship with Solomon Islands can still be salvaged, through economics if not through security. What is clear is that Beijing has lost, for now, the strategic prize it appeared to be winning in 2022: a security relationship with a Pacific island nation on the sea route between the United States and Australia. Whether it can win it back, and at what price, is a question for the years ahead.

Why a country of 900,000 people matters so much

It is easy, looking at a map, to wonder why so much diplomatic energy — a prime ministerial visit, 15 ministers, A$740 million, a treaty signed in Parliament — should be spent on a country of fewer than 900,000 people, scattered across an archipelago most people could not find on a map. The answer is geography, and it is the same answer that made Solomon Islands matter in 1942. The archipelago is sprawled across the sea route between the United States and Australia, the shipping lanes that connect the world's largest economy with one of its most important allies. In the Second World War, the United States fought the Guadalcanal campaign there — six months of brutal jungle fighting, at enormous cost in lives — precisely because whoever held the Solomons controlled the approaches to Australia, and the supply lines that kept it in the war. The islands have not moved since then. The sea lanes have not moved. What has changed is who wants to control them — and the answer, in 2026, is China, a country whose navy is now the largest in the world by number of ships, and whose ambitions in the Pacific have grown with its fleet.

The strategic logic is not complicated, and it is the reason the 2022 pact caused such alarm. A Chinese naval facility in Solomon Islands — even a modest one, even a "logistics" facility, even a port that Chinese warships could use for refuelling and resupply — would have given Beijing a presence 2,000 kilometres from Australia's coast, astride the sea lanes to the United States, and in a position to monitor, and if necessary to threaten, the shipping that connects the two allies. It would have been, for Australia, the equivalent of a hostile power establishing a base in its front garden. The treaty signed on Wednesday removes that possibility — or, at least, it removes it for as long as the treaty holds, and for as long as Solomon Islands honours its commitment. That is why the treaty's central clause — "there will be no foreign military or security base" — is written in such absolute terms, and why it was the first thing both prime ministers emphasised in their statements. It is a line drawn on a map, in the form of a treaty, and it is drawn in the place where it matters most.

But the treaty is also about more than geography. It is about the kind of region the Pacific will be — whether it will be a region of independent island nations, choosing their own partners and deciding their own security, or a region divided into spheres of influence, in which great powers compete for bases and for loyalty. Wale's decision to approve the Australian treaty was taken by his entire cabinet, many of whom travelled to Canberra for the signing, and it was presented, by both prime ministers, as a sovereign choice — a decision by Solomon Islands, for Solomon Islands, about what kind of country it wants to be. That framing matters, because it is the answer to the charge — made by China, and by some critics of Australia — that the treaty is an exercise in great-power rivalry, in which a small country is being used as a pawn. Wale's answer, delivered in Canberra, was that Solomon Islands is not a pawn, and that it has chosen its partner freely. "Those days are gone," he said, of the years of uncertainty. Whether the choice holds, and whether the treaty survives the next election, the next government, and the next offer from Beijing, is a question that will be answered in Honiara, not in Canberra — and not in Beijing, either.

What happens next

The treaty now goes into effect, and its first tests will come quickly. The Australian funds will be in place by January, and the work they are meant to fund — in education, in health, in policing, in roads and bridges — will begin to be visible in the months that follow. The consultation requirement — that Solomon Islands consult Australia before making deals with other countries on critical infrastructure or security outside the Pacific Islands Forum — will be tested the first time Honiara contemplates such a deal, and the way that consultation is handled will reveal how the treaty works in practice: whether it is a genuine partnership, or a veto in disguise. The status of the 2022 China pact will, sooner or later, have to be clarified — whether it expires in early 2027, as reported, whether it is formally terminated, or whether it simply lapses, unrenewed and unmourned. And the Chinese police trainers in Honiara will, sooner or later, have to leave, or be replaced, or be explained — because their continued presence would sit uneasily with a treaty that commits Solomon Islands to keeping its security free from foreign interference.

The broader contest, meanwhile, will continue. China has not abandoned its ambitions in the Pacific, and it has other partners, other loans, and other infrastructure projects across the region. Australia has not finished its work, either: the pacts with Papua New Guinea, Vanuatu and Fiji are in place, but the region is large, the island nations are many, and the competition for their loyalty is ongoing. The United States, which has its own interests in the Pacific, will be watching closely — and, in all likelihood, quietly pleased. The treaty signed on Wednesday is, in the end, a single document, signed by two prime ministers, in a Parliament building in Canberra. But it is also a statement about the future of the Pacific: that the island nations will choose their own security partners, that they will not host foreign military bases, and that, for now at least, they have chosen Australia. The question is whether that choice will hold — and whether, in five years, or ten, the map of the Pacific will look the way it does today, or the way it looked in 2022, when a secretive pact with China seemed to be the future. On Wednesday, in Canberra, Solomon Islands gave its answer. The rest of the Pacific is listening.
