TITLE: Houthi Drone Hits Riyadh's Tallest Tower for the Second Time in a Week — 11 People Injured in Saudi Arabia's Financial District LABELS: World SEARCH DESCRIPTION: A Houthi drone struck the 385-metre PIF Tower in Riyadh's financial district, injuring 11 people — the second attack on the tower in a week. ---BODY---

The King Abdullah Financial District in Riyadh, home to the Public Investment Fund Tower. Photo: Ahmed / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0).

A drone launched by Yemen's Houthi rebels struck the tallest tower in Saudi Arabia's capital on Tuesday, November 24, injuring 11 people and damaging the building that houses the headquarters of the kingdom's sovereign wealth fund. It was the second attack on the same tower in less than a week — and a sharp escalation in a conflict that has been spreading across the Middle East since Yemen's frozen civil war reignited in July, as a knock-on effect of the war between the United States, Israel and Iran. Saudi Arabia's Civil Defence said the 11 injured included two Saudi citizens, four Egyptians, three Nepalis and two Indians. The Saudi-led coalition fighting the Houthis called the strike a "cowardly terrorist attack" and promised a firm response. And across the region, governments from Qatar to Pakistan condemned an attack on the gleaming financial district that was supposed to symbolise Saudi Arabia's future — and that has now been hit twice in five days.

The target, according to witnesses, was the Public Investment Fund Tower — the 385-metre skyscraper that dominates the King Abdullah Financial District, known as KAFD, and the most prominent landmark of Saudi Arabia's showcase for its economic ambitions. The Saudi-led coalition did not officially name the building that was struck, but three separate witnesses told reporters that the strike had targeted the PIF Tower, and Saudi Civil Defence posted a photograph of the landmark tower showing damage to the building about halfway up its facade — damage similar to the impact from the first strike on the same tower on Thursday, November 19, which caused material damage but no injuries. On Tuesday, the injuries were real: 11 people, most of them foreign workers, hurt in the heart of the capital's financial district, in the middle of the working day.

The attack was carried out using a drone, according to Major General Turki al-Malki, the spokesperson for the Coalition to Restore Legitimacy in Yemen, who said in a statement on social media that "the deliberate targeting of civilians and civilian objects by the terrorist Houthi militia will be met with a firm response." Authorities were conducting an on-site investigation, he said. The Houthi group, which controls much of western Yemen including the capital Sanaa, has been firing at Saudi airports, oil infrastructure and office buildings since the civil war reignited in July — and the strike on Riyadh's tallest tower, twice in less than a week, is the most brazen demonstration yet of how far its reach now extends.

What happened on Tuesday

The attack came on Tuesday, November 24, in the middle of a working day in Riyadh's financial district. Saudi Arabia's Civil Defence said emergency services had responded to an incident in which a tower in the district was targeted in what it called a terrorist attack carried out by the Houthi militia. The toll, confirmed by the civil defence spokesperson, was 11 people injured: two Saudi citizens, two Indian nationals, three Nepali nationals and four Egyptian nationals. The Indian embassy in Saudi Arabia said in a post on X that it had learned two Indian nationals had sustained injuries in the attack. Most of the injured were foreign workers — the men and women who keep Riyadh's financial district running, and who have no part in the wars being fought around them.

The building that was hit matters. The PIF Tower stands 385 metres high, the tallest skyscraper in Riyadh, and it is the headquarters of the Public Investment Fund, Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund and the engine of the kingdom's plan to diversify its economy away from oil. The tower is the most prominent landmark of the King Abdullah Financial District, a purpose-built financial quarter of glass towers that was designed to be the showcase of Saudi Arabia's economic ambitions — a place where the world's banks, funds and companies would come to do business in the new Saudi Arabia. An attack on the PIF Tower is, by design, an attack on that ambition. It says: your showcase is not safe, your sovereign wealth fund is not safe, and your capital is within our reach.

The damage, as shown in the photograph posted by Saudi Civil Defence, was visible about halfway up the tower's facade. It was not catastrophic — the building still stands, and the district continues to function — but it was unmistakable, and it was the second time in five days. The first strike, on Thursday, November 19, had caused damage but no injuries. Tuesday's strike injured 11 people. The direction of travel is clear: the attacks are becoming more frequent, and more harmful.

The second strike in five days

The fact that the PIF Tower has now been struck twice in less than a week is, in itself, the story. The first attack, on November 19, was treated by many as a one-off — a demonstration, a warning, a single drone that got through. The second attack, five days later, shows that it was not a one-off. It was the start of a campaign. The Houthis have now shown that they can hit the tallest building in the Saudi capital, not once but twice, and that Saudi Arabia's air defences — among the most expensive in the world — cannot reliably stop them.

The pattern is familiar from the Houthis' earlier campaigns. The group, which is backed by Iran, has a long history of using drones and missiles to strike targets deep inside Saudi Arabia, including airports, oil facilities and cities. What is new is the context: the strikes are now part of a wider regional war, and they are hitting targets that were previously considered safe. The financial district of Riyadh is not a military base or an oil terminal. It is the centre of Saudi Arabia's civilian economy — offices, banks, funds, workers. Striking it is a deliberate choice, and it is a choice that the Houthis have now made twice.

The Saudi-led coalition's response, so far, has been words and a promise. Major General Turki al-Malki called the attack "cowardly" and said the deliberate targeting of civilians and civilian objects "will be met with a firm response." But the coalition has not yet said what that response will be. The Yemeni government forces that the coalition supports claim to have carried out 697 strikes against the Houthis over the past few hours, according to the army's spokesman — a figure that, if accurate, suggests a major escalation is already under way. The question is whether that escalation will stop the attacks, or whether it will simply invite more of them.

The war behind the attack

The strike on Riyadh cannot be understood on its own. It is part of a war that has been spreading across the Middle East for months, and that now involves the United States, Israel, Iran, the Houthis, and the governments of the Gulf. Yemen's civil war, which had been frozen for years, reignited in July, in what Al Jazeera described as a knock-on effect of the US-Israel war on Iran. Since then, the Houthis have been firing at Saudi airports, oil infrastructure and office buildings, and the Saudi-led coalition has been striking back. The attack on the PIF Tower is the latest, and most audacious, move in that exchange.

The wider war is, if anything, intensifying. Six oil tankers have now been hit in the Strait of Hormuz by Iranian attacks and naval mines, according to Iran's army and the navy of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps — a campaign that has turned the world's most important oil chokepoint into a war zone. The commander-in-chief of the IRGC, Major General Ahmad Vahidi, said on Tuesday that future wars "will not necessarily be confined to the region," and called on regional countries to reconsider their security cooperation with the United States, saying lasting security could only be achieved through reliance on domestic capabilities and regional cooperation, not dependence on foreign powers. Iran's security chief, Mohsen Rezaei, warned again that Tehran would respond to any new aggression with "full force." And Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesperson, Esmaeil Baghaei, said Iran would use all its tools to confront the US naval blockade — a blockade first imposed on April 13, 2026, lifted in June, and reinstated on July 14.

Diplomacy, meanwhile, is struggling to keep up. On Monday, November 23, the Qatari Prime Minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman bin Jassim Al Thani, met separately with US Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio at the White House, as Doha sought to revive US-Iran diplomacy. Qatar, which has long acted as a mediator in the region's conflicts, condemned Tuesday's attack on Riyadh as a "shameful act of aggression, an unacceptable disregard for civilian lives, a flagrant violation of Saudi sovereignty, and a clear breach of the rules of international law." But condemnation is not a ceasefire, and the meetings in Washington have not yet produced one. The war goes on, and the Houthis, for now, are winning the battle of the headlines.

Why the PIF Tower matters

To understand why the Houthis chose the PIF Tower — twice — it helps to understand what the tower represents. The Public Investment Fund is not just a sovereign wealth fund. It is the central instrument of Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's plan to transform Saudi Arabia's economy, the vehicle through which the kingdom is investing hundreds of billions of dollars in new industries, new cities and new technologies, in an effort to reduce its dependence on oil. The fund is behind the NEOM megacity, the Red Sea tourism projects, the investments in sports, entertainment and technology that have made Saudi Arabia one of the most talked-about economies in the world. An attack on its headquarters is an attack on the symbol of that transformation.

The King Abdullah Financial District, where the tower stands, is the physical embodiment of the same ambition. Conceived as a financial centre to rival Dubai and Singapore, the district is a cluster of gleaming towers in the north of Riyadh, designed to house the banks, funds and regulators that would make Saudi Arabia a global financial hub. For years, it was a white elephant — a grand project that struggled to attract tenants. In recent years, it has begun to fill up, as the PIF moved in and as international firms followed. The district's success is, in many ways, a test of whether Saudi Arabia's economic transformation is real. The Houthis' decision to strike it twice in five days is a deliberate attempt to answer that question in the negative — to say that no amount of glass and steel can make Riyadh safe while the region is at war.

There is also a practical dimension. The PIF Tower is 385 metres tall, visible from across Riyadh, and impossible to miss. Hitting it is not just a military act. It is a message, broadcast to the entire region: we can reach the heart of your capital, we can hit your most prominent building, and we can do it again. That message is aimed not just at Saudi Arabia, but at the investors, bankers and companies that the kingdom is trying to attract. It is a message that says: your money is not safe here. And it is a message that, if the attacks continue, will become harder and harder to ignore.

The victims

Amid the geopolitics, it is worth remembering who was hurt on Tuesday. The 11 injured were two Saudis, four Egyptians, three Nepalis and two Indians — a roll call that reflects the reality of who works in Riyadh's financial district, and in the Gulf more broadly. The towers of KAFD are cleaned, maintained, guarded and serviced by migrant workers from across Asia and the Arab world, men and women who have left their families to earn a living in Saudi Arabia. They are not combatants. They are not policymakers. They are workers, and they were injured because they happened to be in a building that someone else decided to attack.

Their injuries, according to Saudi Civil Defence, were the result of a drone strike on a civilian building — a deliberate targeting of civilians and civilian objects, in the coalition's words. That is a violation of the laws of war, and it is the reason the attack has been condemned so widely. But condemnation does not heal injuries, and it does not answer the question that the families of the injured will be asking: why were they in harm's way, and who will protect them next time? The answer, for now, is that no one can guarantee their safety — not the Saudi government, not the coalition, and not the air defences that failed to stop the drone.

The embassies of the countries whose nationals were injured have begun to respond. The Indian embassy in Riyadh said it had learned that two Indian nationals had sustained injuries, and it is presumably in contact with the hospitals and the families. The Egyptian and Nepali authorities will be doing the same. But the deeper question — whether foreign workers should be in Riyadh's financial district at all while it is being targeted — is one that no embassy can answer. It is a question for the workers themselves, and for the companies that employ them, and it will be asked with increasing urgency if the attacks continue.

The regional response

The condemnation of Tuesday's attack was swift and widespread. Qatar called it a "shameful act of aggression." The United Arab Emirates condemned it "in the strongest terms." Pakistan's Foreign Office strongly condemned what it called "the terrorist attack carried out today by the Houthi militia." The Arab states and the Muslim World League issued their own condemnations, expressing solidarity with Saudi Arabia. The Lebanese Prime Minister, Nawaf Salam, described the attack as an act of "terrorism." The message from the region was unanimous: this was an attack on a civilian target, in a capital city, and it was unacceptable.

But unanimity in condemnation is not the same as unity in action. The countries that condemned the attack have different interests, different relationships with the Houthis and with Iran, and different ideas about what should be done. Qatar, which condemned the attack in the strongest terms, is also the country that is trying to mediate between the United States and Iran — a role that requires it to talk to all sides. The UAE, which condemned the attack, has its own history of conflict with the Houthis, and its own reasons to want them defeated. Pakistan, which condemned the attack, has a large expatriate community in Saudi Arabia and a deep interest in the kingdom's stability. The condemnations are real, but they are also, in part, performances — statements of solidarity that cost nothing and commit the speaker to nothing.

What matters more is what happens next. The coalition has promised a "firm response." The Yemeni government forces claim to have already carried out 697 strikes against the Houthis. The question is whether the response will be a sustained campaign that degrades the Houthis' ability to launch drones, or a short burst of retaliation that leaves their capabilities intact. The history of the Yemen war suggests the latter is more likely. The Houthis have survived years of bombing, and they have continued to launch attacks throughout. A few hundred more strikes are unlikely to change that. What might change it is a political settlement — and that, for now, seems further away than ever.

The Houthis' growing reach

The strike on the PIF Tower is also a reminder of how far the Houthis' capabilities have come. A decade ago, the group was a rebel militia fighting a civil war in one of the poorest countries in the world. Today, it controls much of western Yemen, including the capital Sanaa, and it can launch drones that strike the tallest building in the Saudi capital, twice in five days, despite Saudi Arabia's expensive air defences. That is a remarkable transformation, and it is one that has been made possible, in large part, by support from Iran — support that the Houthis themselves do not hide, and that the coalition and its allies have documented for years.

The Houthis' arsenal is not limited to drones. They have fired missiles at Saudi airports and oil facilities, attacked ships in the Red Sea, and, according to the coalition, launched strikes on office buildings and civilian infrastructure. Their ability to sustain these attacks, despite years of bombing, suggests that their supply lines remain intact and that their capabilities are, if anything, growing. The 697 strikes claimed by Yemeni government forces in the past few hours are a measure of how seriously the coalition takes the threat. But they are also a measure of how difficult it has proven to eliminate it.

There is a broader lesson here, and it is about the changing nature of warfare in the Middle East. The Houthis are not a conventional army. They are a militia that has mastered the use of cheap drones and missiles to strike expensive targets — a strategy that has been called, by some analysts, the democratisation of air power. A drone that costs a few thousand dollars can damage a tower that cost hundreds of millions to build. A missile that costs a fraction of an interceptor can force a country to spend millions on defence. That asymmetry is the Houthis' greatest advantage, and it is one that Saudi Arabia, for all its wealth and weaponry, has not yet found a way to overcome.

What happens next

The immediate future is likely to bring more of the same: more strikes by the Houthis, more retaliation by the coalition, more condemnation by the region's governments, and more injuries to the workers caught in between. The Yemeni government forces' claim of 697 strikes suggests that a major escalation is already under way, and the coalition's promise of a "firm response" suggests that more is coming. But unless that response is accompanied by a political strategy — a negotiation, a ceasefire, a settlement — it is unlikely to end the attacks. The Houthis have survived worse, and they have shown, twice in five days, that they can hit the heart of Riyadh whenever they choose.

The longer-term future depends on the wider war. The US-Iran war, the conflict in Gaza, the fighting in Yemen — these are not separate wars. They are connected, and they will only end when the connections are addressed. The Qatari Prime Minister's meetings in Washington on Monday were an attempt to do just that: to revive diplomacy between the United States and Iran, and to find a path to a ceasefire. But diplomacy takes time, and the Houthis are not waiting. They struck on Thursday. They struck again on Tuesday. And there is no reason to think they will stop.

For Saudi Arabia, the attacks pose a question that goes to the heart of its national project. The kingdom has spent hundreds of billions of dollars building a new economy — a financial district, a sovereign wealth fund, a tourism industry, a technology sector — on the assumption that it could provide security for the investors and workers who would make that economy function. The Houthis' attacks challenge that assumption directly. If the tallest tower in Riyadh can be hit twice in a week, what is safe? The answer, for now, is that nothing is entirely safe — and that the kingdom's transformation will have to proceed under fire, or not at all.

For the rest of the world, the attack is a reminder that the Middle East's wars are not contained. They affect oil prices, shipping routes, financial markets and the safety of the millions of foreign workers who keep the Gulf's economies running. Six tankers have been hit in the Strait of Hormuz. A tower has been hit twice in Riyadh. The war is spreading, and it is spreading to the places that were supposed to be safe. The question is not whether it will spread further. The question is how far, and how fast, and what it will take to stop it.
